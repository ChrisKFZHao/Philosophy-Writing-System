# Foucault, Michel (1926--1984)

- **Tradition**: Continental philosophy; post-structuralism; historical epistemology
- **Major Works**: *The Order of Things* (1966), *The Archaeology of Knowledge* (1969), *Discipline and Punish* (1975), *The History of Sexuality* (3 vols., 1976--1984)
- **Key Positions**:
  - Power-knowledge: power and knowledge are mutually constitutive; knowledge is produced within and sustains power relations
  - Epistemes: historical periods are characterized by deep conceptual structures (epistemes) that determine what can count as knowledge in a given era
  - Disciplinary power: modern power operates through disciplinary institutions (prisons, schools, hospitals) that produce docile bodies through surveillance and normalization
  - Genealogy as method: excavate the contingent historical conditions under which concepts, institutions, and practices emerged, revealing that what appears necessary is the product of power and accident
  - Truth is a thing of this world, produced by multiple constraints and inducing regular effects of power; each society has its regime of truth
- **Key Arguments**:
  - The Panopticon argument: Bentham's Panopticon is the architectural diagram of modern disciplinary society; the possibility of constant surveillance induces self-regulation without overt coercion
  - The historical a priori argument: what counts as true or false in a given period is determined by historically contingent discursive formations that are themselves neither true nor false
  - The argument for bio-power: modern states exercise power over populations (birth rates, health, longevity) rather than merely threatening death; the management of life replaces the threat of death
- **Relevance**: `phil-conceptual-analysis` (concepts as historically constituted within discursive formations), `phil-dialectical-map` (power relations and discursive structures), `phil-premise-detect` (archaeological and genealogical methods in argument analysis), `phil-normativity` (the normative implications of genealogical critique), `phil-moral-disagreement` (genealogy of moral concepts showing their historical contingency)
- **Related**: Nietzsche (primary influence; genealogy as historical critique of moral and epistemic concepts); Kuhn (both emphasize historical discontinuity in knowledge systems); Derrida (fellow post-structuralist; key differences on the status of discourse versus text); Habermas (criticized Foucault's normative ambiguity; the Habermas-Foucault debate on power and reason)
