# Stevenson, C.L. (1908--1979)

- **Tradition**: Analytic metaethics; emotivism
- **Major Works**: *Ethics and Language* (1944), *Facts and Values* (1963)
- **Key Positions**:
  - Sophisticated emotivism: ethical terms have both emotive meaning (expressing and evoking attitudes) and descriptive meaning (referring to natural properties)
  - The two-patterns analysis: "this is good" means roughly "I approve of this; do so as well" -- it has both a descriptive component (speaker's attitude) and a dynamic, imperative component (inviting the hearer to share)
  - Persuasive definitions: definitions are not purely descriptive; they redirect attitudes by changing descriptive reference while preserving emotive meaning
  - Disagreement in attitude vs. disagreement in belief: moral disagreement is fundamentally disagreement in attitude, not disagreement about facts
  - Moral reasoning is ultimately a form of non-rational persuasion when it engages fundamental evaluative differences
- **Key Arguments**:
  - The argument from the working models: Stevenson provides detailed working models (analyses) of ethical terms showing how they function both to describe and influence; the models' success confirms sophisticated emotivism
  - The argument from persuasive definitions: the fact that ethical definitions are contested and used persuasively (defining "justice" as "equality" or "entitlement") shows ethical terms have a dynamic, attitudinal function beyond mere description
  - The argument for disagreement in attitude: moral disputes cannot be resolved solely by settling factual disputes; there is always a remainder of attitudinal conflict explaining the persistence of moral disagreement
- **Relevance**: `phil-moral-semantics` (the two-patterns analysis as a sophisticated non-cognitivist semantics), `phil-moral-disagreement` (disagreement in attitude vs. disagreement in belief), `phil-moral-psychology` (emotive meaning and the role of attitudes in moral language), `phil-conceptual-analysis` (persuasive definitions as non-neutral conceptual analysis)
- **Related**: Ayer (Stevenson refines and defends Ayer's emotivism against the charge that it reduces ethics to irrational ejaculation); Moore (Stevenson accepts that the naturalistic fallacy shows ethical terms have a special non-descriptive function); Hare (prescriptivism is a rationalist development of Stevenson's insight that ethical language has a directive function); Gibbard (contemporary expressivist who develops Stevenson's project with greater formal sophistication)
