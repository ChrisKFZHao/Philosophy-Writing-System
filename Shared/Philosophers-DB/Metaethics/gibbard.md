# Gibbard, Allan (b. 1942)

- **Tradition**: Analytic metaethics; expressivism; norm-expressivism
- **Major Works**: *Wise Choices, Apt Feelings* (1990), *Thinking How to Live* (2003), *Meaning and Normativity* (2012), *Reconciling Our Aims* (2008)
- **Key Positions**:
  - Norm-expressivism: moral judgments express states of norm-acceptance; to call an action "wrong" is to express acceptance of a system of norms that rules out that action
  - The rationality of feelings: the central normative concept concerns the rationality of guilt, resentment, and other reactive attitudes
  - Planning theory of normative thought: normative thinking is a matter of planning -- forming intentions about what to do in hypothetical circumstances
  - Naturalistic expressivism: normative thought can be fully explained in naturalistic terms without positing non-natural normative facts
  - Moral disagreement is fundamentally disagreement in plan: two people disagree morally when their normative states rule out accepting a single plan for action
- **Key Arguments**:
  - The argument for norm-expressivism: moral judgment is fundamentally about what it makes sense to feel (guilt for wrongdoing, resentment for being wronged); thinking an action is wrong is accepting norms that prescribe guilt for doing it and resentment toward the doer
  - The argument from the planning theory: normative thinking is not belief about facts but a kind of contingency planning; to think one ought to do X in C is to form a plan to do X in C. This accounts for the motivational force of normative judgment
  - The argument for expressivism via the Frege-Geach turn: expressivism can handle negations, conditionals, and other complex constructions by modeling normative language as expressing plans for combinations of action and affect
- **Relevance**: `phil-moral-semantics` (norm-expressivism as a leading contemporary expressivist theory), `phil-moral-psychology` (the role of plans, norms, and emotions in moral thought), `phil-normativity` (the planning theory as an account of normative thought), `phil-moral-disagreement` (expressivist account of the nature of moral disagreement)
- **Related**: Blackburn (fellow expressivist; Gibbard's norm-expressivism and Blackburn's quasi-realism are the two leading contemporary expressivist programs); Hare (prescriptivism is a direct ancestor of Gibbard's planning theory); Scanlon (Gibbard's account of blame engages Scanlon's contractualism); Stevenson (Gibbard follows Stevenson in taking attitudes as central while giving a more systematic account of their logic)
