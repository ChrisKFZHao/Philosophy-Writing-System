# Ayer, A.J. (1910--1989)

- **Tradition**: Logical positivism; analytic philosophy; emotivism in metaethics
- **Major Works**: *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936), *The Foundations of Empirical Knowledge* (1940), *The Problem of Knowledge* (1956)
- **Key Positions**:
  - The verification principle: a statement is cognitively meaningful only if it is either analytic (true by definition) or empirically verifiable (testable by experience)
  - Emotivism: moral judgments are not statements of fact but expressions of emotion; to say "stealing is wrong" is to say "stealing!!" in a shocked tone -- it is not a truth-apt claim
  - Metaphysical statements, theological claims, and ethical pronouncements are all cognitively meaningless because they are neither analytic nor empirically verifiable
  - All necessary truths are analytic; there is no synthetic a priori, and Kant was wrong about substantive a priori knowledge
  - Moral disagreement is reducible to disagreement in attitude, not disagreement about facts
- **Key Arguments**:
  - The argument for emotivism: if I say "stealing is wrong," I am not making a factual claim that can be verified or falsified; I am expressing my disapproval of stealing. The ethical term adds no factual content but serves to express and arouse feeling
  - The argument from the function of ethical terms: ethical terms function to express feeling and stimulate action, not to make assertions; this explains why ethical disagreement persists -- there are no facts to settle the matter
  - The argument against moral naturalism: if "good" meant "conducive to pleasure," then "pleasure is good" would be a tautology; but it is not -- the sentence has substantive content. Therefore naturalistic definitions of moral terms fail
- **Relevance**: `phil-moral-semantics` (emotivism as the classic non-cognitivist theory of moral language), `phil-moral-epistemology` (rejection of moral knowledge as impossible given non-cognitivism), `phil-moral-realism` (emotivism as the anti-realist foil against which realism defines itself), `phil-conceptual-analysis` (verification principle as criterion of meaningfulness)
- **Related**: Moore (Ayer accepts the open question argument against naturalism but draws non-cognitivist conclusions); Stevenson (refined Ayer's crude emotivism into a more sophisticated theory of emotive meaning); Carnap (Ayer's verificationism derives from Carnap and the Vienna Circle); Hare (developed a more rationalist non-cognitivism in prescriptivism, rejecting Ayer's irrationalist emotivism)
