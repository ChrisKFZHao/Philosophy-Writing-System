# Gettier, Edmund (1927--2021)

- **Tradition**: Analytic epistemology
- **Major Works**: "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" (1963) -- a three-page paper that reshaped epistemology
- **Key Positions**:
  - The justified-true-belief (JTB) analysis of knowledge is insufficient: there are cases where a person has a justified true belief that falls short of knowledge
  - Epistemic luck: the Gettier cases show that justification and truth can come apart in ways that prevent knowledge, typically through accidental or lucky connections between justification and truth
  - The classic pattern: a subject justifiably believes a false proposition, validly infers a true proposition from it, and the inferred proposition is true by coincidence
- **Key Arguments**:
  - Case I (Smith and Jones): Smith has strong evidence that Jones will get a job (the company president told him) and that Jones has ten coins in his pocket; Smith infers "the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket"; it turns out Smith himself gets the job and, unknown to Smith, also has ten coins -- Smith's justified true belief is not knowledge
  - Case II (Brown in Barcelona): Smith has strong evidence that Jones owns a Ford; Smith infers "Jones owns a Ford or Brown is in Barcelona"; Jones has sold his Ford (the evidence is now misleading) but, by sheer coincidence, Brown is in Barcelona -- again, justified true belief without knowledge
- **Relevance**: `phil-counterexample` (the Gettier cases are the paradigm example of the counterexample method in analytic philosophy -- two simple cases refute a centuries-old analysis), `phil-thought-experiment` (the Gettier cases as canonical thought experiments in epistemology), `phil-conceptual-analysis` (the JTB analysis and its vulnerability to counterexample), `phil-argument-reconstruct` (the simple logical structure: two premises entail the failure of JTB)
- **Related**: Plato (the *Theaetetus* as the origin point of the JTB analysis); Williamson (rejects the analysis project entirely by taking knowledge as a primitive, unanalyzable state); Nozick (proposed a tracking theory of knowledge partly in response to Gettier); Goldman (causal and reliabilist theories as major post-Gettier responses)
