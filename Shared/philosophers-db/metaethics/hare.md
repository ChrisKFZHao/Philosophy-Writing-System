# Hare, R.M. (1919--2002)

- **Tradition**: Analytic metaethics; prescriptivism; utilitarianism
- **Major Works**: *The Language of Morals* (1952), *Freedom and Reason* (1963), *Moral Thinking* (1981), *Sorting Out Ethics* (1997)
- **Key Positions**:
  - Universal prescriptivism: moral judgments are universalizable prescriptions -- they guide action and commit the speaker to applying the same judgment in relevantly similar circumstances
  - The logic of moral terms: moral words like "ought" have a distinctive logical grammar involving prescriptivity (action-guidingness) and universalizability
  - Two-level utilitarianism: moral thinking operates at two levels -- the intuitive level (governed by prima facie principles) and the critical level (governed by act-utilitarian calculation)
  - Non-cognitivism with rational constraints: moral judgments are not descriptive beliefs, but they are constrained by logic, consistency, and universalizability
  - The logical character of moral language forces a utilitarian conclusion: if one must prescribe universally, one must take into account everyone's preferences impartially
- **Key Arguments**:
  - The argument from the logical character of "ought": from the fact that moral judgments are universalizable prescriptions, it follows (via the logical properties of these concepts) that moral reasoning leads toward utilitarianism
  - The fanatic argument: the universal prescriptivist must accept that if a fanatic is willing to universalize their preference even when occupying the victim's position, their judgment is internally consistent; Hare seeks to show genuine fanaticism is psychologically impossible
  - The argument for the two-level structure: given human cognitive limitations and the need for reliable moral education, reasoning must be split into critical level (for reflective deliberation) and intuitive level (for everyday decision-making)
- **Relevance**: `phil-moral-semantics` (prescriptivism as a rationalist non-cognitivism), `phil-moral-supervenience` (universalizability as a supervenience constraint on moral judgment), `phil-argument-reconstruct` (Hare's logical derivation of utilitarianism from the semantics of moral terms), `phil-moral-disagreement` (logical constraints on moral disagreement)
- **Related**: Kant (Hare's universalizability constraint is a formalized descendant of the categorical imperative); Stevenson (Hare develops Stevenson's non-descriptivism but insists on logical constraints Stevenson downplayed); Mill (Hare's two-level utilitarianism systematizes Mill's qualitative hedonism); Blackburn (both are non-cognitivists but Blackburn's quasi-realism is a more defensible successor)
