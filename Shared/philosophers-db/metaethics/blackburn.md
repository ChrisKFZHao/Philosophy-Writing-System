# Blackburn, Simon (b. 1944)

- **Tradition**: Analytic metaethics; quasi-realism; expressivism
- **Major Works**: *Spreading the Word* (1984), *Essays in Quasi-Realism* (1993), *Ruling Passions* (1998), *Truth: A Guide* (2005), *Think* (1999)
- **Key Positions**:
  - Quasi-realism: starting from an expressivist (anti-realist) position about moral judgment, a quasi-realist shows how we can legitimately earn the right to talk as if moral properties exist, moral truths obtain, and moral knowledge is possible
  - Moral judgments express attitudes, not beliefs; to call something "wrong" is to avow a negative sentiment, not to describe a fact
  - The supervenience challenge: the quasi-realist must explain why anyone who holds a moral attitude toward something must hold the same attitude toward anything sharing all its natural properties
  - Projectivism: we project our sentiments onto the world and then mistakenly take them to be properties of things; the quasi-realist shows this projection is legitimate
  - The Frege-Geach problem is the central challenge: expressivism must explain how moral terms function in embedded contexts (negation, conditionals) without treating them as descriptive
  - Truth can be understood deflationarily: the quasi-realist may legitimately speak of moral truths without commitment to substantive moral realism
- **Key Arguments**:
  - The quasi-realist program: expressivism can explain all the realist features of moral discourse (truth-aptness, embedding in conditionals, moral progress) by showing that expressing attitudes naturally evolves to include these features
  - The argument from the best explanation of supervenience: if moral properties were objective, their supervenience on natural properties would be mysterious; if they are projections of evaluative sensibilities, supervenience is explained by evaluation tracking natural patterns
  - The staircase argument against the Frege-Geach problem: expressivism can handle logical complexity by modeling moral terms as devices for expressing attitudes toward combined objects of evaluation, ascending step by step through increasing complexity
- **Relevance**: `phil-moral-semantics` (quasi-realism as the most sophisticated non-cognitivist semantic theory), `phil-moral-realism` (quasi-realism profoundly challenges the realism/anti-realism distinction), `phil-moral-supervenience` (the supervenience challenge as a canonical formulation of the explanatory burden on expressivists), `phil-moral-psychology` (the role of attitudes and sentiments in moral judgment)
- **Related**: Mackie (Blackburn accepts Mackie's projectivism but rejects error theory; quasi-realism says we can talk as if moral facts exist without believing in them); Hare (Blackburn develops Hare's non-cognitivism but replaces prescriptivism with expressivism of attitudes); Ayer (crude emotivism as the ancestor of Blackburn's refined expressivism); Gibbard (fellow expressivist; norm-expressivism is the closest relative to quasi-realism)
