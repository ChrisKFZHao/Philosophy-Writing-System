# Glossary of Metaethics

## Introduction

This glossary defines the core technical vocabulary of analytic metaethics as it emerged through the 20th and 21st centuries. Metaethics is the branch of moral philosophy that investigates the metaphysical, epistemological, semantic, and psychological presuppositions of moral discourse and practice. Unlike normative ethics (which asks what we ought to do) or applied ethics (which asks how principles apply to cases), metaethics asks second-order questions: What does it mean to say something is right or wrong? Are moral claims capable of being true or false? Do moral properties exist, and if so, what kind of thing are they? How, if at all, can we come to know moral truths?

The entries below are organized into seven thematic clusters, reflecting major subdomains of inquiry within the field. Each entry provides a precise definition, identifies significant variants where applicable, and supplies canonical source references. Cross-references within entries are marked in **bold**; a term appearing in bold signals that its own entry in a different cluster supplies further elaboration. This glossary draws primarily on the Anglophone analytic tradition from G.E. Moore's *Principia Ethica* (1903) through the most influential contemporary contributions. It is intended to serve as a knowledge base for metaethics analysis skills across the full range of positions, arguments, and debates surveyed here.

## 1. Realism and Anti-Realism

### Moral Realism

The thesis that there are mind-independent moral facts or properties, and that at least some moral claims are true in virtue of those facts. Realism minimally involves two commitments: (a) a **cognitivist** semantics, holding that moral statements express beliefs that are **truth-apt**; and (b) a success thesis, holding that some such beliefs are true. Realism divides into several distinct varieties. **Robust realism** (Enoch 2011), the strongest variant, holds that moral truths are irreducibly normative, stance-independent, and not reducible to or derivable from non-normative facts; Enoch defends this via an indispensability argument paralleling arguments for mathematical realism. **Quietist realism** (Scanlon 1998, 2014; Dworkin 1996, 2011) resists metaphysical interpretation of moral truths altogether, arguing that the normative domain is autonomous and that no informative answer can be given to the question of what moral facts "consist in"; on this view, realism requires no substantive metaphysical commitments beyond the truth of first-order moral judgments. **Naturalist realism** claims that moral properties are identical with or constituted by natural properties, themselves accessible via empirical inquiry. Within naturalism, **synthetic naturalism** (Railton 1986; Boyd 1988) treats the identity between moral and natural properties as a posteriori and synthetic, analogous to the identity of water with H₂O, while **analytic naturalism** (Jackson 1998; Jackson and Pettit 1995) treats the connection as conceptual or a priori, discovered via conceptual analysis and **Ramsey-Carnap-Lewis functionalism** applied to folk morality. **Non-naturalist realism** (Moore 1903; Shafer-Landau 2003; Parfit 2011) holds that moral properties are sui generis, irreducible to natural properties, and known via a distinct faculty or capacity.

"How to Be a Moral Realist." In Sayre-McCord (ed.), *Essays on Moral Realism*. Cornell University Press. | Jackson, F. (1998). *From Metaphysics to Ethics*. Oxford University Press. | Scanlon, T.M. (1998). *What We Owe to Each Other*. Harvard University Press. | Dworkin, R. (1996). "Objectivity and Truth: You'd Better Believe It." *Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 25(2), 87–139. | Shafer-Landau, R. (2003). *Moral Realism: A Defence*. Oxford University Press. | Enoch, D. (2011). *Taking Morality Seriously*. Oxford University Press. | Parfit, D. (2011). *On What Matters*, Vol. 2. Oxford University Press.

### Moral Anti-Realism

An umbrella term covering all views that deny the existence of mind-independent moral facts, or deny that any moral claims are true, or deny that moral claims are truth-apt at all. Anti-realism is not a single positive doctrine but a genus whose species include **error theory**, **non-cognitivism**, **moral relativism**, and **fictionalism**. Anti-realists agree in rejecting the **robust realist** picture, but they diverge sharply on why realism fails and what moral discourse should be understood as doing. Some anti-realists (e.g., error theorists) are cognitivists who think moral claims are false; others (e.g., expressivists) are non-cognitivists who think moral claims are not in the business of stating facts at all. The term is occasionally used interchangeably with "moral irrealism" or "moral nihilism," though nuance differs.

*Key sources:* Sayre-McCord, G. (ed.) (1988). *Essays on Moral Realism*. Cornell University Press. | Joyce, R. (2001). *The Myth of Morality*. Cambridge University Press.

### Error Theory (Mackie)

The thesis, associated paradigmatically with J.L. Mackie, that first-order moral discourse is systematically false: moral statements are **cognitivist** in form (they purport to state facts) and carry an implicit claim to objective prescriptivity, but the properties they invoke do not exist. Mackie's argument proceeds in two stages. The **conceptual claim** is that ordinary moral judgment presupposes that moral values are objectively prescriptive—"queer" entities that would be intrinsically action-guiding and knowable by some mysterious faculty. The **ontological claim** is that no such entities exist; the world contains nothing answering to that description. Mackie supports the ontological claim with two sub-arguments: the **argument from queerness** (objective prescriptivity would be metaphysically and epistemologically bizarre) and the **argument from relativity** (the best explanation of persistent **moral disagreement** is not that one party tracks objective moral facts while another fails to, but that moral judgments reflect divergent ways of life). Unlike **non-cognitivism**, error theory grants that moral utterances are assertions but concludes they are uniformly untrue, much as an atheist judges all claims about Zeus to be false.

*Key sources:* Mackie, J.L. (1977). *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong*. Penguin. | Joyce, R. (2001). *The Myth of Morality*. Cambridge University Press. | Olson, J. (2014). *Moral Error Theory: History, Critique, Defence*. Oxford University Press.

### Non-Cognitivism

The view that moral statements are not **truth-apt**: they do not express beliefs or make factual assertions, and consequently are neither true nor false. Non-cognitivism is a family of theories united by the claim that the surface grammar of moral language is misleading; while sentences like "Murder is wrong" share the declarative form of factual claims, their function is non-descriptive. The classic forms are **emotivism** (Ayer 1936; Stevenson 1944), which holds that moral utterances express or evince emotions and aim to influence the attitudes of others; **prescriptivism** (Hare 1952, 1963), which holds that they prescribe or commend courses of action universally; and **expressivism** (Blackburn 1984; Gibbard 1990, 2003), a more sophisticated descendant that treats moral utterances as expressions of conative or plan-like states and offers systematic compositional semantics. The principal challenge to all non-cognitivist views is the **Frege-Geach problem**.

*Key sources:* Ayer, A.J. (1936). *Language, Truth and Logic*. Gollancz. | Stevenson, C.L. (1944). *Ethics and Language*. Yale University Press. | Hare, R.M. (1952). *The Language of Morals*. Oxford University Press.

### Expressivism

A form of **non-cognitivism** holding that the meaning of a moral sentence is explained by the mental state it conventionally expresses. Unlike simple emotivism, expressivism aspires to provide a compositional semantics that accounts for the behavior of moral terms in embedded contexts (see **Frege-Geach problem**). **Blackburn's expressivism** (1984, 1993, 1998) treats moral judgment as the expression of a conative attitude—an approval or disapproval of sensibilities—and supplements this with a **quasi-realist** program that aims to explain why moral discourse mimics realist-sounding features (truth-talk, objectivity, knowledge-attributions). **Gibbard's norm-expressivism** (1990, 2003), later refined as "plan-laden expressivism," analyzes normative judgment as the expression of a state of planning or contingency-planning: to judge that an action is wrong is to express a plan to blame the agent. Expressivism today encompasses a range of sophisticated semantic proposals including **hybrid expressivism** and **relational expressivism**.

*Key sources:* Blackburn, S. (1984). *Spreading the Word*. Oxford University Press. | Blackburn, S. (1998). *Ruling Passions*. Oxford University Press. | Gibbard, A. (1990). *Wise Choices, Apt Feelings*. Harvard University Press. | Gibbard, A. (2003). *Thinking How to Live*. Harvard University Press.

### Prescriptivism

The metaethical theory developed by R.M. Hare (1952, 1963, 1981) according to which moral judgments are a species of prescription—they are action-guiding utterances that commend, advise, or direct. Hare's analysis yields two defining features: **universalizability** (a moral judgment about one case commits the speaker to the same judgment in any relevantly similar case) and **prescriptivity** (sincerely assenting to a moral judgment entails being motivated to act accordingly, linking prescriptivism to **motivational internalism**). Unlike **emotivism**, prescriptivism is governed by logical constraints; moral discourse admits of rational argument via consistency-testing. Hare's later work (1981) develops a two-level utilitarian framework in which "critical" thinking deploys act-utilitarian reasoning while "intuitive" thinking relies on prima facie principles, with both levels governed by the logic of universal prescription.

*Key sources:* Hare, R.M. (1952). *The Language of Morals*. Oxford University Press. | Hare, R.M. (1963). *Freedom and Reason*. Oxford University Press. | Hare, R.M. (1981). *Moral Thinking*. Oxford University Press.

### Quasi-Realism

A project rather than a thesis, associated with Simon Blackburn (1984, 1993, 1998): the attempt to "earn the right" to realist-sounding moral discourse—talk of moral truth, knowledge, objectivity, and moral facts—from an **expressivist** starting point that denies these notions their full realist interpretation. Quasi-realism aims to show that the surface features of moral thought and language that appear to presuppose **moral realism** can be explained without the metaphysical commitments realism incurs. It is a deflationary, projectivist program: we project attitudes onto the world and then treat the resulting discourse as if it described independent facts, and the quasi-realist aims to show that there is nothing illegitimate about doing so. Blackburn appeals to a **minimalist** or deflationary account of truth, holding that to call a moral claim "true" is simply to endorse it, not to posit a metaphysical truth-maker. Quasi-realism thus positions itself as a third way between full-blooded realism and eliminativist anti-realism.

*Key sources:* Blackburn, S. (1984). *Spreading the Word*. Oxford University Press. | Blackburn, S. (1993). *Essays in Quasi-Realism*. Oxford University Press. | Blackburn, S. (1998). *Ruling Passions*. Oxford University Press.

### Fictionalism

A family of anti-realist views holding that moral discourse involves some form of pretense or fiction. **Revolutionary fictionalism** (Joyce 2001, 2005) recommends that, having accepted **error theory**, we continue to employ moral discourse as a useful fiction—uttering moral sentences while not asserting them—because moral thinking serves practical purposes (e.g., stabilizing cooperation) even if it lacks truth. **Hermeneutic fictionalism** (Kalderon 2005) makes the descriptive, interpretive claim that ordinary speakers already use moral language non-assertorically, engaging in a kind of make-believe rather than asserting falsehoods; this variant avoids the revisionary character of revolutionary fictionalism. Fictionalism is thus compatible with **cognitivism** about moral propositions (one can think they are false) while recommending a non-assertoric linguistic practice. Fictionalism in metaethics draws explicitly on Kendall Walton's (1990) general theory of make-believe and fictional discourse.

*Key sources:* Joyce, R. (2001). *The Myth of Morality*. Cambridge University Press. | Joyce, R. (2005). "Moral Fictionalism." In Kalderon (ed.), *Fictionalism in Metaphysics*. Oxford University Press. | Kalderon, M.E. (2005). *Moral Fictionalism*. Oxford University Press. | Walton, K. (1990). *Mimesis as Make-Believe*. Harvard University Press.

### Moral Relativism

The thesis that the truth or justification of moral judgments is relative to a framework—typically a culture, convention, or individual standpoint—rather than absolute. **Agent relativism** (sometimes called "appraiser relativism") holds that moral claims are true or false relative to the moral standards of the person making the judgment; **appraiser relativism** (or "assessor relativism") holds that truth is relative to the context of assessment. **Harman's moral relativism** (1975, 2000) ties morality to an implicit agreement among a group: moral judgments are true relative to a framework of reasons generated by such an agreement, and there is no further question of whether the framework itself is correct. **Wong's pluralistic relativism** (1984, 2006) accommodates universal constraints on adequate moralities (grounded in human nature and the requirements of social cooperation) while insisting that multiple incompatible moralities can satisfy those constraints; the result is a limited relativism that permits objective cross-cultural criticism within limits. Moral relativism is distinct from **error theory** (which denies that any moral claims are true) and from descriptive cultural relativism (the anthropological observation that moral codes vary).

*Key sources:* Harman, G. (1975). "Moral Relativism Defended." *Philosophical Review*, 84(1), 3–22. | Harman, G., and Thomson, J.J. (1996). *Moral Relativism and Moral Objectivity*. Blackwell. | Wong, D. (1984). *Moral Relativity*. University of California Press. | Wong, D. (2006). *Natural Moralities*. Oxford University Press.

### Cornell Realism

A distinctive version of **synthetic naturalism** developed at Cornell University in the 1980s by Nicholas Sturgeon (1985), Richard Boyd (1988), and David Brink (1989). Cornell realists combine a causal theory of reference (drawing on Putnam and Kripke) with a commitment to **moral realism**: moral terms like "right" and "good" refer rigidly to natural properties, but the reference relation is fixed not by speakers' concepts or platitudes but by causal interaction between speakers and the world. This allows Cornell realists to concede Moore's **open question argument** (speakers may be unable to articulate the natural property they are tracking) while insisting that moral terms nonetheless refer to natural properties. The view is allied with **motivational externalism** and rejects the necessary connection between moral judgment and motivation, holding that moral beliefs represent facts about human flourishing or social cooperation and that their motivational force is supplied by contingent desires. Cornell realism has been criticized for being insufficiently normative and for relying on an implausible causal epistemology for moral properties, given that moral properties, unlike natural-kind properties, appear causally inert. The Moral Twin Earth argument (see separate entry) is the most prominent semantic challenge to Cornell realism.

*Key sources:* Sturgeon, N. (1985). "Moral Explanations." In Copp and Zimmerman (eds.), *Morality, Reason and Truth*. Rowman & Allanheld. | Boyd, R. (1988). "How to Be a Moral Realist." In Sayre-McCord (ed.), *Essays on Moral Realism*. Cornell University Press. | Brink, D.O. (1989). *Moral Realism and the Foundations of Ethics*. Cambridge University Press.

### Moral Twin Earth Argument

A semantic argument against **Cornell realism** and **synthetic naturalism** developed by Terence Horgan and Mark Timmons (1991, 1992). The argument adapts Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment to moral semantics. Imagine a community on Moral Twin Earth where the term "right" is causally regulated by a different natural property than on Earth (say, consequentialist-property on Earth, deontological-property on Twin Earth). On the causal theory of reference, the two communities refer to different properties when using "right." But intuitively, the two communities genuinely disagree about what is right—they share a concept and are engaged in a substantive normative dispute, not merely talking past each other. The argument concludes that the causal theory of reference fails for moral terms, and that the best explanation of moral disagreement is that moral terms have their semantic content fixed by functional role or conceptual platitudes, not by causal links to natural properties. This is an argument against synthetic naturalism in favor of **analytic naturalism** or **non-cognitivist** alternatives.

*Key sources:* Horgan, T., and Timmons, M. (1991). "New Wave Moral Realism Meets Moral Twin Earth." *Journal of Philosophical Research*, 16, 447–465. | Horgan, T., and Timmons, M. (1992). "Troubles for New Wave Moral Semantics: The 'Open Question Argument' Revived." *Philosophical Papers*, 21(3), 153–175.

## 2. Cognitivism and Semantics

### Cognitivism vs. Non-Cognitivism

**Cognitivism** holds that moral sentences express beliefs, are **truth-apt** (capable of being true or false), and that their primary function is to describe or represent moral reality. Cognitivism is a semantic thesis and does not by itself entail **moral realism**, since one can be a cognitivist who holds that all moral beliefs are false (**error theory**) or true only relative to a framework (**moral relativism**). **Non-cognitivism** denies these claims: moral sentences do not express beliefs, are not truth-apt, and their function is non-descriptive—typically expressive, prescriptive, or performative. The cognitivsm/non-cognitivism distinction is orthogonal to the realism/anti-realism distinction: a realist must be a cognitivist, but a cognitivist need not be a realist.

*Key sources:* van Roojen, M. (2015). *Metaethics: A Contemporary Introduction*. Routledge. | Miller, A. (2013). *Contemporary Metaethics: An Introduction*, 2nd ed. Polity.

### Truth-Aptness

The capacity of a sentence or utterance to bear a truth-value—to be true or false. A sentence is truth-apt if it makes a claim about how things are, such that it is evaluable in terms of truth and falsity. Truth-aptness is the minimal semantic commitment of **cognitivism**. The scope of truth-aptness is contested: **non-cognitivists** deny that moral sentences are truth-apt; **minimalists** about truth argue that truth-aptness is a trivial property, present in any discourse that exhibits appropriate surface syntactic features (disquotation, embedding, etc.), thereby threatening to collapse the distinction between cognitivism and non-cognitivism.

*Key sources:* Boghossian, P. (1990). "The Status of Content." *Philosophical Review*, 99(2), 157–184. | Wright, C. (1992). *Truth and Objectivity*. Harvard University Press.

### The Frege-Geach Problem (Embedding Problem)

The central challenge for **non-cognitivist** theories, first articulated by Geach (1960, 1965) drawing on Frege's distinction between asserted and unasserted occurrences of propositions. The problem: if the meaning of a moral predicate is explained in terms of the expression of a non-cognitive attitude (say, disapproval), how does that predicate function when it occurs in unasserted contexts—the antecedents of conditionals ("If lying is wrong, then getting your sister to lie is wrong"), questions ("Is lying wrong?"), negation ("Lying is not wrong"), and other embedded positions where the speaker does not express the attitude in question? A compositional semantics for moral language must assign the same meaning to a moral predicate across asserted and unasserted contexts. The Frege-Geach problem demands that non-cognitivists supply such a semantics, and much of the technical development in **expressivism** (e.g., Blackburn's commitment-theoretic semantics, Gibbard's possible-worlds semantics for norms, and more recent **hybrid** and **relational expressivist** proposals) has been driven by the attempt to solve it. The problem is also called the "embedding problem" because it centers on embedded occurrences.

*Key sources:* Geach, P.T. (1960). "Ascriptivism." *Philosophical Review*, 69(2), 221–225. | Geach, P.T. (1965). "Assertion." *Philosophical Review*, 74(4), 449–465. | Schroeder, M. (2008). *Being For: Evaluating the Semantic Program of Expressivism*. Oxford University Press. | Unwin, N. (1999). "Quasi-Realism, Negation and the Frege-Geach Problem." *Philosophical Quarterly*, 49(196), 337–352.

### Minimalism/Deflationism about Truth

A family of views holding that truth is not a substantive property and that the concept of truth is exhaustively captured by the equivalence schema: "P" is true if and only if P. On this view, to assert that a proposition is true adds nothing beyond asserting the proposition itself. Minimalism plays a pivotal role in metaethics because it erodes the barrier between **cognitivism** and **non-cognitivism**: if truth is a logically transparent, non-substantive notion, then expressivists can freely deploy truth-talk about moral judgments without incurring realist metaphysical commitments. A moral sentence S can be called "true" simply in virtue of the speaker's endorsement of S. Blackburn's **quasi-realism** depends heavily on a minimalist account of truth. Critics argue that minimalism trivializes the truth predicate too far and that the interesting question is not whether moral discourse employs the word "true" but whether it aims at robust, representational truth.

*Key sources:* Horwich, P. (1990). *Truth*. Blackwell. | Wright, C. (1992). *Truth and Objectivity*. Harvard University Press. | Blackburn, S. (1998). *Ruling Passions*. Oxford University Press.

### The Open Question Argument

G.E. Moore's (1903) argument against analytic naturalism (the view that "good" can be defined in natural terms such as "pleasurable" or "desired"). For any proposed naturalistic definition of "good" of the form "Good = N," it remains an open question—a question that is not settled merely by linguistic competence—whether N is indeed good. If the definition were correct, Moore contends, the question would be closed: asking "Is N good?" would be like asking "Is an unmarried man a bachelor?" The open question argument purports to show that goodness is a simple, indefinable, non-natural property. The argument has been challenged on multiple grounds: that open questions can persist even for correct analytic identities (the **paradox of analysis**), that it begs the question by assuming a picture of conceptual analysis that naturalists reject, and that sophisticated forms of **synthetic naturalism** and **analytic naturalism** (e.g., via functional role analysis) evade it. Nevertheless, it remains one of the most influential arguments in 20th-century metaethics.

*Key sources:* Moore, G.E. (1903). *Principia Ethica*. Cambridge University Press. | Darwall, S., Gibbard, A., and Railton, P. (1992). "Toward Fin de Siècle Ethics: Some Trends." *Philosophical Review*, 101(1), 115–189.

### The Naturalistic Fallacy

Moore's (1903) charge that any attempt to define "good" in naturalistic terms commits a fallacy—a mistake in reasoning, not a logical error. Specifically, the fallacy consists in identifying the property goodness with some natural property and then supposing that this identification licenses substantive normative conclusions without further argument. Moore's target includes both naturalistic definitions (e.g., "good = pleasant") and metaphysical definitions (e.g., "good = that which the universe tends toward"). The naturalistic fallacy is distinct from, though related to, **Hume's Law** (the is-ought gap): Moore's charge is that naturalists mistake a synthetic identity for an analytic one, not that they derive an "ought" from an "is." The term has generated considerable confusion because Moore's own non-naturalism holds that goodness supervenes on natural properties, which is itself a synthetic necessary connection of the sort Moore appears to reject.

*Key sources:* Moore, G.E. (1903). *Principia Ethica*. Cambridge University Press. | Frankena, W. (1939). "The Naturalistic Fallacy." *Mind*, 48(192), 464–477.

### Ramsey-Carnap-Lewis Functionalism

A method for defining theoretical terms, originating with Frank Ramsey and refined by David Lewis (1970, 1972), adopted in metaethics primarily by Frank Jackson (1998) in defense of **analytic naturalism**. The method proceeds by collecting the platitudes of folk morality—the widely shared, pre-theoretical claims that constitute ordinary moral understanding (e.g., "If an action is right, there is a reason to do it"; "It is wrong to cause suffering for fun"). These platitudes are conjoined into a single long sentence containing moral predicates. The moral predicates are then replaced with existentially quantified variables, producing a Ramsey sentence that says: there exist properties X1, X2, ... such that the platitudes hold of X1, X2, ... . The properties that realize this functional role are the referents of moral terms. The analysis is a priori because the platitudes fix the reference of moral terms by conceptual entailment, but identifying which natural properties play the role requires a posteriori investigation. The method is designed to evade Moore's **open question argument**: if "good" is functionally defined via the platitudes, questions of the form "Is this good?" are not open in the problematic sense, because the platitudes themselves fix the meaning.

*Key sources:* Lewis, D. (1970). "How to Define Theoretical Terms." *Journal of Philosophy*, 67(13), 427–446. | Lewis, D. (1972). "Psychophysical and Theoretical Identifications." *Australasian Journal of Philosophy*, 50(3), 249–258. | Jackson, F. (1998). *From Metaphysics to Ethics*. Oxford University Press.

### Synthetic Naturalism vs. Analytic Naturalism

A major division within **naturalist moral realism**. **Synthetic naturalism** (Railton 1986; Boyd 1988) holds that the identity between moral and natural properties is a posteriori and synthetic, analogous to theoretical identity statements in science (e.g., "water = H₂O," "heat = mean molecular kinetic energy"). On this view, the good is identified with what would satisfy certain objective interests under idealized epistemic conditions, discovered empirically rather than by analysis of concepts. **Analytic naturalism** (Jackson 1998; Jackson and Pettit 1995) holds that the connection between moral and natural properties is conceptual and knowable a priori. Using the method of **Ramsey-Carnap-Lewis functionalism**, one collects the platitudes of folk morality into a Ramsey sentence, replaces moral predicates with variables, and then discovers which natural properties satisfy the resulting functional role. The analysis is a priori because it proceeds via conceptual entailment from the platitudes, though identifying the realizers of the role requires empirical inquiry. The debate between these variants concerns the epistemology of moral-natural property identity and the extent to which conceptual analysis can fix moral reference.

*Key sources:* Railton, P. (1986). "Moral Realism." *Philosophical Review*, 95(2), 163–207. | Boyd, R. (1988). "How to Be a Moral Realist." In Sayre-McCord (ed.), *Essays on Moral Realism*. Cornell University Press. | Jackson, F. (1998). *From Metaphysics to Ethics*. Oxford University Press. | Jackson, F., and Pettit, P. (1995). "Moral Functionalism and Moral Motivation." *Philosophical Quarterly*, 45(178), 20–40.

## 3. Normativity and Reasons

### Normativity

The distinctive "ought"-ness or reason-giving character of moral and other normative claims. A claim is normative if it guides action, thought, or feeling—if it says not merely how things are but how they ought to be, what we have reason to do, believe, or feel. Metaethicists distinguish several dimensions of normativity: **formal normativity** (conformity to a standard or rule), **rational normativity** (what one has reason to do), and **moral normativity** (what one morally ought to do). A central question is whether moral normativity is a species of rational normativity or an independent domain. The challenge for the naturalist is to explain how natural facts can be normative—how an "is" can entail an "ought." See also **Hume's Law**, **categorical vs. hypothetical imperatives**.

*Key sources:* Korsgaard, C. (1996). *The Sources of Normativity*. Cambridge University Press. | Parfit, D. (2011). *On What Matters*. Oxford University Press. | Finlay, S. (2014). *Confusion of Tongues: A Theory of Normative Language*. Oxford University Press.

### Categorical vs. Hypothetical Imperatives

A distinction drawn from Kant's moral philosophy and refined within contemporary metaethics. A **categorical imperative** commands an action as good in itself, unconditionally, without reference to any further end; it applies to agents regardless of their contingent desires or goals. Kant's Formula of Universal Law ("Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law") is the paradigmatic example. A **hypothetical imperative** commands an action as a means to some end the agent already has: "If you want X, you ought to do Y." Philippa Foot (1972) influentially challenged the notion that morality supplies categorical imperatives, arguing that moral requirements bind only hypothetically, relative to the agent's commitments or the standards of a shared practice. The debate connects to **internal vs. external reasons**: if all reasons are internal (Williams), morality cannot issue genuinely categorical demands; if there are external reasons, it may be able to.

*Key sources:* Kant, I. (1785). *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*. | Foot, P. (1972). "Morality as a System of Hypothetical Imperatives." *Philosophical Review*, 81(3), 305–316. | Kant, I. (1788). *Critique of Practical Reason*.

### Internal vs. External Reasons

A debate about the nature of reasons for action, framed by Bernard Williams (1981). An **internal reason** is one that bears a rational connection to the agent's existing motivational set (her "subjective motivational set," comprising desires, projects, commitments, and dispositions). On Williams's internalist view, if there is no sound deliberative route from an agent's current motivations to a reason, that statement cannot be a reason for that agent. An **external reason**, by contrast, would be a reason that applies to an agent regardless of her motivational set. Williams denies that there are any such reasons. The internalist position is defended by Williams as supporting a modest **moral relativism** and as consistent with the **Humean Theory of Motivation**. External reasons are defended by McDowell (1995), who argues that a virtuous agent can come to be motivated by considerations that have no rational connection to her prior desires, and by Parfit (2011), who argues that reasons are irreducibly normative facts that hold independently of any agent's psychology. The debate cuts across the **reasons fundamentalism** and **constitutivism** literatures.

*Key sources:* Williams, B. (1981). "Internal and External Reasons." In *Moral Luck*. Cambridge University Press. | Williams, B. (1995). "Internal Reasons and the Obscurity of Blame." In *Making Sense of Humanity*. Cambridge University Press. | McDowell, J. (1995). "Might There Be External Reasons?" In *World, Mind, and Ethics*, ed. Altham and Harrison. Cambridge University Press. | Parfit, D. (2011). *On What Matters*. Oxford University Press.

### Normative vs. Motivating Reasons

A distinction central to action theory and metaethics. A **normative reason** is a consideration that counts in favor of an action—what makes the action right, good, justified, or what there is reason to do (a "justifying reason"). A **motivating reason** is a consideration in light of which an agent acts—what the agent takes to count in favor of the action and on which her action is based (an "explanatory reason"). The distinction separates the justificatory and explanatory dimensions of reasons talk. Both Williams and Parfit deploy this distinction: in asking whether there are external reasons, the question concerns normative reasons; in explaining action, the focus is on motivating reasons. The relationship between the two types of reasons is contested: some hold that a normative reason must be capable of being a motivating reason for the agent (a constraint linked to **motivational internalism**), while others treat them as fully independent domains.

*Key sources:* Dancy, J. (2000). *Practical Reality*. Oxford University Press. | Smith, M. (1994). *The Moral Problem*. Blackwell. | Alvarez, M. (2010). *Kinds of Reasons*. Oxford University Press.

### Constitutivism

The metaethical view that normative standards are derivable from constitutive features of agency, action, or practical identity. The strategy is to locate the source of normativity in what is non-optional for agents as such. **Korsgaard's constitutivism** (1996, 2009) argues that the categorical imperative is constitutive of agency itself: to act at all, an agent must constitute herself as a unified self by adopting a practical identity, and this requires conforming to the universalizability test. **Velleman's constitutivism** (2000, 2009) derives normative standards from the constitutive aim of action (self-understanding or self-knowledge), which generates standards of correctness for action without appealing to substantive normative premises. Constitutivism aims to answer the skeptical question "Why be moral?" by showing that morality is inescapable for agents—a strategy that, if successful, avoids **Hume's Law** by deriving norms from the metaphysics of agency. Critics charge that constitutivism either collapses into emptiness (the derived norms are too thin to yield substantive morality) or smuggles in substantive normative assumptions.

*Key sources:* Korsgaard, C. (1996). *The Sources of Normativity*. Cambridge University Press. | Korsgaard, C. (2009). *Self-Constitution: Agency, Identity, and Integrity*. Oxford University Press. | Velleman, J.D. (2000). *The Possibility of Practical Reason*. Oxford University Press. | Velleman, J.D. (2009). *How We Get Along*. Cambridge University Press.

### Reasons Fundamentalism

The view, defended in different forms by Scanlon (1998, 2014) and Parfit (2011), that the concept of a reason is primitive, irreducible, and the fundamental normative notion in terms of which all other normative concepts (ought, value, rightness) are to be analyzed. On this picture, reasons are not reducible to natural facts, desires, or social conventions; they are normative bedrock. Scanlon's **buck-passing account of value** is a signature form of reasons fundamentalism: for something to be good is for it to have properties that provide reasons to respond to it in certain ways (to admire, pursue, or respect it). Parfit (2011) similarly treats normative reasons as irreducibly normative truths, not identifiable with natural facts in any reductionist sense; he combines this with a **non-naturalist realism** that takes reasons to be objective and mind-independent. Reasons fundamentalism is often paired with a rejection of **analytic naturalism** and with the claim that the naturalist program of locating normativity in the natural world fails.

*Key sources:* Scanlon, T.M. (1998). *What We Owe to Each Other*. Harvard University Press. | Scanlon, T.M. (2014). *Being Realistic about Reasons*. Oxford University Press. | Parfit, D. (2011). *On What Matters*. Oxford University Press.

### Buck-Passing Account of Value

An analysis of value defended by T.M. Scanlon (1998) and named by him, according to which the property of being valuable is a "buck-passing" property: to say that something is good (or valuable) is not to attribute an irreducible, non-natural property to it, but to say that it has other, natural properties that provide reasons to respond to it in certain ways (to admire it, to pursue it, to respect it, etc.). The value property itself does no normative work—it merely "passes the buck" to the underlying reason-giving properties. The buck-passing account is a form of **reasons fundamentalism**, since it analyzes value in terms of primitive reasons. It is neutral between **naturalism** and **non-naturalism**: the reason-giving properties can be natural (as Scanlon's quietist version holds) or irreducibly normative (as Parfit holds). The account is motivated by the thought that if we already know all the natural facts about an object and all the reasons those facts provide, adding that the object is "good" adds nothing further. Critics charge that the buck-passing account fails for attributive uses of "good" ("a good knife," "a good person") where value seems tied to kind-membership standards rather than reasons for pro-attitudes.

*Key sources:* Scanlon, T.M. (1998). *What We Owe to Each Other*. Harvard University Press. | Stratton-Lake, P., and Hooker, B. (2006). "Scanlon versus Moore on Goodness." In Horgan and Timmons (eds.), *Metaethics after Moore*. Oxford University Press. | Suikkanen, J. (2009). "Buck-Passing Accounts of Value." *Philosophy Compass*, 4(5), 768–779.

## 4. Moral Psychology

### Motivational Internalism

The thesis that there is a necessary or internal connection between sincere moral judgment and motivation: necessarily, if an agent genuinely judges that she morally ought to perform some action, she is (at least to some degree) motivated to perform it. The connection may be defeasible (motivation can be overridden by competing desires, weakness of will, or depression) but not contingent. Strong internalism holds that moral judgment entails motivation; weak internalism holds that moral judgment entails motivation only in rationally or psychologically normal agents. Motivational internalism is a premise in the **amoralist** challenge (if an amoralist is conceivable, internalism is false) and plays a crucial role in the **Moral Problem** (Smith 1994): how to reconcile the objectivity of morality with its practical character. Non-cognitivists and prescriptivists typically endorse strong internalism, while naturalist realists and some externalists reject it.

*Key sources:* Smith, M. (1994). *The Moral Problem*. Blackwell. | Brink, D.O. (1989). *Moral Realism and the Foundations of Ethics*. Cambridge University Press. | Björnsson, G., et al. (eds.) (2015). *Motivational Internalism*. Oxford University Press.

### Motivational Externalism

The denial of **motivational internalism**: the connection between moral judgment and motivation is contingent and external. An agent can sincerely judge that she morally ought to perform an action and yet lack any motivation to do so, without thereby exhibiting conceptual confusion or irrationality. Externalism is typically associated with **cognitivist** forms of **moral realism**, since realism treats moral beliefs as representing facts rather than as inherently action-guiding states. The externalist must explain why moral judgment is typically accompanied by motivation (since empirically it often seems to be) without positing a necessary connection; common strategies appeal to sympathy, socialization, or the content of moral beliefs themselves. David Brink (1989) provides a classic defense, arguing that the demands of agency and objectivity pull in opposite directions and that externalism better accommodates the objectivity of morality.

*Key sources:* Brink, D.O. (1989). *Moral Realism and the Foundations of Ethics*. Cambridge University Press. | Svavarsdóttir, S. (1999). "Moral Cognitivism and Motivation." *Philosophical Review*, 108(2), 161–219.

### Humean Theory of Motivation

The thesis that belief alone cannot motivate action; motivation requires the presence of a desire (or other conative state) in addition to any belief. The theory takes its name from Hume's dictum that "reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions" (*Treatise*, 2.3.3). Formally: an action is motivated by a belief-desire pair, where the belief represents a means-end relation and the desire supplies the end. The Humean theory is a psychological claim about the structure of motivation, not a normative claim about what ought to motivate. It is typically endorsed by **non-cognitivists** and **expressivists**, who argue that moral judgment itself is a conative rather than cognitive state precisely because it is intrinsically motivating. The theory is rejected by **anti-Humeans**, who hold that beliefs themselves or rational considerations alone can motivate.

*Key sources:* Hume, D. (1739–40). *A Treatise of Human Nature*. | Smith, M. (1994). *The Moral Problem*. Blackwell. | Sinhababu, N. (2017). *Humean Nature*. Oxford University Press.

### Anti-Humeanism

The rejection of the **Humean Theory of Motivation**. Anti-Humeans hold that beliefs, rational considerations, or cognitive states can motivate action without the mediation of an independent desire. Historically, Kant is the paradigmatic anti-Humean: respect for the moral law, itself a product of pure practical reason, can motivate action without antecedent inclination. In contemporary metaethics, anti-Humeanism appears in several forms: **McDowell's** (1978, 1979) view of the virtuous agent, for whom moral perception directly engages motivation; **Dancy's** (1993) pure cognitivism, on which beliefs about reasons can themselves motivate; and rationalist views that treat rational agency as capable of generating motivation from the recognition of reasons alone. Anti-Humeanism is often paired with **cognitivism** and a rejection of **motivational internalism** of the strong sort.

*Key sources:* McDowell, J. (1978). "Are Moral Requirements Hypothetical Imperatives?" *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society*, Supp. Vol. 52, 13–29. | McDowell, J. (1979). "Virtue and Reason." *The Monist*, 62(3), 331–350. | Dancy, J. (1993). *Moral Reasons*. Blackwell.

### The Amoralist

In metaethics, the amoralist is a thought-experimental figure: someone who makes sincere moral judgments but is systematically unmoved by them—lacking any motivation whatsoever to act in accordance with those judgments. The conceivability (or possibility) of the amoralist is a standard argument against strong **motivational internalism**: if an amoralist is conceptually possible, the connection between moral judgment and motivation cannot be necessary. Externalists deploy the amoralist to show that moral judgment is a cognitive state (a belief) disengaged from motivation in the absence of auxiliary desires. Internalists typically respond that the amoralist is not genuinely making moral judgments—that she is using moral language in an "inverted commas" sense or has not mastered the concept—or that internalism requires only that motivation be present under normal or rational conditions, and the amoralist falls outside those parameters. The amoralist debate connects to **akrasia**, which involves motivation that conflicts with judgment rather than its absence.

*Key sources:* Brink, D.O. (1989). *Moral Realism and the Foundations of Ethics*. Cambridge University Press. | Smith, M. (1994). *The Moral Problem*. Blackwell.

### Akrasia (Weakness of Will)

The phenomenon of acting against one's better judgment—doing what one believes, all things considered, one ought not to do. The ancient term *akrasia* (literally "lack of mastery") captures a familiar experience: I judge that I should not have another drink, yet I do. The philosophical problem is to explain how akrasia is possible without positing a split self or mental fragmentation. In metaethics, akrasia is relevant to **motivational internalism**: if moral judgment necessarily motivates, akratic action appears impossible; since akrasia seems actual, internalism must be qualified (e.g., by claiming that moral judgment motivates only *pro tanto*, not all-things-considered). Akrasia is also central to the debate between **Humeans** and **anti-Humeans**: Humeans can explain akrasia as a conflict between a present desire and a long-term rational judgment, while anti-Humeans must explain how rational judgment can be motivationally overpowered by non-rational forces.

*Key sources:* Davidson, D. (1970). "How Is Weakness of the Will Possible?" In *Essays on Actions and Events*. Oxford University Press. | Mele, A. (1987). *Irrationality*. Oxford University Press. | Holton, R. (2009). *Willing, Wanting, Waiting*. Oxford University Press.

### Moral Emotions

A set of reactive attitudes and self-directed emotions that constitute the affective core of moral practice. P.F. Strawson's (1962) landmark essay "Freedom and Resentment" identifies the reactive attitudes—**resentment** (a response to injury from another), **indignation** (a vicarious form of resentment on behalf of another), and **guilt** (the first-personal analogue of resentment, felt when one has wronged another)—as central to the fabric of interpersonal moral life. These emotions presuppose that agents are responsible, and Strawson argues that the question of moral responsibility is not a theoretical question about **determinism vs. free will** but a practical question about when it is appropriate to suspend the reactive attitudes. **Shame**, distinguished from guilt, involves a negative assessment of the self as a whole rather than of a particular action, and has received renewed attention via Williams (1993). Beyond the reactive attitudes, moral psychology investigates **empathy** (Slote 2010), **disgust** (Kelly 2011), and **sympathy** (Darwall 1998) within the economy of moral motivation and judgment.

*Key sources:* Strawson, P.F. (1962). "Freedom and Resentment." *Proceedings of the British Academy*, 48, 1–25. | Williams, B. (1993). *Shame and Necessity*. University of California Press. | Gibbard, A. (1990). *Wise Choices, Apt Feelings*. Harvard University Press. | Prinz, J. (2007). *The Emotional Construction of Morals*. Oxford University Press.

### The Moral Problem (Smith)

The formulation, due to Michael Smith (1994), of a trilemma that frames much of contemporary metaethics. Three platitudes about morality appear to be jointly inconsistent: (1) **Objectivity**: moral judgments express beliefs about objective facts (a **cognitivist** commitment); (2) **Practicality**: moral judgments are intrinsically motivating—to sincerely judge that one ought to phi is, ceteris paribus, to be motivated to phi (**motivational internalism**); (3) **Humean Theory of Motivation**: belief alone cannot motivate; motivation always requires a desire in addition to belief. The apparent tension is: if (1) and (3) hold, then moral beliefs, being beliefs, cannot motivate on their own, contradicting (2); if (2) and (3) hold, then moral judgments must be desire-like states, not beliefs, contradicting (1); if (1) and (2) hold, then Humeanism must be false. Smith's own solution is to reject strong **motivational internalism** in favor of a rationalist account: a rational agent who believes she ought to phi will be motivated to phi, not because moral beliefs intrinsically motivate, but because rationality itself generates desires that align with one's normative beliefs. The Moral Problem's trilemma sets the agenda for much post-1994 metaethics, and different theories can be located by which horn they grasp: non-cognitivists accommodate (2) and (3), externalists accommodate (1) and (3), and anti-Humeans reject (3).

*Key sources:* Smith, M. (1994). *The Moral Problem*. Blackwell. | Smith, M. (1995). "Internal Reasons." *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research*, 55(1), 109–131. | Dreier, J. (2015). "Another World: The Metaethics and Metametaethics of Reasons Fundamentalism." In *Passions and Projections*, ed. Johnson and Smith. Oxford University Press.

## 5. Moral Epistemology

### Moral Intuitionism

The view that there are basic, non-inferentially justified moral beliefs—moral intuitions—that serve as epistemic foundations for moral knowledge. Intuitionism comes in several varieties. **Rationalist intuitionism** (Moore 1903; Ross 1930; Audi 2004) holds that we grasp self-evident moral truths through a faculty of rational intuition or intellectual seeming, analogous to our grasp of mathematical or logical axioms. Ross's (1930) *prima facie* duties are known via this capacity. **Perceptual intuitionism** or **moral perception** views hold that we can literally perceive moral properties or moral aspects of situations. **Sentimentalist intuitionism** treats moral intuitions as grounded in emotional responses rather than rational insight. Intuitionism faces epistemological challenges: the variation in moral intuitions across cultures and individuals is used to challenge their reliability (**debunking arguments**), and critics charge that appeal to a *sui generis* faculty of moral intuition is methodologically suspect. Contemporary intuitionists respond by moderating claims to self-evidence and integrating intuitions within a broader **reflective equilibrium** framework.

*Key sources:* Moore, G.E. (1903). *Principia Ethica*. Cambridge University Press. | Ross, W.D. (1930). *The Right and the Good*. Oxford University Press. | Audi, R. (2004). *The Good in the Right*. Princeton University Press. | Stratton-Lake, P. (2002). *Ethical Intuitionism: Re-evaluations*. Oxford University Press.

### Reflective Equilibrium

A method of moral justification developed by Rawls (1951, 1971) and subsequently adopted as a general account of moral epistemology. The method proceeds by moving back and forth between considered moral judgments about particular cases and general moral principles that are meant to systematize them, adjusting each in light of the other until a coherent equilibrium is achieved. **Narrow reflective equilibrium** considers only moral judgments and principles; **wide reflective equilibrium** incorporates background theories from psychology, sociology, metaphysics, and the natural sciences, and involves coherence across the full web of belief. Reflective equilibrium is a **coherentist** methodology: it treats justification as a function of overall coherence rather than foundationally grounded basic beliefs. Daniels (1979, 1996) argued that wide reflective equilibrium is the method appropriate to moral theorizing in general. Critics charge that reflective equilibrium is methodologically conservative (privileging initial intuitions), lacks an account of why coherence is truth-conducive, and cannot ground objectivity without assuming a convergence that is not empirically guaranteed.

*Key sources:* Rawls, J. (1951). "Outline of a Decision Procedure for Ethics." *Philosophical Review*, 60(2), 177–197. | Rawls, J. (1971). *A Theory of Justice*. Harvard University Press. | Daniels, N. (1979). "Wide Reflective Equilibrium and Theory Acceptance in Ethics." *Journal of Philosophy*, 76(5), 256–282. | Daniels, N. (1996). *Justice and Justification*. Cambridge University Press.

### Coherentism about Moral Justification

The epistemological view that moral beliefs are justified not by inference from foundational, self-evident premises (as in **moral intuitionism**) but by their coherence with an overall system of moral and non-moral beliefs. Coherentism denies that there are epistemically privileged moral beliefs. Justification is holistic, non-linear, and a matter of degree. The leading advocate is Norman Daniels (1979, 1996), who defends **wide reflective equilibrium** as a coherentist method. Coherentism faces standard objections from the epistemology literature—the isolation objection (a coherent system can be disconnected from reality) and the alternative coherent systems problem (multiple incompatible systems can be equally coherent)—applied to the moral domain. Coherentists respond that wide reflective equilibrium's inclusion of non-moral background theories provides empirical anchoring, and that convergence on a single coherent system is a regulative ideal.

*Key sources:* Daniels, N. (1979). "Wide Reflective Equilibrium and Theory Acceptance in Ethics." *Journal of Philosophy*, 76(5), 256–282. | Sayre-McCord, G. (1996). "Coherentist Epistemology and Moral Theory." In Sinnott-Armstrong and Timmons (eds.), *Moral Knowledge?* Oxford University Press.

### Moral Perception

The view, associated primarily with Sarah McGrath (2004) and Robert Audi (2013), that moral properties can be objects of perceptual experience, in something like the way that colors, shapes, or the emotional states of others are perceived. On this account, a suitably sensitive agent can literally see that an action is wrong or hear that a remark is cruel, just as she can see that a ball is red. The perceptual model is meant to avoid the mystery of a specialized rational faculty (**moral intuitionism**) and to ground moral knowledge in ordinary perceptual capacities. Critics argue that moral perception collapses into inference: to "see" that an act is wrong, one must already possess moral concepts and apply them to the perceived non-moral facts—the perception is not of moral properties *simpliciter* but of non-moral properties that trigger moral responses. Defenders respond that concept-ladenness characterizes all perception, including color perception, and does not disqualify a perceptual account.

*Key sources:* McGrath, S. (2004). "Moral Knowledge by Perception." *Philosophical Perspectives*, 18, 209–228. | Audi, R. (2013). *Moral Perception*. Princeton University Press. | Cullison, A. (2010). "Moral Perception." *European Journal of Philosophy*, 18(2), 159–175.

### Evolutionary Debunking Arguments

A family of epistemological arguments that use the evolutionary origins of human moral judgment to challenge its reliability or truth-tracking capacity. **Street's Darwinian dilemma** (2006) argues that evolutionary forces have shaped our evaluative attitudes for fitness-enhancing purposes, not truth-tracking ones. This confronts the **moral realist** with a dilemma: either there is no relation between evolutionary pressures and moral truth (in which case it would be a massive coincidence if our evolved moral judgments tracked the truth, and realism leads to skepticism), or there is a relation (in which case the realist must provide a non-question-begging account of it). **Joyce's debunking argument** (2006, 2016) is conditional: given that evolution explains our moral judgments without reference to their truth, we are not justified in those judgments unless we have independent confirmation of their truth—and we lack such confirmation. Debunking arguments do not claim that evolution shows moral judgments are false; they claim it undercuts whatever justification those judgments might otherwise have had. Realist responses include **third-factor explanations** (Enoch 2010: survival is good, so evolutionary selection for survival-tracking judgments is also selection for truth-tracking) and challenges to the empirical premise that moral judgment is an evolutionary adaptation rather than a cultural by-product.

*Key sources:* Street, S. (2006). "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value." *Philosophical Studies*, 127(1), 109–166. | Joyce, R. (2006). *The Evolution of Morality*. MIT Press. | Joyce, R. (2016). *Essays in Moral Skepticism*. Oxford University Press. | Enoch, D. (2010). "The Epistemological Challenge to Metanormative Realism." *Philosophical Studies*, 148(3), 413–438.

### Moral Disagreement as Epistemic Challenge

The argument from persistent moral disagreement to skepticism about objective moral truth. Mackie (1977) placed the argument from relativity at the center of his case for **error theory**: if there were objective moral facts, we would expect convergence in moral judgment over time as inquiry progresses, as occurs in the sciences. The persistence of deep, intractable moral disagreement—across cultures, traditions, and individual well-informed inquirers—is better explained by the hypothesis that moral judgments are expressions of culturally specific ways of life than by the hypothesis that they track objective facts that some groups systematically fail to detect. Contemporary advocates refine the argument: the relevant disagreement is not bare diversity of opinion but disagreement among epistemic peers who share evidence and methodological standards yet reach incompatible conclusions. Realist responses include: the disagreement is exaggerated (there is convergence on core values); disagreement in ethics is no worse than in philosophy generally; and moral truths may be especially difficult to access, explaining persistent divergence without undermining their objectivity.

*Key sources:* Mackie, J.L. (1977). *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong*. Penguin. | Tersman, F. (2006). *Moral Disagreement*. Cambridge University Press. | Enoch, D. (2009). "How Is Moral Disagreement a Problem for Realism?" *Journal of Ethics*, 13(1), 15–50.

## 6. Supervenience and Metaphysics

### Moral Supervenience

The principle that there can be no moral difference between two possible situations without some natural or descriptive difference: moral properties supervene on natural properties. Formally: for any moral property M and any two worlds (or situations) w₁ and w₂, if w₁ and w₂ are identical with respect to their natural properties, then they are identical with respect to M. Supervenience is typically treated as a conceptual truth that any adequate metaethical theory must accommodate. The supervenience relation has been analyzed in terms of **strong supervenience** (across all possible worlds) vs. **weak supervenience** (within a single world), drawing on the broader metaphysics literature (Kim 1984, 1993). Supervenience alone does not entail reduction: moral properties can supervene on natural properties without being identical with or reducible to them. The supervenience of the moral on the natural is widely accepted by realists and anti-realists alike; what is contested is its explanation (see **the supervenience challenge**).

*Key sources:* Kim, J. (1984). "Concepts of Supervenience." *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research*, 45(2), 153–176. | Kim, J. (1993). *Supervenience and Mind*. Cambridge University Press. | Blackburn, S. (1984). *Spreading the Word*. Oxford University Press.

### The Supervenience Challenge (Blackburn)

A challenge posed by Simon Blackburn (1971, 1984) to **moral realism**. If moral properties supervene on natural properties, the realist must explain why this is so: why cannot two descriptively identical worlds differ morally? The realist, Blackburn argues, has no satisfactory answer. If realism takes moral properties to be distinct and independent (as **non-naturalism** does), it implies that moral-natural supervenience holds as a brute necessity—but brute, unexplained necessary connections are metaphysically suspect. If realism identifies moral properties with natural properties, it secures supervenience but at the cost of abandoning the autonomy of the moral. Blackburn argues that **expressivism** has an advantage: an expressivist can explain supervenience as a constraint internal to moralizing practice (our commitment to consistency in moral judgment forbids differential moral assessment of identical natural circumstances). The supervenience challenge is thus a constraint-of-explanation argument against non-naturalist realism and in favor of expressivist or reductionist accounts.

*Key sources:* Blackburn, S. (1971). "Moral Realism." In Casey (ed.), *Morality and Moral Reasoning*. Methuen. | Blackburn, S. (1984). *Spreading the Word*. Oxford University Press. | Blackburn, S. (1993). *Essays in Quasi-Realism*. Oxford University Press.

### Hume's Law / The Is-Ought Gap

The principle, drawn from a famous passage in Hume's *Treatise* (3.1.1), that one cannot validly deduce a normative conclusion (an "ought") from purely descriptive premises (an "is"). Hume observes that authors routinely slide from "is" and "is not" to "ought" and "ought not" without explaining how this transition is licensed. The principle, sometimes called "Hume's guillotine," is standardly interpreted as a logical point: no set of purely descriptive premises entails a normative conclusion unless a normative premise is supplied. Hume's Law is distinct from Moore's **naturalistic fallacy** (which targets definitions rather than deductions) but is often invoked alongside it. Whether Hume's Law is a genuine theorem of logic, a substantive normative principle, or merely a pragmatic caution is debated. Some forms of **analytic naturalism** and **constitutivism** are explicitly designed to bridge or bypass the is-ought gap by showing that normative conclusions are entailed by certain descriptive claims about agency or conceptual role.

*Key sources:* Hume, D. (1739–40). *A Treatise of Human Nature*. | Prior, A.N. (1960). "The Autonomy of Ethics." *Australasian Journal of Philosophy*, 38(3), 199–206. | Pigden, C. (ed.) (2010). *Hume on Is and Ought*. Palgrave Macmillan.

### Thick vs. Thin Moral Concepts

A distinction introduced by Bernard Williams (1985). **Thin moral concepts** are highly general and purely evaluative: good, bad, right, wrong, ought. They appear to have no descriptive content beyond their evaluative force. **Thick moral concepts** combine evaluation with substantial descriptive content: courageous, cruel, generous, treacherous, honest, kind. To apply a thick concept requires both descriptive conditions (certain factual features must obtain) and an evaluative stance. Thick concepts have been central to debates about the fact-value distinction: some (Williams) argue that thick concepts demonstrate that fact and value are inseparably entangled in ethical thought, challenging sharp **non-cognitivist** and **error-theoretic** pictures. Others (Vayrynen 2013) defend the separability of evaluation from thick concepts, arguing that thick concepts' evaluative content can be pragmatically canceled or accounted for via conversational implicature, preserving a distinction between the descriptive and the evaluative. The thick-thin distinction also surfaces in debates about **moral perception**, **moral disagreement**, and the nature of virtue.

*Key sources:* Williams, B. (1985). *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy*. Harvard University Press. | Vayrynen, P. (2013). *The Lewd, the Rude and the Nasty*. Oxford University Press. | Roberts, D. (2011). "Shapelessness and the Thick." *Ethics*, 121(3), 489–520.

### Response-Dependence

The thesis that moral properties are response-dependent: what it is for something to be good, right, or wrong is for it to be such as to elicit certain responses (approval, disapproval, blame) from suitably specified subjects under suitably specified conditions. Response-dependence accounts occupy an intermediate position between full-blooded **moral realism** (which treats moral properties as wholly response-independent) and pure projectivism (which treats them as mere projections of sentiment). **McDowell** (1985) and **Wiggins** (1987) defend a response-dependence model on which moral properties are analogous to secondary qualities such as color: they are real properties of objects, but their nature must be understood partly in terms of their disposition to affect suitably sensitive perceivers. This allows a kind of realism about moral properties without the metaphysical commitments of **non-naturalism**, since the properties are not "queer" platonic entities but dispositions grounded in natural facts and human sensibility. Response-dependence is closely allied with **sensibility theories** and **moral perception** views and often invokes an analogy with John Locke's distinction between primary and secondary qualities. Critics question whether the secondary quality analogy can sustain the objectivity to which morality aspires.

*Key sources:* McDowell, J. (1985). "Values and Secondary Qualities." In Honderich (ed.), *Morality and Objectivity*. Routledge. | Wiggins, D. (1987). "A Sensible Subjectivism?" In *Needs, Values, Truth*. Blackwell. | Johnston, M. (1989). "Dispositional Theories of Value." *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society*, Supp. Vol. 63, 139–174.

## 7. Contemporary Debates

### Companions in Guilt Arguments

A family of arguments that defend morality against skeptical or anti-realist challenges by showing that the challenged feature is shared by another domain (typically mathematics, logic, or epistemic norms) that the skeptic is not prepared to abandon. The strategy: if you reject morality because it has feature F (e.g., it posits non-natural, causally inert properties; it involves a priori knowledge; it is subject to persistent disagreement), you must, on pain of inconsistency, also reject mathematics or epistemology—but you do not want to do that, so your argument against morality fails. Versions of the argument trace to Sidgwick, but the label is due to Lillehammer (2007). Recent advocates include Cuneo (2007: the **normative web**—epistemic norms and moral norms are so intertwined that rejecting moral facts entails rejecting epistemic facts) and Enoch (2011: indispensability arguments for moral realism parallel indispensability arguments for mathematical realism). Critics respond that companions-in-guilt arguments can be reversed: if you accept moral realism on these grounds, you may be forced into implausible realism about domains where antirealism is independently motivated.

*Key sources:* Lillehammer, H. (2007). *Companions in Guilt: Arguments for Ethical Objectivity*. Palgrave Macmillan. | Cuneo, T. (2007). *The Normative Web*. Oxford University Press. | Enoch, D. (2011). *Taking Morality Seriously*. Oxford University Press.

### Grounding in Metaethics

The application of the metaphysics of grounding (Fine 2001, 2012; Rosen 2010; Schaffer 2009) to metaethical questions. Grounding is a non-causal, constitutive determination relation: one fact grounds another when the latter holds *in virtue of* the former, or the former explains the latter in a distinctively metaphysical way. In metaethics, grounding is invoked to articulate views about the structure of moral facts. **Non-naturalist realists** may claim that moral facts are grounded in natural facts without being reducible to them, providing a model for **moral supervenience** that does not collapse into identity. Grounding-theoretic approaches also promise to clarify debates about **constitutivism**, the explanation of supervenience, and the relationship between moral and natural properties. The grounding framework introduces technical distinctions—between full and partial grounding, mediate and immediate grounding, factive and non-factive grounding—that refine traditional debates about reduction and explanation in ethics.

*Key sources:* Fine, K. (2001). "The Question of Realism." *Philosophers' Imprint*, 1(1), 1–30. | Fine, K. (2012). "Guide to Ground." In Correia and Schnieder (eds.), *Metaphysical Grounding*. Cambridge University Press. | Rosen, G. (2010). "Metaphysical Dependence: Grounding and Reduction." In Hale and Hoffmann (eds.), *Modality*. Oxford University Press. | Bader, R.M. (2017). "The Grounding Argument against Non-reductive Moral Realism." In Shafer-Landau (ed.), *Oxford Studies in Metaethics*, Vol. 12.

### Hybrid Expressivism

A family of **expressivist** theories that incorporate a descriptive or representational component into the semantics of moral sentences, combining an expressive element (a conative attitude) with a belief-like element. Hybrid expressivism is motivated by the desire to solve the **Frege-Geach problem** while retaining the expressivist advantage of explaining the intimate connection between moral judgment and motivation (**motivational internalism**). **Ridge's ecumenical expressivism** (2006, 2014) analyzes moral judgments as involving both a normative perspective (an endorsement of a standard) and a descriptive belief about whether the action meets that standard. **Toppinen's** (2013) version treats moral sentences as expressing both a desire-like state and a belief. Hybrid views differ on whether the two components are semantically encoded or pragmatically conveyed, and whether the belief component secures truth-conditions. By conceding a role for descriptive content, hybrid expressivism brings expressivism closer to **cognitivism** and raises the question of whether the view is genuinely non-cognitivist or a form of cognitivism in disguise.

*Key sources:* Ridge, M. (2006). "Ecumenical Expressivism: Finessing Frege." *Ethics*, 116(2), 302–336. | Ridge, M. (2014). *Impassioned Belief*. Oxford University Press. | Toppinen, T. (2013). "Believing in Expressivism." In Shafer-Landau (ed.), *Oxford Studies in Metaethics*, Vol. 8. | Schroeder, M. (2009). "Hybrid Expressivism: Virtues and Vices." *Ethics*, 119(2), 257–309.

### Relational Expressivism

The expressivist theory developed by Mark Schroeder (2008) in critical dialogue with traditional expressivism. Schroeder argues that the core semantic challenge for expressivism is to give a compositional account of what attitude is expressed by moral sentences in which the moral predicate appears in embedded positions. He proposes that the attitude expressed by "Murder is wrong" is not a simple disapproval of murder but a "being for" attitude—a pro-attitude—toward blaming for murder. Relational expressivism thus analyzes moral judgment as a non-cognitive attitude directed at an ordered pair: being for (blaming, murder), or more generally, being for (some response, some object). This relational structure provides the formal resources to solve the **Frege-Geach problem**, because the same "being for" attitude can be expressed across asserted and unasserted contexts, preserving compositional semantics without requiring distinct attitudes for complex constructions. Schroeder's view is sometimes classified among **hybrid expressivist** or "bifurcated attitude" approaches and is notable for its formal rigor and its commitment to a purely non-cognitive analysis without descriptive truth-conditions.

*Key sources:* Schroeder, M. (2008). *Being For: Evaluating the Semantic Program of Expressivism*. Oxford University Press. | Schroeder, M. (2010). *Noncognitivism in Ethics*. Routledge.

### Normative Error Theory

A broader error theory that extends Mackie's moral **error theory** to the entire normative domain. While Mackie (1977) targeted moral discourse specifically, normative error theorists (Streumer 2017; Olson 2014) argue that the argument from queerness applies equally to all normative claims—claims about what we have reason to do, what we prudentially ought to do, what is epistemically justified—since these all presuppose irreducibly normative properties that do not exist. Bart Streumer (2017) argues for a global normative error theory according to which there are no normative truths of any kind, and defends the striking conclusion that this very theory cannot be believed (since believing it would involve taking oneself to have reason to do so, which is a normative claim). Normative error theory raises the stakes of the metaethical debate considerably: if successful, it eliminates not only morality but all normative domains, including epistemology and practical rationality. Responses include **companions in guilt** arguments (if you reject all normativity, you undermine the very epistemic norms on which that rejection depends) and quietist responses that deny error theory's characterization of normative commitments.

*Key sources:* Streumer, B. (2017). *Unbelievable Errors: An Error Theory about All Normative Judgments*. Oxford University Press. | Olson, J. (2014). *Moral Error Theory: History, Critique, Defence*. Oxford University Press. | Street, S. (2011). "Mind-Independence without the Mystery: Why Quasi-Realists Can't Have It Both Ways." In Shafer-Landau (ed.), *Oxford Studies in Metaethics*, Vol. 6.

### Moral Particularism

The view, associated principally with Jonathan Dancy (1993, 2004), that the possibility of moral thought and judgment does not depend on the provision of a suitable supply of moral principles. The particularist denies that moral reasoning consists in subsuming particular cases under general principles (as in deductive or rule-based models); instead, moral judgment is sensitive to the holism of reasons—the way a feature that counts in favor of an action in one context may count against it in another, depending on the overall configuration of features. Particularism is a view about the structure of moral reasons and the nature of moral competence, not a first-order normative theory. It is compatible with **cognitivism** (moral judgments can be true or false even without principled support) and with **moral realism** (the world can contain moral facts that are not articulated by exceptionless principles). Critics charge that particularism cannot explain moral learning, moral disagreement, or the normative force of consistency without tacitly relying on generalizations. Particularism connects to debates about **thick vs. thin moral concepts** and to **Moral Particularism** as a challenge to principle-based normative ethics (Sidgwick, Ross, Kant).

*Key sources:* Dancy, J. (1993). *Moral Reasons*. Blackwell. | Dancy, J. (2004). *Ethics Without Principles*. Oxford University Press. | McKeever, S., and Ridge, M. (2006). *Principled Ethics: Generalism as a Regulative Ideal*. Oxford University Press. | Hooker, B., and Little, M. (eds.) (2000). *Moral Particularism*. Oxford University Press.
