# Grounding Argument

## Form

### Core Structure — Full Grounding

```
[1] The fact that P grounds the fact that Q   (P < Q)
[2] If X grounds Y, then Y ontologically depends on X
[3] If Y ontologically depends on X, then X is more fundamental than Y
∴ [4] X (the truthmaker for P) is more fundamental than Y (the truthmaker for Q)
```

### Grounding as Metaphysical Explanation

```
[1] Q obtains
[2] Q obtains because P obtains
      (The "because" here is metaphysical, not causal)
∴ [3] P grounds Q       (The fact that P explains, in a non-causal sense, the fact that Q)
```

### Grounding Chains and Well-Foundedness

```
[1] P₁ < P₂ < P₃ < … Pₙ   (P₁ grounds P₂, P₂ grounds P₃, etc.)
[2] Either: this chain terminates in an ungrounded ground (foundationalism)
[3] Or: the chain is non-terminating but each link is grounded in its predecessor (infinitism)
[4] Or: grounding forms a closed loop (coherentism — typically rejected for grounding)
```

## Distinctive Features in Philosophical Analysis

1. **Grounding vs. causation**: Grounding is synchronic and metaphysical; causation is diachronic and (typically) physical. Grounding explains what it is *in virtue of* which a fact obtains, not what *brought it about*.

2. **Grounding vs. supervenience**: Supervenience is a purely modal covariation relation (no B-difference without A-difference); grounding is a non-modal dependence relation. Supervenience is symmetric in its structural properties; grounding is asymmetric and irreflexive.

3. **Grounding vs. identity**: If P grounds Q, then P and Q are distinct — grounding is irreflexive. If every fact grounds itself, grounding collapses into triviality.

4. **Formal properties**: Standard views take grounding to be asymmetric (if P grounds Q, Q does not ground P), irreflexive (nothing grounds itself), and transitive (if P grounds Q and Q grounds R, then P grounds R). These are not universally accepted — Schaffer (2012) argues for non-transitivity in certain cases.

5. **The purity condition**: Grounding claims are typically held to be "pure" — the grounded fact and the grounding fact must involve the same individuals (or the grounding fact must not introduce new individuals). This is debated; Sider (2011) denies the purity constraint.

## Evaluation Criteria

1. **Is the "because" metaphysical or causal?** — The most common error in evaluating grounding arguments is confusing grounding with causation. Ask: does the relation hold synchronically and non-causally?

2. **Does the argument respect irreflexivity?** — If P and Q are claimed to be the same fact, the grounding relation is violated. Identity precludes grounding.

3. **Is asymmetry maintained?** — Some proposed grounding relations turn out to be symmetric on closer inspection (e.g., truthmaking relations can go both ways). Check whether the relation genuinely runs in only one direction.

4. **Does the grounding chain satisfy well-foundedness?** — If the argument appeals to an infinite regress of grounds, is this regress virtuous or vicious? Foundationalists require termination; infinitists permit non-termination but deny that this is vicious.

5. **Is the grounding relation "full" or "partial"?** — Partial grounding (P is part of what grounds Q) is weaker and may not support the conclusions the author draws.

## Famous Cases

| Argument | Proponent(s) | Grounding Claim | Target Conclusion |
|----------|-------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Truthmaking | Armstrong (2004) | Truths are grounded in being (truthmakers) | Truth supervenes on being |
| Priority Monism | Schaffer (2010) | The whole (cosmos) grounds its proper parts | The cosmos is the sole fundamental entity |
| Grounding of the Mental | Various | Mental facts are grounded in physical facts | Physicalism (non-reductive) |
| Grounding of Morality | Various | Moral facts are grounded in natural facts | Moral naturalism |
| Metaphysical Foundationalism | Various | All facts are ultimately grounded in fundamental facts | Reality has a fundamental level |

## Reconstruction Notes

- In standard reconstructions, use the symbol `<` (read: "grounds") to represent the grounding relation: `P < Q`.
- Mark grounding claims with `[G]` annotations to distinguish them from logical entailment or causal explanation.
- The distinction between **full grounding** and **partial grounding** should be annotated: `[G-full]` vs. `[G-partial]`.
- Pay special attention to whether the grounding relation is **factive** (if P grounds Q, then both P and Q obtain). Most grounding theorists accept factivity, but not all.
- When a grounding argument appears alongside a supervenience argument, distinguish them explicitly — many arguments move from the weaker supervenience claim to the stronger grounding claim without justification.
- Grounding is typically expressed with the sentential operator: `P < Q` (the fact that P grounds the fact that Q), not with a predicate of objects or events.
