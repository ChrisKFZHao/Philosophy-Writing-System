# Normative Regress Argument (Korsgaard)

## Form

### Core Structure

```
[1] To obligate, a normative principle P must itself be normatively authorized —
    it cannot simply assert its own binding force; we need a reason to obey P
[2] If the authorization of P comes from another normative principle P′, then P′
    itself requires further authorization from P′′, and so on → infinite regress
[3] An infinite normative regress is vicious — it provides no final authorization
    and leaves every normative claim ungrounded
[4] The regress can only be stopped by principles that are constitutive of agency
    or action itself — they require no external normative authorization because
    to act at all, to engage in practical deliberation, is already to be committed
    to these constitutive standards
∴ [5] The source of normativity is the constitutive standards of practical reason /
    agency (reflective endorsement within the structure of practical identity)
```

The argument is structurally a **transcendental argument** embedded in a regress structure: it asks what must be the case for normative obligation to be possible at all, argues that candidate sources of normativity either generate a vicious regress (voluntarism, realism, rational intuitionism) or are insufficient (Humean desire-based accounts), and concludes that only standards constitutive of agency can stop the regress.

### Regress Structure (Explicit)

```
[1] Why must I obey principle P?
[2] Answer 1: Because of P′ — but then why must I obey P′? → regress
[3] Answer 2: Because I desire to — but why must I obey my desires? → regress
[4] Answer 3: Because P is an objective normative fact — how does this fact
    obligate me? To recognize it as binding, I must already be committed to a
    norm of recognizing normative facts → regress
[5] Answer 4: Because P is constitutive of agency — to ask the question "why
    must I obey?" is already to engage in practical deliberation, which
    presupposes the norms governing practical reason
∴ Only answer 4 stops the regress
```

## Distinctive Features

1. **Transcendental regress structure**: Unlike standard infinite regress arguments (which show that a proposed ground is insufficient), Korsgaard's argument combines regress with a transcendental move — the very act of asking the normative question presupposes the answer.

2. **The normative question as starting point**: The argument begins from the first-personal question "Why must I be moral?" rather than from third-personal observation about morality. This gives it a distinctive practical-deliberative character absent from most metaethical arguments.

3. **Constitutivism**: The argument is the central argument for **constitutivism** — the view that normative force derives from constitutive standards of agency. Constitutivism stands as an alternative to realism, expressivism, and Humean subjectivism.

4. **Comparison with the Euthyphro dilemma**: The regress has the same logical structure as the Euthyphro dilemma: asking what makes a normative principle binding either cites another principle (regress) or cites something non-normative (category mistake).

## Evaluation Criteria / Objections

| Objection | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **The "shmagency" objection (Enoch)** | Constitutive standards of agency may be inescapable, but they are escapable as *the kind of agent we are*. An agent can ask: "Why should I be an agent?" — that is, why not be a "shmagent" (a being that superficially resembles an agent but follows different constitutive rules)? If shmagency is possible, the regress is not stopped. |
| **Substantive realism stops the regress (Parfit)** | Normative truths may be primitive — they do not need authorization because they are simply true. The question "Why must I obey the truth?" is not a further normative question but a misunderstanding. Realists can deny premise [1]: some principles are self-authorizing. |
| **Expressivism stops the regress (Gibbard)** | Normative claims express states of norm acceptance, not beliefs about authorization. The regress arises only if we assume a cognitivist semantics. If normative language is expressive, the regress never gets started. |
| **Velleman's alternative constitutivism** | Even if constitutivism is accepted, there are different ways to specify the constitutive aim of agency (autonomy, intelligibility, self-constitution). Korsgaard's Kantian specification is not forced by the regress structure alone. |
| **The scope problem** | If the argument shows that constitutive standards of agency are normative for me, it shows they are normative only insofar as I am an agent. But many moral obligations concern non-agents (animals, future generations, the severely disabled). The argument may not generate the full scope of morality. |

## Famous Cases

| Proponent | Stopping Point | Metaethical Position |
|-----------|---------------|---------------------|
| Korsgaard (1996) | Reflective endorsement / practical identity | Kantian constitutivism |
| Velleman (2000) | The constitutive aim of action (self-understanding) | Alternative constitutivism |
| Parfit (2011) | Irreducible normative truths | Non-naturalist realism |
| Gibbard (1990) | States of norm acceptance | Expressivism |
| Enoch (2006) | Robust realism — no regress because the question is mistaken | "Shmagency" critique |

## Reconstruction Notes

- The argument is best understood as an **argument by elimination**: it surveys candidate sources of normativity, eliminates those that generate a regress or are otherwise insufficient, and identifies the only candidate that survives.
- The **implicit premise** is: `[IP] Normative force cannot be brute — there must be an answer to the question "Why must I obey?" that is satisfying from the first-personal deliberative perspective.`
- Reconstruct with care: the argument is partly a **transcendental argument** (what must be the case for the normative question to be answerable?) and partly an **eliminative argument** (which candidate succeeds?).
- Note that Korsgaard's argument draws heavily on the Kantian tradition, especially Kant's argument in *Groundwork* III that the moral law is the law of a free will. The regress argument can be seen as a contemporary reconstruction of Kant's strategy.
- In reconstruction, distinguish the **regress problem** (which all theories must face) from the **constitutivist solution** (which is Korsgaard's preferred answer). The problem is widely acknowledged; the solution is contested.
