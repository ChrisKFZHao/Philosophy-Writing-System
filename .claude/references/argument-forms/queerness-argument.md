# Argument from Queerness (Mackie)

## Form

### Core Structure

```
[1] If objective moral values exist, they must be entities of a very strange sort —
    utterly different from anything else in the universe
    (metaphysical queerness: they would be intrinsically action-guiding, non-natural,
    and objectively prescriptive)
[2] If we could know objective moral values, we would need some special faculty of
    moral intuition utterly different from ordinary ways of knowing
    (epistemological queerness: a mysterious capacity to detect non-natural,
    intrinsically motivating facts)
[3] The postulation of such queer entities and faculties is unwarranted —
    it violates Ockham-like principles of ontological parsimony and methodological
    naturalism
∴ [4] Objective moral values do not exist (error theory: ordinary moral discourse is
    systematically false because it presupposes the existence of such values)
```

Mackie's argument has a **two-pronged structure**: the first prong attacks the alleged *objects* of moral knowledge (metaphysical queerness), while the second attacks the alleged *faculty* for moral knowledge (epistemological queerness). Neither prong depends on the other, but together they form a cumulative case — the realist must answer *both* challenges.

### Refined / Strengthened Form (Supervenience Variant)

```
[1] Moral properties supervene on natural properties: necessarily, any two worlds
    identical in natural properties are identical in moral properties
[2] For the non-naturalist realist, moral properties are distinct from and independent
    of natural properties
[3] But then why does this necessary covariance hold? It is metaphysically queer for
    independent non-natural properties to be modally tethered to natural ones
[4] The best explanation of supervenience is projectivism/expressivism, not realism
∴ [5] Realism cannot explain supervenience — an additional reason to reject it
```

## Distinctive Features

1. **Error theory, not non-cognitivism**: Mackie's conclusion is not that moral judgments lack truth-values (non-cognitivism), but that they are systematically false because the properties they presuppose do not exist. This distinguishes the queerness argument from Ayer's emotivism and Hare's prescriptivism.

2. **Two independent prongs**: The metaphysical and epistemological queerness arguments can function independently. Even if one prong is answered, the other may remain forceful.

3. **Metaphysical naturalism as background commitment**: The argument presupposes that we should avoid postulating entities that do not fit into a naturalistic worldview. This background naturalism is itself contestable.

4. **The "objective prescription" problem**: A central strand of metaphysical queerness is the idea that moral properties would need to be *both* objective facts about the world *and* intrinsically motivating — a combination Mackie finds incoherent.

## Evaluation Criteria / Objections

| Objection | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Companions in guilt** | Mathematical facts, modal facts, and epistemic norms are equally "queer" by Mackie's criteria — they are abstract, causally inert, and known by non-sensory means. Yet we do not reject them. If the queerness does not force us to abandon mathematics, it should not force us to abandon morality. (See `companions-in-guilt.md`.) |
| **Non-naturalist realism** | Shafer-Landau and Enoch argue that non-natural moral properties are not uniquely queer — they are just one species of irreducibly normative fact, which exist also in epistemology (reasons for belief). Moral facts are no queerer than epistemic facts. |
| **Evolutionary debunking distinction** | Some realists accept that moral facts *are* queer but argue that this is just what we should expect from a non-natural domain. Queerness, by itself, is not a refutation unless we antecedently commit to naturalism. |
| **The argument proves too much** | If the argument works against morality, it also works against normativity in general (including epistemic norms, norms of rationality), leaving us with global normative nihilism — a result even Mackie may not have intended. |
| **Selective ontological parsimony** | The principle that we should not postulate queer entities is not self-evident. Many accepted entities (numbers, possible worlds, sets) are queer by ordinary standards. The burden of proof may be symmetrical. |

## Famous Cases

| Application | Domain | Queerness Claim |
|-------------|--------|-----------------|
| Mackie (1977) | Morality | Categorical reasons are metaphysically and epistemologically queer |
| Joyce (2001) | Morality | Evolutionary debunking: moral concepts evolved for cooperation, not truth-tracking |
| Olson (2014) | Metaethics | Extended error theory: even moral error theorists may not escape queerness worries |
| Streumer (2017) | Normativity | Global error theory: *all* normative judgments are false |

## Reconstruction Notes

- The metaphysical prong is sometimes reconstructed as an argument from **causal isolation**: moral properties, being non-natural, cannot causally interact with us, so our beliefs about them cannot be explained by their truth. This yields an additional epistemological objection separate from the faculty-based one.
- Distinguish the **argument from queerness** from Mackie's **argument from relativity** (disagreement). The two are complementary: queerness targets the *content* of moral realism; relativity targets the *explanation* of moral belief. (See `disagreement-argument.md`.)
- The implicit premise is often: `[IP] We should not postulate entities that cannot be integrated into a naturalistic ontology and epistemology.`
- In reconstruction, note whether the author endorses the (stronger) metaphysical or epistemological prong — many critics respond only to one of the two.
