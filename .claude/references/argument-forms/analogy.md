# Argument from Analogy

## Form

### Basic Structure

```
[1] A possesses properties F₁, F₂, …, Fₙ and G
[2] B possesses properties F₁, F₂, …, Fₙ
∴ [3] B possesses property G
```

### Explicit Version (with Analogical Relation)

```
[1] A and B are similar in relevant respects (both possess F₁…Fₙ)
[2] A has property G
[3] F₁…Fₙ are relevant to determining whether G obtains
∴ [4] B has property G
```

## Evaluation Criteria

| Criterion | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Relevant similarities** | Whether the properties shared by A and B are relevant to G |
| **Irrelevant differences** | Whether the differences between A and B weaken the analogy (whether they undercut the transmission of G) |
| **Number of analogies** | Multiple analogies (convergent analogies) are stronger than a single one |
| **Scope of the analogy** | The breadth of case types covered by the analogy |
| **Counter-analogies** | Whether there are counterexamples that share F₁…Fₙ with A but lack G |

## Central Role in Philosophical Analysis

Arguments from analogy are extraordinarily common in analytic philosophy because:

1. **Thought experiments** are essentially a type of analogical argument (world in the thought experiment → actual world).
2. **Model-based arguments**: Constructing a minimal model to exhibit a certain structure.
3. **Intuition pumps**: Analogy is the primary operative mechanism of intuition pumps (see `phil-intuition-pump`).
4. **Counterexamples**: A counterexample is also a kind of analogy -- "If the argument holds, it should also hold for case C, but in C the conclusion is manifestly false."

## Types of Analogy

| Type | Structural Feature | Philosophical Use |
|------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Positive analogy | A and B share F₁…Fₙ | Supports B possessing G |
| Negative analogy | Differences D₁…Dₘ between A and B | Weakens the inference that B possesses G |
| Convergent analogy | A₁, A₂, A₃ all analogized to B, each pointing to G | Multiple independent lines of support |
| Reductio analogy | If B possesses G → bad consequence C | Rejects G (reductio form) |

## Reconstruction Notes

- **Most critically, clarify the "relevant properties"**: The text may list only F₁ and F₂, but the inference actually depends on F₃ (an implicit premise).
- Annotate the **irrelevant differences** between A and B that may undermine the analogy.
- Analogical arguments in philosophy are often embedded within larger argumentative structures -- they are not themselves conclusive but provide preliminary support.
- Combined analogy + reductio forms (`phil-analogy-check` handles these further).
