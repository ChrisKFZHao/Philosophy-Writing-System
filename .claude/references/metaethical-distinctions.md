# Metaethical Distinctions Catalog

> The conceptual terrain of metaethics is structured by a set of fundamental distinctions that cross-cut one another in complex ways. This catalog maps those distinctions, providing clear definitions, key philosophers, canonical arguments, and the relationships among them. Each distinction is an axis of disagreement that has shaped the landscape of contemporary metaethical inquiry.

---

## I. Cognitivism vs. Non-Cognitivism

**Definition**: The distinction concerns whether moral sentences express beliefs (truth-apt propositions) or non-cognitive attitudes (emotions, prescriptions, plans). Cognitivists hold that moral utterances are in the business of stating facts and are therefore truth-evaluable. Non-cognitivists deny this, holding that moral language primarily expresses attitudes, commands, or commitments.

**Significance**: Widely regarded as the most fundamental fault line in metaethics. Nearly every other metaethical position must first take a stand on whether moral discourse is assertoric or expressive. The debate shapes downstream commitments in moral semantics, moral psychology, and moral epistemology.

**Sub-Distinctions within Cognitivism**:

| Position | Core Claim | Key Figures |
|----------|------------|-------------|
| Naturalism | Moral facts are natural facts; moral properties are natural properties | Railton, Boyd, Sturgeon, Jackson |
| Non-Naturalism | Moral facts exist but are sui generis, not reducible to natural facts | Moore, Shafer-Landau, Parfit, Enoch |
| Error Theory | Moral sentences are truth-apt but systematically false; no moral facts exist | Mackie, Joyce, Olson |

**Sub-Distinctions within Non-Cognitivism**:

| Position | Core Claim | Key Figures |
|----------|------------|-------------|
| Emotivism | Moral utterances express emotions; "X is wrong" means "Boo X!" | Ayer, early Stevenson |
| Prescriptivism | Moral utterances are universalizable prescriptions | Hare |
| Expressivism | Moral utterances express pro-attitudes or con-attitudes | Blackburn, Gibbard |
| Quasi-Realism | Expressivism can earn the right to realist-sounding talk (truth, facts, knowledge) | Blackburn |
| Hybrid Expressivism | Moral utterances express both beliefs and desires | Ridge, Boisvert, Schroeder |

**Canonical Arguments**:
- The Frege-Geach Problem (against non-cognitivism): How can non-cognitivist accounts explain the logical validity of moral reasoning in unasserted contexts (antecedents of conditionals, disjunctions)?
- The Open Question Argument (Moore, against naturalism): For any natural property N, it remains an open question whether something with N is good; therefore "good" is not synonymous with any natural predicate.
- The Argument from Queerness (Mackie, for error theory): Moral properties would be metaphysically queer (unlike anything else in the universe) and epistemically queer (requiring a special faculty to detect).

## II. Moral Realism vs. Anti-Realism

**Definition**: This distinction admits of multiple characterizations. It is best understood as a family of positions rather than a single binary.

**Multiple Definitions**:

| Definition Type | Characterization | Key Proponents |
|-----------------|-----------------|----------------|
| Mind-Independence Realism | Moral facts obtain independently of human mental states, attitudes, or conventions | Shafer-Landau, Enoch |
| Stance-Independence Realism | Moral truths do not depend on the stances (beliefs, desires, endorsements) of any actual or idealized agent | Street, Enoch |
| Truth-Maker Realism | There exist robust truth-makers for moral propositions — entities or facts that make moral claims true | Armstrong-influenced realists |
| Minimalist/Quietist Realism | Moral claims are truth-apt and some are true, but this requires no heavy metaphysical machinery | Dworkin, Scanlon, Kramer |

**Critical Clarification**: Cognitivism does not entail realism. Error theory is cognitivist (moral sentences are truth-apt) but anti-realist (no moral sentences are true). Conversely, some forms of expressivism aspire to quasi-realist status — earning the right to realist talk without realist metaphysics.

**Realism Spectrum** (from most robust to most deflationary):

```
Robust Realism  →  Non-Naturalist Realism  →  Naturalist Realism  →  Response-Dependent Realism  →  Quietist Realism  →  Quasi-Realism  →  Error Theory  →  Non-Cognitivism
(Enoch)             (Shafer-Landau, Parfit)    (Railton, Boyd)         (Wiggins, McDowell)          (Dworkin, Scanlon)    (Blackburn)       (Mackie)        (Ayer)
```

**Canonical Arguments**:
- The Argument from Moral Disagreement (against realism): Persistent, intractable moral disagreement is best explained by the absence of objective moral facts (Mackie).
- The Companions in Guilt Argument (for realism): If we reject moral facts, we must also reject epistemic facts, which are subject to the same queerness worries; but we cannot reasonably reject epistemic facts (Cuneo, Terence).
- Evolutionary Debunking Arguments (against realism): Our moral beliefs are explained by evolutionary pressures tracking fitness, not truth; this undercuts their justification unless a link between evolutionary success and moral truth is established (Street, Joyce).

## III. Naturalism vs. Non-Naturalism

**Definition**: This distinction operates at two levels — methodological and metaphysical. Methodological naturalism holds that moral inquiry is continuous with empirical inquiry. Metaphysical naturalism holds that moral properties are natural properties. Non-naturalism denies these claims.

**Methodological vs. Metaphysical**:

| Level | Naturalist Claim | Non-Naturalist Counter |
|-------|------------------|------------------------|
| Methodological | Moral knowledge is acquired through empirical methods, broadly construed | Moral knowledge involves a priori intuition or non-empirical rational insight |
| Metaphysical | Moral properties are natural properties | Moral properties are sui generis, non-natural properties |

**Varieties of Naturalism**:

| Variety | Core Claim | Key Proponent |
|---------|------------|---------------|
| Cornell Realism (Synthetic Naturalism) | Moral properties are natural but identified a posteriori via causal regulation | Boyd, Sturgeon, Brink |
| Analytic Naturalism | Moral terms are analytically equivalent to natural terms via conceptual analysis | Jackson, Pettit |
| Canberra Plan Naturalism | Moral properties are defined by their role in folk theory; natural properties are the realizers of that role | Jackson |
| Reductive Naturalism | Moral properties are identical to natural properties | Railton |
| Non-Reductive Naturalism | Moral properties are constituted by but not identical to natural properties | Boyd, Sturgeon |

**Varieties of Non-Naturalism**:

| Variety | Core Claim | Key Proponent |
|---------|------------|---------------|
| Moorean Non-Naturalism | Goodness is a simple, indefinable, non-natural property known by intuition | Moore |
| Rationalist Non-Naturalism | Non-natural moral truths are accessed through rational intuition; moral properties are sui generis | Shafer-Landau, Parfit, Enoch |
| Quietist Non-Naturalism | Moral truths exist but are not "metaphysically heavyweight"; anti-metaphysical reading of non-naturalism | Dworkin, Scanlon |

**Canonical Arguments**:
- The Open Question Argument (Moore): For any proposed naturalistic definition of "good," it remains an open question whether the thing satisfying that definition is good; therefore the definition fails.
- The Causal Criterion Argument: Natural properties have causal powers; if moral properties have causal powers, there is a presumption in favor of naturalism (Railton, Boyd).
- Parfit's Triviality Objection: If moral properties are natural properties, then substantive moral disagreements become merely verbal — settling the natural facts settles the moral facts, which seems to miss the point of moral inquiry (Parfit, *On What Matters*).

## IV. Reductive vs. Non-Reductive Naturalism

**Definition**: For those who accept that moral properties are natural properties, a further question arises: Are moral properties identical to some natural property (reductive), or are they constituted by natural properties without being strictly identical to them (non-reductive)?

**Reductive Naturalism**:

- **Core Claim**: Moral properties are strictly identical to natural properties — e.g., "rightness is identical to the property of maximizing expected utility."
- **Key Figure**: Peter Railton. In Railton's account, moral rightness is identified with what would be approved by an idealized agent from a social point of view, and this is a natural property.
- **Mechanism**: Synthetic identity claims, established a posteriori, analogous to the identity of water with H2O.
- **Advantage**: Parsimony — no need for emergent or sui generis properties.

**Non-Reductive Naturalism**:

- **Core Claim**: Moral properties are constituted by or supervene on natural properties, but are not strictly identical to them. The relationship is one of constitution rather than identity.
- **Key Figures**: Richard Boyd, Nicholas Sturgeon (Cornell Realism). Moral properties are "homeostatic property clusters" — they are natural clusters co-instantiated by underlying natural properties, sustained by causal mechanisms.
- **Mechanism**: Constitution or realization, not identity. Multiple realizability of moral properties by different natural bases is accommodated.
- **Advantage**: Preserves the intuition that moral properties are "something over and above" their natural bases, while remaining within a naturalistic framework.

**Key Structural Differences**:

| Dimension | Reductive Naturalism | Non-Reductive Naturalism |
|-----------|---------------------|--------------------------|
| Relation | Strict identity | Constitution/supervenience |
| Modal status | Necessarily, wherever N, M | Necessarily, wherever N base, M is constituted |
| Multiple realizability | Problematic — requires disjunctive identities | Accommodated — same moral property, different bases |
| Explanatory direction | N explains M-content | M provides explanatory autonomy |

**Canonical Arguments**:
- The Multiple Realizability Argument (for non-reductive): The same moral property can be grounded in different natural properties; strict identity fails because the moral property is multiply realizable.
- The Explanatory Role Argument (for non-reductive): Moral properties do genuine explanatory work in the social and behavioral sciences (Sturgeon); this explanatory autonomy is lost under strict reduction.

## V. Internalism vs. Externalism (Motivational)

**Definition**: This distinction concerns the relationship between moral judgment and motivation. Motivational internalism holds that there is a necessary or internal connection between sincerely making a moral judgment and being motivated to act accordingly. Motivational externalism holds that any such connection is contingent, mediated by an independent desire.

**Varieties of Internalism**:

| Strength | Claim | Key Proponent |
|----------|-------|---------------|
| Strong Internalism | Sincere moral judgment necessarily motivates the agent, regardless of background psychology | Early expressivists, Hare |
| Weak Internalism | Sincere moral judgment motivates in rational or psychologically normal agents; compulsion by irrationality may sever the link | Smith, Korsgaard |
| Conditional Internalism | If an agent judges "I ought to phi," then either they are motivated to phi or they are practically irrational | Smith |

**Externalism**:

- **Core Claim**: The connection between moral judgment and motivation is contingent. An agent may sincerely judge that X is morally required while having no motivation whatsoever to do X. Motivation requires an independent desire (e.g., the desire to do what is right).
- **Key Figures**: David Brink, Philippa Foot, Peter Railton.
- **Analogy**: Just as one can judge that a painting is beautiful without being aesthetically moved, one can judge an action right without being motivated.

**Canonical Arguments**:
- The Amoralist Argument (for externalism): The amoralist — one who makes sincere moral judgments but is entirely unmotivated by them — is conceptually possible. This suggests no necessary connection (Brink).
- Smith's Fetishism Argument (against externalism): If moral judgment only motivates via a de dicto desire to do what is right (externalism), then the good agent is motivated by a fetish — caring about "rightness" rather than about the features that make actions right. This is an unattractive picture of moral motivation (Smith, *The Moral Problem*).
- The Moral Problem (Smith): Three plausible claims — (1) moral judgments are beliefs (cognitivism), (2) beliefs alone do not motivate (Humean psychology), (3) moral judgments necessarily motivate (internalism) — seem jointly inconsistent. Resolving this trilemma shapes much of the metaethical landscape.

## VI. Internalism vs. Externalism (Reasons)

**Definition**: Not to be confused with motivational internalism, reasons internalism concerns what it is to have a normative reason. Internalism about reasons holds that all reasons must connect to an agent's existing motivational set. Externalism about reasons holds that some reasons apply to agents regardless of their actual motivations.

**Reasons Internalism**:

- **Core Claim**: A consideration counts as a reason for an agent A to phi only if it connects, via a sound deliberative route, to A's subjective motivational set S — A's actual desires, projects, commitments, and concerns.
- **Key Figure**: Bernard Williams. In "Internal and External Reasons" (1981), Williams argues that reasons must be capable of explaining action; external reasons cannot do this.
- **The Deliberative Route**: The connection from S to the reason need not be direct; it may proceed through instrumental reasoning, specification of means, resolution of conflicts, and imaginative deliberation. But the starting point must be within S.
- **Implication**: If an agent has no element in their subjective motivational set from which a consideration could be derived as a reason, that consideration is not a reason for that agent.

**Reasons Externalism**:

- **Core Claim**: Some normative reasons apply to agents irrespective of their actual desires or motivations. The fact that an action would cause suffering is a reason not to do it, regardless of whether the agent cares about suffering.
- **Key Figures**: Derek Parfit, John McDowell, Thomas Scanlon.
- **McDowell's Challenge**: Williams's internalism relies on a narrow conception of what counts as "in" the motivational set. A properly educated moral agent has desires that track moral reasons; these desires are not "external" in any troubling sense.

**Canonical Arguments**:
- Williams's Explanatory Argument (for internalism): If R is a reason for A, it must be possible for A to act for that reason. External reasons cannot explain action; therefore there are no external reasons.
- Parfit's Agony Argument (for externalism): If an agent had no desire whatsoever to avoid future agony, it would still be true that the fact of future agony gives them a reason. Reasons internalism makes normativity hostage to contingent desires in an implausible way (Parfit, *On What Matters*).

## VII. Categorical vs. Hypothetical Normativity

**Definition**: This distinction concerns whether normative requirements are binding on agents regardless of their desires, interests, or ends (categorical), or only conditionally, given the agent's contingent ends (hypothetical). It maps onto the question: Is the authority of morality escapable?

**Categorical Normativity**:

- **Core Claim**: Moral requirements are categorical imperatives — they bind agents independently of their desires. You ought not to lie full stop, not merely if you happen to desire trust or honesty.
- **Key Figure (Pro)**: Immanuel Kant. The categorical imperative is the fundamental principle of morality; it commands unconditionally.
- **Key Figure (Anti-Categorical)**: Philippa Foot. In "Morality as a System of Hypothetical Imperatives" (1972), Foot argues that moral requirements only bind those who have the relevant desires or commitments — they are inescapable only for those already "inside" morality.
- **Modern Defenders of Categorical Force**: Christine Korsgaard (constitutivism — moral requirements are constitutive of agency itself), Derek Parfit (non-naturalist realism).

**Hypothetical Normativity**:

- **Core Claim**: Normative requirements have the form "If you want X, you ought to Y." Morality, if it binds at all, binds only those who care about moral ends. There is nothing rationally compulsory about morality for the amoralist.
- **Key Figure**: Philippa Foot. Morality is a system of hypothetical imperatives in the same way that etiquette is — binding only on participants.
- **Key Implication**: The amoralist makes no rational mistake. The authority of morality is contingent on commitment.

**Canonical Arguments**:
- Foot's Challenge: Morality's binding force cannot be demonstrated from outside the moral point of view. The amoralist who simply does not care about moral considerations is not guilty of irrationality — merely of not sharing our concerns.
- The Rationalist Response: Morality is uniquely rational — to violate moral requirements is to violate requirements of practical reason itself. This requires bridging the gap between morality and rationality (Korsgaard, Smith).

## VIII. Analytic vs. Synthetic Moral Naturalism

**Definition**: This distinction applies within naturalist cognitivism and concerns the semantic mechanism by which moral terms connect to natural properties. Are moral terms analytically equivalent to natural descriptions, or do they rigidly designate natural properties via a posteriori discoveries?

**Analytic Naturalism**:

- **Core Claim**: Moral terms are synonymous with or analytically equivalent to some natural description. The connection is a priori, discoverable through conceptual analysis.
- **Key Figure**: Frank Jackson. In *From Metaphysics to Ethics* (1998), Jackson defends the Canberra Plan: moral terms are defined by their role in a network of platitudes (folk moral theory); the natural properties that realize this role can then be identified empirically. The connection between the moral concept and its role is analytic.
- **Objection**: The Open Question Argument (Moore). If the connection is analytic, why does "X maximizes utility, but is it good?" remain an intelligible question?

**Synthetic Naturalism**:

- **Core Claim**: Moral terms rigidly designate natural properties, but the connection is a posteriori — akin to the scientific identification of water with H2O or heat with mean molecular kinetic energy. We cannot discover the identity through armchair analysis.
- **Key Figures**: Richard Boyd, Nicholas Sturgeon, David Brink (Cornell Realism).
- **Mechanism**: Moral terms are introduced by way of descriptions that fix their reference ("the property that causally regulates our use of 'good'"). The nature of the referent is then discovered empirically.
- **Analogy**: Kripke-Putnam semantics for natural kind terms applied to moral language.

**Key Structural Differences**:

| Dimension | Analytic Naturalism | Synthetic Naturalism |
|-----------|--------------------|----------------------|
| Semantic relation | Synonymy / analytic equivalence | Rigid designation / reference-fixing |
| Epistemic access | A priori (conceptual analysis) | A posteriori (empirical investigation) |
| Open Question resistance | Low — the question should not remain open | Moderate — openness reflects ignorance, not non-identity |
| Model | Definitional reduction (e.g., bachelor = unmarried man) | Theoretical identification (e.g., water = H2O) |

**Canonical Arguments**:
- The Moral Twin-Earth Argument (Horgan & Timmons, against synthetic naturalism): If moral terms rigidly designate natural properties, then on Moral Twin Earth — where "good" rigidly designates a different natural property — speakers mean something different by "good." But intuitively, they share the same moral concept, and substantive disagreement persists rather than merely verbal difference.
- The Permutation Argument (for analytic naturalism): If moral terms are not analytically tied to natural descriptions, radical semantic instability threatens — any natural property could, in principle, count as rightness.

## IX. Thin vs. Thick Moral Concepts

**Definition**: Thin moral concepts are purely evaluative and lack descriptive content beyond that evaluation (e.g., "good," "bad," "right," "wrong," "ought"). Thick moral concepts combine evaluation and description in ways that resist disentanglement (e.g., "courageous," "cruel," "generous," "lewd," "kind").

**Characteristics of Thin Concepts**:

- Highly abstract and general
- Minimal or no descriptive criteria for application
- Action-guiding primarily through their evaluative force
- Central to moral theorizing (consequentialism, deontology)

**Characteristics of Thick Concepts**:

- World-guided: their application is constrained by non-evaluative facts about the world
- Action-guiding: they carry evaluative valence that provides reasons for action
- The entanglement claim: the descriptive and evaluative aspects are not separable into distinct components
- Culturally and historically variable, yet objective enough to support genuine disagreement

**Williams on the Priority of Thick Concepts**:

- Bernard Williams argues that thin concepts are derivative from thick ones. The abstract language of "right" and "good" gets its content from more concrete, thick ethical concepts embedded in social practices. Stripped of thickness, thin concepts are too abstract to guide action.
- This challenges the theoretical ambition of reducing all moral language to a few thin primitives.

**The Disentanglement Argument**:

- Non-cognitivists (Hare, Blackburn) hold that thick concepts can be factored into a descriptive component (truth-conditional) and an evaluative component (expressive/prescriptive). "Courageous" = "risks harm for a worthy end" (descriptive) + "pro!" (evaluative).
- Objection (Williams, McDowell, Dancy): The evaluative interest shapes which descriptions are salient; the entanglement is inextricable. There is no evaluatively neutral descriptive core.

**Key Positions**:

| Position on Thick Concepts | Core Claim | Key Figure |
|---------------------------|------------|------------|
| Reductive/Non-Cognitivist | Thick concepts factor into descriptive + attitudinal components | Hare, Blackburn |
| Inseparability | Description and evaluation are inextricably entangled | Williams, McDowell |
| Pragmatic View | Thick concepts are context-dependent; evaluation is pragmatic, not semantic | Vayrynen |

## X. Response-Dependent vs. Response-Independent

**Definition**: A property is response-dependent if it is best understood as a disposition to elicit certain responses in suitable subjects under suitable conditions. Moral realism that takes moral properties to be response-dependent treats moral facts as grounded in human sensibilities while preserving their objectivity through idealized conditions.

**Response-Dependence**:

- **Core Claim**: Moral properties are analogous to secondary qualities (colors). Just as "red" is a disposition to produce certain visual experiences in normal observers under normal conditions, "wrong" is a disposition to elicit disapproval in morally sensitive agents under suitable conditions.
- **Key Figures**: David Wiggins, John McDowell. The secondary-quality analogy preserves the objectivity of moral judgment — moral properties are real features of the world, not mere projections — while situating them within human sensibility.
- **Suitable Conditions**: The right response is the one that would be elicited under epistemically ideal conditions — full information, vivid imagination, freedom from bias, etc.

**Response-Independence**:

- **Core Claim**: Moral properties are not constitutively tied to human responses. They are objective features of the world that we detect, not features partially constituted by our detecting them.
- **Key Figures**: Russ Shafer-Landau, David Enoch. Robust realism treats moral facts as stance-independent.
- **The Missing-Explanation Objection**: If moral properties are response-dependent, they cannot explain why we respond as we do — the response is part of what constitutes the property, rather than a detection of an independent fact. This makes the "tracking" metaphor inapplicable (Crispin Wright).

**Key Structural Differences**:

| Dimension | Response-Dependent | Response-Independent |
|-----------|--------------------|----------------------|
| Ontology | Dispositional | Categorical |
| Relation to mind | Partially constituted by responses | Mind-independent |
| Analogy | Secondary qualities (color) | Primary qualities (shape, mass) |
| Objectivity | Preserved through idealized conditions | Inherent, not dependent on idealization |

**Canonical Arguments**:
- The Secondary-Quality Analogy Argument (Wiggins, McDowell): Moral properties are like colors — real but response-involving. This captures the phenomenology of moral experience (values seem to call for responses) while avoiding "spooky" non-natural facts.
- The Missing-Explanation Objection (against response-dependence): If "wrongness" is a disposition to elicit disapproval, we cannot explain why we disapprove by citing the wrongness — that would be circular. But we often do explain our responses by citing moral properties. Response-dependence makes such explanations vacuous.

## XI. Humean vs. Anti-Humean Moral Psychology

**Definition**: Humean moral psychology holds that belief and desire are distinct, existentially independent states: beliefs represent the world, desires motivate action, and only desire can motivate. Anti-Humeans deny this, holding that beliefs (or belief-like states) can directly motivate, or that the belief-desire distinction is not exhaustive.

**Humean Psychology**:

- **Core Claim**: The mind contains two fundamentally distinct types of state — beliefs (representational, direction-of-fit: mind-to-world) and desires (motivational, direction-of-fit: world-to-mind). Motivation always requires both — a desire provides the end; belief identifies the means. Neither alone suffices.
- **Key Figure**: David Hume (*A Treatise of Human Nature*, II.iii.3): "Reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions."
- **Modern Defenders**: Michael Smith (in the context of the Moral Problem), Neil Sinhababu.
- **Direction-of-Fit Metaphor (Anscombe)**: Beliefs aim to fit the world (if false, change the belief); desires aim to make the world fit them (if unsatisfied, change the world).

**Anti-Humean Psychology**:

- **Core Claim**: The belief-desire picture is too crude. Some mental states combine representational and motivational aspects. Moral judgments may be beliefs that necessarily motivate (besires), or desires may themselves be cognitive states sensitive to reasons.
- **Key Figures**: John McDowell (moral perception involves motivational sensitivity to reasons), Thomas Nagel (motivated desires are explained by reasons, not the other way around).
- **Key Move**: Anti-Humeans reject the idea that motivation requires a non-cognitive state. Recognition of a reason for action can itself be motivating.

**Implications for Metaethics**:

| Metaethical Position | Default Psychology | Challenge |
|---------------------|--------------------|-----------|
| Non-Cognitivism | Humean (moral judgments are desires, not beliefs) | Accommodates internalism, but faces Frege-Geach |
| Naturalist Cognitivism + Externalism | Humean (moral beliefs + independent desire) | The fetishism objection |
| Rationalist Non-Naturalism | Anti-Humean (recognition of reasons motivates) | Explains internalism without non-cognitivism |

**Canonical Arguments**:
- The Direction-of-Fit Argument (Smith): Beliefs and desires have opposite directions of fit. A state that had both directions would be incoherent. Therefore, no state can be both belief and desire; moral judgments must be one or the other.
- McDowell's Perceptual Model: Moral perception is analogous to perceptual experience — it is both cognitive (representational) and motivational (reason-giving). The forced choice between cognitivism-with-inert-beliefs and non-cognitivism is a false dilemma.

## XII. Constitutivism vs. Realism about Normativity

**Definition**: Constitutivism seeks to ground normative authority in the constitutive features of agency or personhood — what one must do to count as an agent at all. Realism about normativity holds that normative facts are stance-independent, not derived from the structure of action.

**Constitutivism**:

- **Core Claim**: Normative requirements are constitutive standards of agency. Just as the rules of chess are constitutive of playing chess (not external impositions), moral requirements are constitutive of being an agent. To violate them is to fail at agency itself.
- **Key Figures**: Christine Korsgaard (*The Sources of Normativity*, 1996, and *Self-Constitution*, 2009), David Velleman.
- **Korsgaard's Argument**: The need to act — to constitute oneself as a unified agent — requires adopting a practical identity and valuing oneself as a rational being. This generates unconditional obligations to respect rational agency in oneself and others.
- **Velleman's Variant**: The constitutive aim of action is self-understanding; norms derive from this aim.
- **The "Shmagency" Objection (Enoch)**: Why should I care about being an agent? Couldn't I be a "shmagent" — something like an agent but not bound by those constitutive norms? Constitutivism fails to answer: "Why be an agent rather than a shmagent?"

**Realism about Normativity**:

- **Core Claim**: Normative truths are stance-independent. The fact that you have a reason not to cause suffering does not depend on your endorsement, your practical identity, or the structure of agency. Normative facts are part of the furniture of the universe.
- **Key Figures**: Derek Parfit (non-naturalist normative realism), T.M. Scanlon (reasons fundamentalism), David Enoch (robust realism).
- **Parfit's Claim**: Normative truths are not ontologically weighty in a problematic sense. They are irreducibly normative truths of reason — just as there are logical truths and mathematical truths.
- **Enoch's Indispensability Argument**: Normative facts are indispensable to deliberation. Deliberation — the project of deciding what to do — cannot proceed without presupposing that some considerations are reasons. This indispensability justifies belief in normative facts.

**Key Structural Differences**:

| Dimension | Constitutivism | Realism |
|-----------|---------------|---------|
| Source of normativity | Structure of agency | Stance-independent normative facts |
| Metaphysical commitment | Minimal — normativity is explained, not posited | Non-natural or robustly natural normative facts |
| Answer to "why be moral?" | Constitutive of agency — cannot coherently opt out | External reason — binds regardless of standpoint |
| Key vulnerability | Shmagency objection | Queerness / epistemological access |

## XIII. Individualist vs. Social Accounts of Morality

**Definition**: Individualist accounts treat morality as fundamentally a matter of practical reason applied to the context of interpersonal relations. Social accounts treat the interpersonal — the second-personal, the contractual, the relational — as fundamental, such that morality is essentially constituted by the relations between persons.

**Individualist Accounts**:

- **Core Claim**: The basic unit of moral analysis is the individual agent reasoning practically. Morality emerges when practical reason considers the equal standing of other agents, but the normative source remains within individual practical rationality.
- **Key Features**: Starting point is the first-person deliberative perspective; moral requirements are what any rational agent would accept; the social is derivative.
- **Key Figures**: Derek Parfit (reasons are fundamental, impartiality is a demand of reason), aspects of Kantian rationalism.

**Social Accounts**:

- **Core Claim**: Morality is essentially interpersonal or relational. The second-person standpoint — the perspective of addressing claims to one another — is irreducible to the first-person deliberative perspective.
- **Key Figures**: Stephen Darwall (*The Second-Person Standpoint*, 2006), T.M. Scanlon (*What We Owe to Each Other*, 1998).
- **Darwall's Second-Personal Account**: Moral obligation is irreducibly second-personal. To hold someone morally responsible is to address a demand to them from a standpoint of mutual accountability. This relationship — of address and acknowledgement — is constitutive of moral obligation. It cannot be reduced to third-personal facts about what is good or first-personal facts about what is rational.
- **Scanlon's Contractualism**: Morality is about what we owe to each other. An act is wrong if it is disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject. The "reasonable rejection" test is inherently interpersonal — reasons must be justifiable to others.

**Implications**:

| Approach | Source of Moral Authority | Key Question | Paradigm |
|----------|--------------------------|--------------|----------|
| Individualist | Practical reason / rationality | "What ought I to do?" | Prudential reasoning extended interpersonally |
| Social | Mutual accountability / justifiability | "What can I justify to you?" | Contractual agreement / second-personal address |

**Canonical Arguments**:
- Darwall's Argument from Accountability: The reactive attitudes (Strawsonian resentment, indignation) presuppose the second-person standpoint. To hold someone accountable is to address them, not merely to evaluate them from a third-person perspective. A first-personal theory of reasons cannot capture this.
- The Autonomy of the Interpersonal: The moral domain "what we owe to each other" (Scanlon) is a distinct domain of normativity, not derivable from the good or the rational alone.

## XIV. Quietist vs. Metaphysically Robust Realism

**Definition**: Both positions accept that there are moral truths. The disagreement concerns whether moral truths require a metaphysical grounding in robust moral facts or properties. Quietists hold that moral truths are not made true by a special realm of moral facts; robust realists hold that they are.

**Quietism**:

- **Core Claim**: There are moral truths, but there is no interesting metaphysical question about what makes them true. Moral discourse is autonomous — its standards of correctness are internal to the practice, not answerable to external metaphysical demands.
- **Key Figures**: Ronald Dworkin (*Justice for Hedgehogs*, 2011), T.M. Scanlon (*Being Realistic about Reasons*, 2014), Matthew Kramer (*Moral Realism as a Moral Doctrine*, 2009).
- **Dworkin's Argument**: Moral realism is itself a moral position, not a metaphysical one. The claim that morality is "objective" is a substantive moral claim — that there are right answers to moral questions, and some answers are better than others. No metaphysical thesis about "moral facts" can establish or undermine this.
- **Scanlon's View**: Normative truths are in no need of metaphysical grounding. The question "What reasons do I have?" is answered by normative reasoning, not by identifying truth-makers. Normative truths are "domain-specific" — their truth is internal to the normative domain.
- **Kramer's Moral Realism**: Moral realism is itself a moral thesis — the thesis that moral requirements are universally binding and objective. It does not require positing entities.

**Robust Realism**:

- **Core Claim**: Moral truths require robust truth-makers — moral facts or properties that exist mind-independently and make moral propositions true. Quietism evades rather than answers the central metaphysical question.
- **Key Figure**: David Enoch (*Taking Morality Seriously*, 2011). Robust realism holds that there are irreducibly normative facts that are part of the fabric of reality, not derivative from our practices, stances, or internal standards.
- **Enoch's Indispensability Argument**: Deliberation is indispensable, and deliberation presupposes normative facts. This is not just a pragmatic postulate — it is an argument for the existence of robust normative truths.

**Key Structural Differences**:

| Dimension | Quietism | Robust Realism |
|-----------|----------|----------------|
| Moral truths exist? | Yes | Yes |
| Truth-makers needed? | No | Yes |
| Metaphysics of morals | Deflationary — morality answers to itself | Substantive — morality answers to normative reality |
| Continuity with other domains | Domain-specific autonomy | Unified with metaphysics and epistemology |
| Interpretation of "objectivity" | Internal to moral practice | Metaphysical, stance-independent |

**Canonical Arguments**:
- Dworkin's Independence Argument: Morality is an independent domain of thought. The "objectivity" of morality is established from within moral reasoning, not by stepping outside it to examine its metaphysics. There is no neutral, non-moral standpoint from which morality can be assessed.
- Enoch's Indispensability Argument: If quietism cannot explain why moral discourse gets things right — why it tracks truth — it has not earned the right to moral truth. Truth without truth-makers is no truth at all; indispensability in deliberation justifies positing the truth-makers.

## XV. Moral Absolutism vs. Relativism vs. Pluralism

**Definition**: This distinction concerns the scope and variability of moral truth. Absolutism holds that there are universal moral truths binding on all agents at all times. Relativism holds that moral truth is relative to a framework — cultural, individual, or otherwise. Pluralism holds that there are multiple, potentially conflicting, objective values.

**Moral Absolutism**:

- **Core Claim**: There are objective, universally binding moral principles that admit of no exceptions or contextual variation (in the strong form), or universal moral truths that may incorporate contextual factors (in the moderate form).
- **Key Figures**: Kant (strong), most moral realists (moderate — universal but not exceptionless).
- **Objection**: Moral disagreement across cultures and individuals seems to challenge universality. Absolutism must explain disagreement as error on at least one side.

**Moral Relativism**:

- **Core Claim**: The truth or justification of moral judgments is relative to a framework — a culture, an individual's commitments, a historical period, etc. There is no framework-transcendent moral truth.
- **Varieties**:

| Variety | Relativized To | Key Figure |
|---------|---------------|------------|
| Cultural Relativism | Cultural norms and practices | Benedict, early Harman |
| Agent Relativism | Individual commitments and desires | Harman (in some readings), Dreier |
| Appraiser Relativism | The moral standards of the judge | Wong (in some formulations) |
| Pluralistic Relativism | Multiple objectively valid moral systems | Wong (*Natural Moralities*) |

- **Objection**: Relativism seems unable to account for moral progress, cross-cultural moral criticism, or the phenomenology of moral judgment as objective. If morality is relative, what is the basis for criticizing another culture's practices?

**Moral Pluralism**:

- **Core Claim**: There are multiple, irreducible, objective values that may conflict. The source of moral complexity is not merely epistemic (we can't figure out the right answer) but metaphysical (there is no single right answer in some cases).
- **Key Figures**: Isaiah Berlin (value pluralism), W.D. Ross (prima facie duties), Thomas Nagel (the fragmentation of value).
- **Key Distinction from Relativism**: Pluralism holds that the multiple values are objective, not relative. The fact that values can conflict does not mean that value is "relative to a framework."
- **Objection**: If irreducible values can conflict, and there is no master principle to resolve the conflict, how do we avoid moral indeterminacy or decision paralysis?

**Key Structural Differences**:

| Dimension | Absolutism | Relativism | Pluralism |
|-----------|-----------|------------|-----------|
| Scope of moral truth | Universal, framework-transcendent | Framework-relative | Universal but internally complex |
| Source of moral disagreement | Error on at least one side | Different frameworks, no error | Genuine value conflicts |
| Moral progress possible? | Yes — discovery of true principles | Problematic — framework change, not progress | Yes — better understanding of value landscape |
| Conflict resolution | Apply correct principle | None cross-framework needed | Judgment, not algorithmic |

## Cross-Cutting Map: How These Distinctions Interrelate

The distinctions in this catalog do not operate in isolation. Key cross-cutting relationships include:

1. **Cognitivism/Non-Cognitivism and Realism/Anti-Realism**: Cognitivism is necessary but not sufficient for realism. Error theory is cognitivist and anti-realist. Quasi-realism attempts to earn realist talk within a non-cognitivist framework.

2. **Naturalism/Non-Naturalism and Reductive/Non-Reductive**: Reductive vs. non-reductive is an internal debate among naturalists. Non-naturalists reject both reductive and non-reductive naturalist reductions of moral properties.

3. **Motivational Internalism and Humean Psychology**: Motivational internalism plus Humean psychology pushes strongly toward non-cognitivism (the Moral Problem); resolving this tension drives much of contemporary metaethics.

4. **Reasons Internalism/Externalism and Categorical/Hypothetical Normativity**: Reasons internalism naturally leads to a hypothetical conception of normativity. Reasons externalism is required to ground genuinely categorical normative force.

5. **Response-Dependence and Quietism**: Both are intermediate positions between robust realism and anti-realism. Response-dependence grounds moral facts in human sensibility; quietism deflates the metaphysical question.

6. **Thick Concepts and Anti-Humean Psychology**: The inseparability of description and evaluation in thick concepts supports an anti-Humean picture where perception and motivation are not cleanly separable.

7. **Constitutivism and Internalism about Reasons**: Constitutivism offers a way to be internalist about reasons (all reasons connect to agency) while preserving categorical normative force (the constitutive standards of agency are inescapable).

## Distinction Typology for Metaethical Mapping

When analyzing a metaethical text, classify each distinction drawn according to:

| Type | Description | Example in Metaethics |
|------|-------------|----------------------|
| Foundational dichotomy | Carves the logical space into two exhaustive, exclusive positions | Cognitivism vs. Non-Cognitivism |
| Spectrum distinction | Positions lie along a continuum | Robust realism to quietism to quasi-realism |
| Internal sub-distinction | Divides one side of a broader distinction | Reductive vs. Non-Reductive Naturalism (within Naturalism) |
| Cross-cutting axis | Operates independently of other distinctions | Humean vs. Anti-Humean (cross-cuts cognitivism/non-cognitivism) |
| Methodological carve | Divides approaches rather than positions | Analytic vs. Synthetic Naturalism |
