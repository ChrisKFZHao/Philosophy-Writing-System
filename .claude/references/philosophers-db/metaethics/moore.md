# Moore, G.E. (1873--1958)

- **Tradition**: Analytic philosophy; Cambridge realism; non-naturalist moral realism; intuitionism
- **Major Works**: *Principia Ethica* (1903), "The Refutation of Idealism" (1903), "A Defence of Common Sense" (1925), *Some Main Problems of Philosophy* (1953), *Ethics* (1912)
- **Key Positions**:
  - Moral non-naturalism: goodness is a simple, non-natural, unanalyzable property that is not identical to any natural property
  - The naturalistic fallacy: it is a mistake to identify the property "good" with any natural property (e.g., pleasure, evolved fitness, what is desired); any attempt to define "good" in natural terms commits this fallacy
  - Intuitionism about moral knowledge: fundamental moral truths are self-evident and known by rational intuition, not by empirical investigation
  - Ideal consequentialism: the right action is the one that produces the greatest amount of intrinsic value, where intrinsic goods include pleasure, the appreciation of beauty, and personal affection
  - Common sense realism: our ordinary beliefs about the external world are more certain than any philosophical argument purporting to refute them
- **Key Arguments**:
  - The Open Question Argument: for any proposed naturalistic definition of "good" (as pleasure, as what we desire to desire, etc.), it remains an open question whether the thing possessing the natural property is good; this shows that goodness is not identical to any natural property
  - The argument from simplicity: "good" is a simple notion, like "yellow"; just as one cannot explain what yellow is to someone who has never seen it, one cannot define "good" -- one can only point to instances
  - The argument for ideal consequentialism: certain states of consciousness (the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects) are the greatest goods we know; right action maximizes these
- **Relevance**: `phil-moral-realism` (the paradigm non-naturalist realist view), `phil-moral-epistemology` (intuitionism and self-evident moral knowledge), `phil-moral-semantics` (the naturalistic fallacy and the open question argument as semantic constraints), `phil-conceptual-analysis` (the open question argument as a constraint on conceptual analysis), `phil-normativity` (the nature of goodness and rightness as primitive normative properties)
- **Related**: Sidgwick (Moore's ideal consequentialism develops Sidgwick's framework but replaces hedonism with a pluralistic theory of value); Mackie (error theorist who accepts Moore's anti-naturalism but concludes that such properties do not exist); Ayer (accepts Moore's critique of naturalism but draws the non-cognitivist conclusion); Shafer-Landau (contemporary non-naturalist realist in the Moorean tradition)
