# Mackie, J.L. (1917--1981)

- **Tradition**: Analytic metaethics; moral error theory; empiricism
- **Major Works**: *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977), *The Cement of the Universe* (1974, on causation), *Problems from Locke* (1976)
- **Key Positions**:
  - Moral error theory: all positive moral judgments are systematically false because they presuppose the existence of objectively prescriptive properties that do not exist
  - The argument from queerness: objective moral properties would be entities of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe (metaphysical queerness); and we would need a special faculty to detect them (epistemological queerness)
  - The argument from relativity: the deep variation in moral codes across cultures is better explained by the hypothesis that moral judgments reflect ways of life than by the hypothesis that they track objective moral facts
  - Projectivism about moral discourse: we project our subjective emotional responses onto the world and mistake them for objective properties of things
  - The is-ought gap (from Hume) is unbridgeable: no set of purely descriptive premises entails a moral conclusion without the addition of a normative premise
- **Key Arguments**:
  - The argument from queerness (detailed): if objective values existed, they would be non-natural entities with intrinsic to-be-doneness built into them; merely apprehending them would have to motivate action. Neither science nor ordinary inquiry reveals anything like this. How would we detect such entities except through a mysterious intuitive faculty?
  - The argument from relativity (detailed): moral disagreement is pervasive and persistent even among competent users of moral concepts; the best explanation is not that some parties are epistemically defective but that there is no objective moral reality for them to converge on
  - The argument from supervenience: moral properties supervene on natural properties, but this supervenience relation is itself queer if moral properties are sui generis; it is more parsimonious to deny that moral properties exist
- **Relevance**: `phil-moral-realism` (error theory as the primary anti-realist position), `phil-moral-disagreement` (the argument from relativity as a canonical argument from moral disagreement), `phil-moral-epistemology` (the epistemological horn of the queerness argument), `phil-moral-supervenience` (the supervenience-based argument for error theory), `phil-ontological-commitment` (queerness and parsimony in ontological commitment)
- **Related**: Moore (Mackie accepts Moore's claim that moral properties would have to be non-natural but concludes they do not exist); Hume (Mackie's projectivism owes much to Hume's sentimentalism; error theory radicalizes Humean skepticism); Blackburn (projectivist quasi-realist who accepts Mackie's projectivism but denies error theory); Joyce (contemporary defender of moral error theory building on Mackie's arguments)
