# SKILL: phil-parity-check --- Parity / Relevant Similarity Analysis

## 1. Overview

Parity reasoning is among the most powerful and pervasive argumentative strategies in philosophy. At its core lies a deceptively simple move: the arguer identifies an opponent's commitment to some claim about a particular case or domain, then argues that consistency demands the opponent extend that commitment to another case or domain, on the grounds that there is no *relevant* difference between the two. The surface form is "if X, then by parity of reasoning, Y." When successful, parity arguments can force dramatic expansions of philosophical positions---turning a modest claim about one phenomenon into a sweeping thesis about many. When they fail, the failure often traces to a suppressed disanalogy: a difference between the cases that, once brought to light, breaks the claimed symmetry.

The most philosophically charged variant of parity reasoning is the **companions in guilt** argument, most prominently deployed in metaethics. The strategy runs: "You reject moral facts as queer or objectionable, but you accept mathematical facts (or modal facts, or epistemic norms), which share the very features you find objectionable in moral facts. By parity of reasoning, if you reject the former you must reject the latter---or else identify a relevant difference." The argumentative burden shifts decisively to the opponent. Companion-in-guilt reasoning appears wherever a philosopher tries to show that a critique overgeneralizes: if the critique's targets are objectionable, then so are the things the critic accepts. This pattern recurs in debates about abstract objects (if you reject numbers for their causal inefficacy, must you reject propositions too?), about normativity (if you reject categorical moral reasons, must you reject categorical epistemic reasons?), about color properties (if you are an error theorist about color, must you extend the error theory to all perceptual qualities?), and about mental states (if you reject qualia, must you reject intentional states on the same grounds?). In each case, the success of the argument depends entirely on whether the alleged companions are genuinely relevantly similar in the respect that grounds the critique---and whether the opponent can identify a principled, non-ad-hoc difference that exempts the companion from the critique. The companions-in-guilt strategy thus imposes a demanding dialectical requirement on the opponent: the difference they identify must not merely distinguish the cases, but must connect to the grounds of the critique in a way that explains why the critique applies to one but not the other.

Relatedly, in applied ethics, parity arguments structure debates about moral status and moral considerability. The canonical form is the argument from marginal cases: if non-human animals lack some cognitive capacity, do humans with comparable cognitive impairments---the so-called "marginal cases" of humanity, including infants and severely cognitively disabled adults---also lack it? And if those humans nonetheless possess full moral status, while animals do not, then the capacity cited cannot be the ground of the moral-status difference; something else must do the work, or the differential treatment is unjustified. This is parity reasoning at its most politically potent: it challenges a deeply entrenched differential treatment by demanding a relevant difference, and the burden falls on the defender of the status quo to supply one. The same structure appears in debates about abortion (if a fetus at 24 weeks has property P, does a newborn have it too, and what is the ground of the moral difference between infanticide and late-term abortion?), about euthanasia (if withdrawal of life support is permissible, is active euthanasia relevantly different, or must it, by parity, also be permitted?), and about enhancement (if vaccination and education are permissible enhancements, is genetic enhancement relevantly different?). In political philosophy, parity claims underwrite arguments from consistency about distributive principles: if desert matters for income, does it also matter for healthcare, and by parity must we accept desert-based allocation in both domains? If we reject luck-egalitarianism for race and gender, must we reject it for economic class? The structure is always the same---identify a principle the opponent accepts or a differential treatment they endorse, identify a case that shares the principle's operative features or is relevantly similar to the favored case, and demand a relevant difference or an extension of the treatment.

Slippery-slope arguments often embed a chain of parity claims. Each adjacent pair of cases in a spectrum is claimed to be relevantly similar, and the transitivity of relevant similarity is then invoked to yield an unsettling conclusion. The debate over abortion and the moral status of the fetus frequently takes this form: from gametes to zygote to embryo to fetus to newborn to infant, each adjacent stage is claimed to differ in no relevant respect, so that permitting abortion at one stage compels permitting it at all stages, or forbidding it at one stage compels forbidding it at all stages. The logical structure of such arguments raises distinctive questions about whether relevant similarity is transitive, and whether the accumulation of small differences across a chain can amount to a relevant difference at the endpoints even if no single adjacent pair triggers a break.

This skill distinguishes between **formal parity** and **substantive parity**. Formal parity is the purely logical claim that two cases are governed by the same principle, given the arguer's stated premises. If the arguer asserts "all Fs are Gs," and case A is uncontroversially an F, and case B is also an F (or so the arguer claims), then by formal parity the arguer is committed to G applying to both. Formal parity can be assessed by logical reconstruction alone and does not require extra-logical judgment. Substantive parity is the worldly claim that two cases genuinely resemble each other in the respects that matter for the normative or metaphysical conclusion at issue. It requires answering the question: *is* B really an F, given what F means in this domain? Resolving substantive parity requires domain-specific judgment about what counts as a relevant similarity, what counts as a relevant difference, and what principles govern relevance in the first place. Both dimensions appear in the analysis this skill produces, but the skill's primary value lies in its treatment of substantive parity---the harder, more philosophically interesting question.

In addition to the formal-substantive distinction, parity arguments can be classified by their dialectical function. A **burden-shifting parity** argument aims not to settle an issue but to force the opponent to produce a relevant difference or concede inconsistency. A **reductio parity** argument aims to show that the opponent's commitments lead to an absurd or unacceptable conclusion when extended by parity. A **consistency-demanding parity** argument aims to show that the opponent already has the resources to accept the extended conclusion and merely needs to apply their own principles consistently. And a **skeptical parity** argument aims to show that if a certain skeptical challenge succeeds against one domain, it succeeds against all domains, so that the skeptic cannot selectively deploy it. Recognizing which dialectical function the parity argument serves is important for the analysis: a burden-shifting parity that is judged "indeterminate" by the relevance assessment may still succeed in its dialectical function of shifting the burden, while a reducio parity that is judged "indeterminate" may fail to achieve its function because the opponent can simply accept the extended conclusion without absurdity.

Furthermore, the skill distinguishes between **intra-domain parity** (where both cases belong to the same philosophical subfield and share relevance criteria), **cross-domain parity** (where the cases belong to different subfields, as in companions-in-guilt arguments that span ethics and epistemology), and **meta-parity** (where the parity claim concerns the methodology of parity reasoning itself---e.g., "if you accept parity reasoning in domain X, you must accept it in domain Y"). Cross-domain parity arguments are methodologically more demanding because they require establishing that the relevance criteria of one domain are applicable in the other. Meta-parity arguments raise distinctive questions about whether the norms governing parity reasoning are domain-general or domain-specific.

### Common Parity Argument Patterns

Several recurring structural patterns characterize parity arguments in philosophy. Recognizing these patterns aids extraction and diagnosis:

- **The Critique-Overgeneralization Pattern**: An opponent advances a critique of some target T on the basis of feature F. The arguer shows that commitment C, which the opponent accepts, also exhibits F. By parity, if F is a decisive objection to T, it is a decisive objection to C. This is the pattern of companions-in-guilt arguments and appears whenever a philosopher tries to show that a skeptical or debunking argument proves too much. The analysis must determine whether F is present in C to the same degree and in the same way as in T, and whether F is genuinely the feature doing the argumentative work in the original critique.

- **The Differential-Treatment Pattern**: The opponent treats case A and case B differently (permitting something in A that they forbid in B, or ascribing a status to A that they deny to B). The arguer demands a relevant difference to justify the differential treatment. If none can be supplied, consistency demands uniform treatment. This is the pattern of arguments from marginal cases and of legal equal-protection reasoning. The analysis focuses on whether the proffered differences connect to the grounds of the differential treatment in a principled way.

- **The Sorites-Spectrum Pattern**: A continuous spectrum of cases runs from clearly-X to clearly-not-X. The arguer claims that no adjacent pair of cases in the spectrum differs in a relevant respect, so that by repeated application of parity, the cases at the extremes must receive the same treatment. This is the pattern of slippery-slope parity arguments and of sorites arguments about vague predicates. The analysis focuses on the transitivity of relevant similarity: whether the small differences between adjacent cases accumulate to a relevant difference at the extremes.

- **The Symmetry Pattern**: Two cases are claimed to be symmetric with respect to some operation or transformation. If the operation produces no relevant change, then the cases must be treated alike. This pattern appears in arguments from symmetry in physics, from invariance under permutation in ethics, and from translation in philosophy of language. The analysis focuses on whether the symmetry is genuine (does the transformation really produce no relevant change?) and whether the symmetry is complete (are there respects in which the transformation is not symmetric, and are those respects relevant?).

- **The Reductio-by-Extension Pattern**: The arguer shows that the opponent's principle, if consistently applied, would yield an absurd or unacceptable conclusion about some further case. The parity claim is that the further case falls under the principle. This pattern differs from critique-overgeneralization in that the target is not the opponent's critique of something else but the opponent's own positive principle. The analysis focuses on whether the principle's extension to the further case is faithful to the principle's rationale, or whether the principle, properly understood, has implicit restrictions the arguer has ignored.

## 2. Input

**Required:**

- **A reconstructed argument** containing a parity claim, including the conclusion the arguer wishes to extend from one case to another. This should be supplied as a numbered-premise argument reconstruction (ideally produced by `phil-argument-reconstruct`). The reconstruction must make explicit (a) the claim the arguer affirms about case A, (b) the claim the arguer affirms or presupposes about the similarity between A and B, (c) the principle or rule that licenses extending the claim from A to B, and (d) the conclusion asserted about B. Without this structure, the parity analysis cannot proceed because the logical anatomy of the argument is underspecified.

- **The claimed parity statement**, explicitly quoted or paraphrased. This is the assertion that case A and case B are relevantly similar with respect to some feature F or principle P, such that a commitment about A commits one to the same commitment about B. The parity statement should be extracted verbatim from the source argument where possible, or reconstructed in the arguer's own terms where the source is not available. Paraphrase carries risk of distortion; flag any interpretive choices made in the paraphrase.

**Optional:**

- **Domain context**: The philosophical subfield (e.g., metaethics, applied ethics, philosophy of mind, epistemology, political philosophy, metaphysics, philosophy of language) and any relevant background commitments of the interlocutors that constrain what counts as a relevant difference. Domain context matters because relevance criteria are domain-specific. In metaphysics, ontological parsimony, causal profile, and explanatory power are central to relevance. In ethics, the capacity for welfare, autonomy, relational properties, and the distinction between persons and non-persons structure relevance. In epistemology, reliability, sensitivity, and safety may be the relevant dimensions. Supplying domain context prevents the analysis from importing alien relevance standards.

- **Prior admissions**: Any concessions or stipulations the parties have already made about relevant similarities or differences between the cases. If the opponent has already conceded that feature F is present in both cases, or that feature G is absent from both, or that feature H is irrelevant to the conclusion, these admissions constrain the analysis. They narrow the difference inventory and preempt certain lines of relevance assessment. Where admissions are ambiguous (does the opponent concede *all* cases of type T, or only *some*?), flag the ambiguity and consider both interpretations.

- **Stated principles**: Any explicit principles the arguer has endorsed that govern when differences are relevant. For example, if the arguer has stated that "only causal differences are relevant to ontological commitment," the relevance assessment uses that principle rather than importing an alternative criterion. If the arguer has stated no such principle, the analysis will need to reconstruct one from their argumentative commitments, and should note that this reconstruction is interpretive.

- **Dialectical posture**: The argumentative context in which the parity claim is advanced. Is the arguer on the offensive (using parity to challenge the opponent's position) or on the defensive (using parity to deflect a critique)? Does the arguer bear the burden of proof, or does the opponent? These postural facts affect how the analysis weighs burden-shifting considerations and how it assesses the significance of suppressed disanalogies. An offensive parity argument that shifts the burden to the opponent is evaluated differently from a defensive parity argument that merely blocks a critique.

- **Target audience**: The intended audience for the parity argument. Is it addressed to the opponent directly (second-personal parity), to a neutral third party (third-personal parity), or to the arguer themselves as a consistency check (first-personal parity)? The target audience determines which principles and background commitments are dialectically available. A parity argument addressed to an error theorist about morality can appeal to the error theorist's commitment to epistemic norms; a parity argument addressed to a general philosophical audience may need to establish that epistemic norms are genuinely similar to moral norms, not merely that the error theorist is committed to them.

- **Argumentative history**: The dialectical history of the parity dispute. Has this parity claim been advanced before in the literature? Have standard responses been developed? Is there a settled view among specialists about whether the parity claim succeeds? Supplying argumentative history prevents the analysis from treating as novel a parity claim that has been extensively debated, and allows the analysis to engage with the existing literature rather than starting from scratch. Even when argumentative history is not supplied, the analysis should note when the parity claim bears a recognizable structural resemblance to a well-known parity argument (e.g., "this parity claim structurally mirrors the companions-in-guilt argument in metaethics, and the lessons of that debate may transfer").

## 3. Output

The skill produces a **Structured Parity Analysis Report** containing the following sections:

1. **Parity Claim Restatement**: A precise restatement of the claimed parity relation. This section identifies case A (the source case, about which the arguer already has a commitment), case B (the target case, to which the arguer wishes to extend the commitment), the commitment in question (a claim, judgment, attitude, or treatment), and the transfer principle (the rule that licenses extension when no relevant difference exists). The restatement should be maximally faithful to the original argument while resolving any ambiguities in the arguer's framing.

2. **Respect of Comparison**: The dimension or property along which the cases are claimed to be relevantly similar. This may be a single property (e.g., "lacks empirical detectability," "involves suffering," "is the product of a reliable cognitive process") or a cluster (e.g., "is categorical, non-natural, and causally inert," "is sentient without being self-conscious"). The analysis specifies whether the respect is descriptive (a feature the cases share), normative (an evaluative status they share), or structural (a relation to other cases or principles they share). The analysis also assesses whether the stated respect of comparison matches the respect that is actually doing argumentative work. Often the arguer states one respect (e.g., "both involve intentionality") but the argument turns on a different, unstated respect (e.g., "both involve intentionality with the same functional profile"). This mismatch is a common source of parity-argument failure. If the respect is ambiguous or contested---if the arguer characterizes it one way and the opponent would characterize it differently---both characterizations are noted, and the dialectical significance of the disagreement is assessed. This section also identifies whether the respect is the *only* one the arguer needs (a single-respect parity claim) or one of several the arguer needs (a multiple-respect parity claim, where the conclusion requires similarity along multiple independent dimensions).

3. **Difference Inventory**: An exhaustive catalogue of every difference between case A and case B that a reasonable interlocutor could adduce, regardless of whether those differences appear prima facie relevant to the conclusion. Differences are drawn from four sources: (a) features explicitly noted or conceded in the reconstructed argument, (b) features supplied by the domain context, (c) features any competent participant in the relevant philosophical debate would recognize as distinguishing the cases, and (d) differences that are speculative or unestablished but not ruled out by anything in the input. The inventory is organized into descriptive differences (in what the cases are like), normative differences (in what evaluative status they have), and structural differences (in how they relate to other cases or principles). The inventory errs on the side of inclusiveness; differences that are ultimately judged irrelevant are still listed, with their irrelevance explained in the next section.

4. **Relevance Assessment**: The core of the analysis. Each difference from the inventory is tested for relevance to the transferred conclusion. A difference D is relevant just in case D's presence in case A but not case B (or vice versa) would, in conjunction with plausible background principles accepted by both parties (or, failing that, accepted by the opponent alone), justify affirming the conclusion for one case while denying it for the other. For each difference, the assessment assigns one of three statuses:
   - *Clearly irrelevant*: The difference has no bearing on the conclusion, whether because it concerns a feature orthogonal to the respect of comparison, or because it is excluded by an explicit principle both parties accept. Example: if the conclusion is about sentence-meaning, differences in speaker-meaning are irrelevant (absent further argument connecting the two).
   - *Potentially relevant but underdetermined*: The difference would be relevant if certain substantive philosophical theses are true, but whether those theses are true is not settled by the materials supplied. The analysis states which theses are in play and what each party would need to establish or concede for the difference to be decisive. This is the most common status in live philosophical debates.
   - *Genuinely relevant*: The difference, given accepted background principles, explains or justifies treating the cases differently with respect to the conclusion. Example: if the conclusion concerns moral status grounded in the capacity for welfare, and case A involves a being with that capacity while case B does not, the difference is relevant.

5. **Verdict**: A summary judgment in one of four categories, supported by a brief rationale referencing the most salient findings from the relevance assessment:
   - *Parity holds*: No relevant difference identified; the parity claim withstands scrutiny on the materials supplied. The arguer succeeds in extending their commitment, and the opponent must either accept the extension or revise a prior commitment.
   - *Parity fails*: At least one relevant difference identified that severs the claimed analogy. The arguer's extension is blocked, and the opponent can consistently resist it.
   - *Parity indeterminate*: The relevance of key differences cannot be resolved without additional substantive premises not supplied in the input. The analysis identifies what would need to be settled for a determinate verdict.
   - *Parity is trivial*: The claimed respect of comparison is so thin that parity is formally true but substantively vacuous. This is not a successful parity argument; it is a failure to specify a meaningful respect of comparison. The analysis explains why the respect is too thin to do argumentative work.

6. **Suppressed Disanalogies**: Differences not in the inventory because they are not known to obtain, but that *would* break parity if they did obtain. These represent argumentative vulnerabilities: the arguer relying on the parity claim has not excluded them, and an opponent can exploit them. The section distinguishes between disanalogies that are independently plausible (supported by some evidence or theoretical consideration, even if not decisive) and those that are merely logically possible (consistent with everything known, but not independently motivated). Independently plausible disanalogies are serious threats; merely possible ones are dialectically weaker but still worth cataloguing, especially in contexts where the arguer bears a burden of proof.

7. **Dialectical Consequences**: What follows for each party if the parity claim is accepted or rejected. This section has two parts:
   - *If parity holds*: Does the arguer succeed in extending their conclusion? Is the opponent forced to accept an unwelcome consequence, revise a prior commitment, or abandon a position? Does the parity argument, if successful, overgeneralize in some other direction (a "boomerang" effect)?
   - *If parity fails*: Does the arguer's broader argument collapse, or can it be repaired by qualifying the original claim or narrowing the respect of comparison? Does rejecting parity generate an inconsistency in the opponent's position (e.g., treating obviously like cases differently without a stated relevant difference), or does it merely block an attempted extension? Does the failure of this parity claim have implications for other parity claims the arguer might wish to make in the same domain?

## 4. Methodological Foundations

The analysis draws on the following canonical sources and traditions:

1. **Companions in Guilt Literature**: The strategy originates in the metaethical debate about moral realism and moral error theory. Mackie (1977, *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong*, Ch. 1) argues that moral facts would be metaphysically and epistemologically "queer"---unlike any other kind of fact we have reason to accept---and that this queerness counts decisively against moral realism. The companions-in-guilt rejoinder, developed by writers such as Terence Cuneo (2007, *The Normative Web*) and David Enoch (2011, *Taking Morality Seriously*, Ch. 3), argues that epistemic reasons share the allegedly queer features of moral reasons (categoricity, non-naturalness, intrinsic normativity), so that the error theorist who rejects moral facts on grounds of queerness must, by parity, reject epistemic facts---a cost most error theorists are unwilling to pay. The structure of this debate provides the canonical template for evaluating parity moves: identify the allegedly objectionable feature, determine whether it is genuinely present in the companion case, and assess whether it is the feature that does the dialectical work in the original critique. The companions-in-guilt literature also illustrates the importance of the *tu quoque* dimension of parity reasoning: the argument gains force not merely from establishing similarity but from showing that the opponent's own commitments preclude them from consistently denying the similarity. Important further developments include: Hallvard Lillehammer's (2007, *Companions in Guilt: Arguments for Ethical Objectivity*) systematic treatment of the argument form; Richard Joyce's (2006, *The Evolution of Morality*) response arguing that moral and epistemic justification differ because the latter but not the former can be vindicated by evolutionary debunking considerations, which amounts to a proposed relevant difference; and Ramon Das's (2016, "The Companions in Guilt Argument: A Reply to Joyce") rejoinder on behalf of the parity claim. This literature is essential reading for understanding how parity arguments operate at the highest level of philosophical sophistication, where the relevant differences are subtle, theory-laden, and hotly contested.

2. **Relevant-Difference Approaches in Applied Ethics**: The method of arguing by relevant similarity is formalized in the applied ethics literature, most influentially in Peter Singer's argument from equal consideration of interests (Singer 1975, *Animal Liberation*, Ch. 1; 1979, *Practical Ethics*, Ch. 3). Singer contends that the principle of equal consideration of interests requires that we give equal weight to like interests regardless of whose interests they are, and that no relevant difference distinguishes the interests of non-human animals from those of human beings at comparable levels of sentience. The subsequent literature on the argument from marginal cases (see, e.g., Dombrowski 1997, *Babies and Beasts: The Argument from Marginal Cases*; McMahan 2002, *The Ethics of Killing*) provides an extensive catalogue of proposed relevant differences (species membership, potentiality, relational properties, social contract status) and assesses their bearing on moral status. This literature is the primary source for the skill's relevance-assessment methodology: it demonstrates how to test candidate differences against moral principles, how to distinguish between differences that are real but irrelevant from differences that are genuinely relevant, and how to handle the dialectics of shifting burdens of proof.

3. **Slippery-Slope Argumentation Theory**: The logical structure of slippery-slope arguments involving parity chains is analyzed in the argumentation theory literature. Trudy Govier (1982, "What's Wrong with Slippery Slope Arguments?," *Canadian Journal of Philosophy*) distinguishes between conceptual slippery-slope arguments (which claim that a concept's vagueness makes line-drawing impossible, so that accepting the concept compels accepting all its borderline applications) and causal slippery-slope arguments (which claim that accepting some practice will causally lead to accepting further practices). Only the former are parity arguments proper; causal slippery-slope arguments are empirical predictions, not logical parity claims, and this skill handles only the conceptual variety. Douglas Walton (1992, *Slippery Slope Arguments*) provides a formal framework for evaluating whether small differences between adjacent cases in a spectrum accumulate to a relevant difference between the endpoints, and for assessing whether the arguer's tolerance principle (the claim that small differences do not justify different treatment) is defensible. Walton identifies several critical questions for evaluating slippery-slope parity chains: Is there a clear non-arbitrary way to draw a line? Does the arguer provide a reason why no such line can be drawn? Is the tolerance principle consistently applied, or does the arguer themselves draw lines elsewhere? Eugene Volokh (2003, "The Mechanisms of the Slippery Slope," *Harvard Law Review* 116: 1026--1134) supplies the legal-theoretic analogue, analyzing how judicial decision-making can produce parity-driven doctrinal expansion through the mechanisms of "equal treatment" and "cost-lowering" slippery slopes. Volokh's framework distinguishes between slopes where parity reasoning is the primary mechanism and slopes where it interacts with empirical and institutional factors. The skill draws on this literature to handle parity chains in Step 6 (assessing transitivity), and specifically to determine whether the chain's tolerance principle is independently defensible or is merely an artifact of the arguer's framing.

4. **Conceptual Analysis of "Relevant Similarity"**: The notion of a "relevant difference" itself admits of philosophical analysis, and the depth of the problem should not be underestimated. Nelson Goodman's work on the "new riddle of induction" (Goodman 1955, *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast*, Ch. 3) demonstrates that what counts as a relevant similarity depends on which predicates we "project"---which properties we treat as inductively projectible. "Grue" (green if observed before time t, blue otherwise) is extensionally equivalent to "green" for all past observations, but we do not project "grue." The lesson for parity reasoning is that relevance is not a purely formal notion; it depends on background theory, explanatory interests, and domain-specific norms about which properties are projectible. Amie Thomasson's work on metaphysical methodology (Thomasson 2015, *Ontology Made Easy*) extends this insight to debates about existence questions: which similarities are "relevant" to whether something exists depends on which existence questions are well-formed, which in turn depends on which terms we have introduced and how they function. The skill's relevance assessment is sensitive to this Goodmanian point: it does not assume that relevance is a pre-theoretical given, but rather identifies the theoretical commitments that underwrite relevance judgments.

5. **The Particularism/Generalism Debate**: The status of parity reasoning in ethics is itself contested terrain. Moral particularists such as Jonathan Dancy (2004, *Ethics Without Principles*) deny that the mere absence of a salient difference between two cases compels the same normative verdict about both. Their argument rests on holism about reasons: a feature that functions as a reason in one context may be inert or even a counter-reason in another context, because reasons interact in ways that resist codification into principles. If holism is true, then the absence of a discrete relevant difference does not guarantee parity, because the *combination* of features in each case may differ in ways that defeat the transfer of the normative verdict, even though no single feature can be identified as the relevant difference. Dancy's particularism thus provides a principled basis for resisting parity claims that is not available to atomists about reasons, and the skill must recognize this as a live philosophical option that may block parity even when the difference inventory yields no individually relevant differences. The generalist response (see McKeever and Ridge 2006, *Principled Ethics*; Väyrynen 2006, "Moral Generalism: Enjoy in Moderation") holds that reasons are at least sometimes default reasons whose valence is context-invariant, and that the particularist challenge overstates the prevalence of holistic reason-interaction. Sean McKeever and Michael Ridge argue that even if some reasons are context-sensitive, enough reasons are context-invariant to sustain the kind of principle-based parity reasoning that ethics requires. Pekka Väyrynen argues that moderate generalism can accommodate the particularist's insights without abandoning the project of codifying moral reasons into principles. The skill's verdict section is sensitive to this debate: in cases where the parity argument operates in a normative domain and the opponent could appeal to reason-holism, the analysis should note that the particularist has a principled basis for resisting parity even in the absence of a discrete relevant difference, and that this basis itself requires philosophical adjudication. The analysis should also assess whether the arguer's parity claim implicitly assumes atomism about reasons and whether that assumption is dialectically available given the state of the debate.

6. **Formal Models of Similarity and Analogical Reasoning**: From the formal epistemology and cognitive science literature, several approaches provide analytical tools for diagnosing parity claims. David Lewis's (1973, *Counterfactuals*) analysis of comparative similarity of possible worlds formalizes the idea that similarity is a matter of weighted dimensions, and that which similarity ordering is appropriate depends on which respects of comparison are salient for a given purpose. Amos Tversky's (1977, "Features of Similarity," *Psychological Review* 84: 327--352) contrast model treats similarity as a function of shared and distinctive features, with the weighting of features determined by context and diagnostic value. Paul Bartha's (2010, *By Parallel Reasoning*) theory of analogical reasoning provides criteria for evaluating whether a source-target mapping is plausible, including prior association (is there a known connection between the mapped features?), potential for generalization (does the mapping support a general principle?), and lack of essential disanalogy (are the differences between source and target genuinely irrelevant?). The skill draws on these formal resources to structure the difference inventory and relevance assessment, while acknowledging that the formal models are tools for analysis rather than algorithms that mechanically yield verdicts.

## 5. Steps

Perform the following steps in order. Each step builds on the preceding ones, and the output of later steps may require revisiting earlier ones if new considerations emerge.

1. **Extract the Parity Claim**: Identify the precise claim being made. Distinguish between three levels:
   - (a) The arguer's **explicit parity statement**: what the arguer says about the similarity between A and B. This may be vague ("A and B are analogous") or specific ("A and B share property P, which is the only property relevant to conclusion C"). Record the verbatim text where possible. If the arguer uses figurative language ("A is just B under another description," "A and B are two sides of the same coin"), translate into literal terms, flagging the interpretation.
   - (b) The **logically presupposed parity claim**: what the argument's structure requires even if the arguer does not state it. If the arguer moves from "X is true of A" to "X is true of B" without argument, the suppressed premise is that A and B are relevantly similar with respect to X. Make this premise explicit, even if it is implausible when stated openly. An argument that relies on a suppressed parity claim without acknowledging it is vulnerable to the charge of begging the question.
   - (c) The **rhetorically needed parity claim**: what the arguer would need to establish to persuade the intended audience, even if the arguer's stated claim is weaker. Sometimes an arguer hedges their parity claim ("A and B share property P," without claiming P is the only relevant property) but their argument only succeeds if a stronger parity claim holds ("P is the only property relevant to C, and A and B share P"). The gap between the stated claim and the needed claim is a measure of the argumentative distance the arguer must travel.
   Restate each in canonical form: "Case A and Case B are relevantly similar with respect to property P (or properties P1...Pn), such that commitment to conclusion C about A commits one to conclusion C about B." Flag any gaps between the stated, presupposed, and needed parity claims. An argument whose stated parity claim is weaker than its needed parity claim has a structural weakness, regardless of the substantive merits of the parity analysis.

2. **Identify the Respect of Comparison**: Determine the property or cluster of properties that is supposed to make the cases relevantly similar. Ask: *in virtue of what* are A and B claimed to be relevantly similar? The respect of comparison should be specified with enough precision that it is possible to determine whether a given feature of A and B is part of the respect, outside it but compatible with it, or incompatible with it.
   - Is the respect a single property or a cluster? If a cluster, are all elements of the cluster equally important, or is one element doing the primary argumentative work?
   - Is the respect descriptive (a feature the cases share), normative (an evaluative status they share), or structural (a relational property they share, such as standing in the same relation to some further principle)?
   - Is the respect intrinsic to the cases or relational? A relational respect of comparison (e.g., "both fall under principle P") may collapse formal parity into substantive parity, and the analysis should note this.
   - Are there ambiguities or equivocations in how the respect is characterized? If the arguer shifts between a thin and a thick characterization of the respect at different points in the argument, flag the equivocation.

3. **Inventory All Differences**: Produce an exhaustive catalogue of differences between the two cases. Sources of differences:
   - (a) Features explicitly noted or conceded in the reconstructed argument. These are the easiest to catalogue: if the arguer says "A and B differ in respect R, but R is irrelevant," or the opponent says "A and B differ in respect R, and R is relevant," R goes in the inventory regardless of its ultimate status.
   - (b) Features supplied by domain context and philosophical background knowledge. A competent participant in the debate knows, for example, that moral reasons are typically taken to be categorical while prudential reasons are hypothetical; that persons are typically taken to be self-conscious while sentient beings need not be; that abstract objects are typically taken to be non-spatiotemporal while concrete objects are spatiotemporal. These domain-standard distinctions belong in the inventory.
   - (c) Features any competent participant in the relevant philosophical debate would recognize as distinguishing the cases, even if those features are not explicitly mentioned in the input. This source requires the analyst to exercise philosophical judgment: what would a well-informed interlocutor in this debate notice? The analyst should err on the side of inclusion here; it is better to include a difference and explain why it is irrelevant than to omit a difference an interlocutor would reasonably raise.
   - (d) Speculative differences: features that are not established by the input but are consistent with it and, if true, would distinguish the cases. These are distinguished from suppressed disanalogies (Step 5) in that they are catalogued here as part of the difference landscape, even if their truth is unknown; suppressed disanalogies are a subset of speculative differences that would be decisive if true.
   Organize the inventory into three categories:
   - **Descriptive differences**: What the cases are like. In metaphysics: causal profile, ontological category, modal status, explanatory role, spatiotemporal location, fundamentality. In ethics: cognitive capacity, sentience, self-consciousness, relational properties (standing in caring relationships, being part of a community), capacity for autonomy, capacity for moral responsibility, having interests or a welfare. In epistemology: reliability, sensitivity, safety, accessibility, external vs. internal justification, inferential role.
   - **Normative differences**: What evaluative status the cases already have, independent of the parity argument. Do the cases already differ in the respect that the conclusion concerns (e.g., one case is already widely judged permissible while the other is not)? Are there independent normative reasons, not derived from the principle under dispute, to treat them differently? Does differential treatment of the cases have different downstream consequences for other normative commitments?
   - **Structural differences**: How the cases relate to other cases, principles, or theoretical commitments. Is one case aligned with a broader pattern of judgments while the other is an outlier? Does one case implicate further cases (via additional parity arguments) in a way the other does not? Is one case central to a theoretical framework while the other is peripheral? Structural differences can be relevant even when descriptive differences are minimal, because the theoretical cost of treating one case one way may differ from the theoretical cost of treating the other case that way.
   Err on the side of inclusiveness. Differences ultimately judged irrelevant should still be listed, with their irrelevance explained in Step 4. The inventory is not a verdict; it is a map of the argumentative terrain.

4. **Test Each Difference for Relevance**: For each difference in the inventory, determine whether it bears on the truth of the conclusion the arguer wishes to transfer. A difference D is relevant just in case its presence in one case but not the other would, in conjunction with plausible background principles, justify affirming the conclusion for one case while denying it for the other.
   The test proceeds in three sub-steps:
   - (a) **Identify the principle** that would make D relevant. If D is the presence of phenomenal consciousness in A but not B, the principle might be "phenomenal consciousness is a necessary condition for full moral status." If no such principle can be formulated, D is likely irrelevant.
   - (b) **Check the principle's status** in the dialectical context. Is the principle one both parties accept? One the arguer accepts? One the arguer is plausibly committed to? One the opponent accepts? If the principle is disputed between the parties, the relevance of D is underdetermined by the materials supplied.
   - (c) **Assess interaction effects**. A difference that is irrelevant on its own may become relevant in combination with other differences. If the cases differ along several dimensions, the question is whether the *constellation* of differences is relevant, not merely whether each difference individually is relevant. This is where the particularist challenge (see Methodological Foundations, item 5) has genuine dialectical purchase: even if no single difference is decisive, the holistic configuration of differences---the gestalt of the case---may justify differential treatment. The analysis should assess whether the arguer's parity claim is robust against this kind of holistic challenge, or whether it presupposes an atomistic picture of relevance that may be independently contestable.
   Assign each difference one of three statuses: *clearly irrelevant*, *potentially relevant but underdetermined*, or *genuinely relevant*. Where the status of several differences is interdependent (because they interact), a supplementary note should explain the interaction pattern and identify the conditions under which the constellation as a whole would be relevant or irrelevant.

5. **Check for Suppressed Disanalogies**: Identify differences that are not in the inventory because they are not known to obtain, but that *would* break parity if they did obtain. These are argumentative vulnerabilities: the arguer has not excluded them, and an opponent can exploit them.
   Distinguish two grades:
   - **Independently plausible disanalogies**: Supported by some evidence, theoretical consideration, or analogy with other cases, even if not decisively established. Example: in a parity argument about moral and epistemic reasons, the suppressed disanalogy that epistemic reasons are constitutive of belief while moral reasons are not. This is a live philosophical thesis with defenders, so it is an independently plausible disanalogy.
   - **Merely possible disanalogies**: Consistent with everything known, but not independently motivated. Example: in a parity argument about abortion and infanticide, the suppressed disanalogy that newborns possess an immaterial soul that fetuses lack. This is logically possible but not independently motivated in a secular philosophical context.
   The grade matters for burden of proof. If the arguer bears the burden (e.g., because they are making a revisionary claim), merely possible disanalogies may not suffice to resist parity. If the opponent bears the burden (e.g., because they are defending a status quo differential treatment against a parity challenge), even merely possible disanalogies may have force.

6. **Assess Transitivity of Parity**: When the argument involves a chain of parity claims (as in slippery-slope or sorites arguments), test whether relevant similarity is transitive across the chain.
   - **Identify the tolerance principle**: The claim that small differences between adjacent cases do not justify different treatment. What is the threshold for "small"? Is the threshold principled or arbitrary?
   - **Test for accumulation**: Even if each adjacent pair is relevantly similar (because the differences between them are small enough to fall under the tolerance principle), do these small differences accumulate across the chain to produce a relevant difference between the endpoints? If so, the tolerance principle is not transitive, and the parity chain fails despite each link being individually sound.
   - **Test for higher-order similarity**: Even if relevant similarity is not strictly transitive, are there higher-order similarities between the endpoints that ground parity directly, without needing to pass through the intermediate cases? If the arguer can establish direct parity between the endpoints, the intermediate chain is logically unnecessary (though it may retain rhetorical force).
   - **Check for vague boundaries**: If the chain exploits the vagueness of a concept (as in sorites arguments), note that parity arguments about vague concepts are subject to the general problems of vagueness. The skill does not resolve the sorites paradox but flags when a parity argument presupposes a particular resolution.

   Additional considerations for transitivity assessment:

   - **Check for scope of the tolerance principle**: Does the tolerance principle apply uniformly across the entire spectrum, or does its plausibility vary across the range? A tolerance principle that is plausible for one region of the spectrum (e.g., between clearly permissible and borderline cases) may be implausible for another region (e.g., between borderline and clearly impermissible cases). The analysis should test the principle regionally as well as globally.

   - **Distinguish strong from weak tolerance**: Strong tolerance holds that no small difference can ever justify different treatment. Weak tolerance holds that small differences typically do not justify different treatment, but acknowledges that the accumulation of small differences can matter. Most parity-chain arguments require strong tolerance, but arguer's may slide between strong and weak formulations. The analysis should identify which version the argument requires and whether the arguer has established it.

   - **Identify the stopping-point problem**: If the arguer resists the parity chain by insisting that some adjacent pair *does* differ in a relevant respect, they face the stopping-point problem: why that pair and not the pair before or after it? The analysis should assess whether the arguer's proffered stopping point is principled or arbitrary. A principled stopping point can break the chain; an arbitrary one merely relocates the problem.

7. **Evaluate Dialectical Consequences**: Articulate what follows for each party under the competing assumptions that the parity claim holds or fails.
   - **If parity holds**:
     - Does the arguer succeed in extending their conclusion? What exactly must the opponent now accept that they previously resisted?
     - Is the opponent forced to accept an unwelcome consequence (a *reductio* of their position) or merely to extend a principle they already accept?
     - Does the successful parity argument generate further, unintended parity claims that would be problematic for the arguer? A parity argument can "boomerang": if the arguer establishes that their opponent's critique of X applies equally to Y, the opponent may respond that the critique of X was mistaken, and that Y is not in fact objectionable.
     - Is the opponent's position still tenable, or does the parity claim force its abandonment?
   - **If parity fails**:
     - Does the arguer's broader argument collapse, or can it be repaired by narrowing the respect of comparison, qualifying the conclusion, or restricting the domain of the parity claim?
     - Does the failure of this parity claim have broader methodological implications? If a parity claim fails because of a particularist-style appeal to holistic reasons, does this undermine parity reasoning generally in this domain?
     - Does the opponent's resistance to parity commit them to any general principle about when differences are relevant that they might later regret? For example, if the opponent resists parity by appealing to a feature that is not present in some other case they want to treat differently, have they opened themselves to a further parity challenge?
     - Is the debate advanced or merely stalemated? A parity analysis that returns "indeterminate" identifies the further premises that would need to be settled for progress. An analysis that returns "holds" or "fails" identifies the dialectical situation the parties now face. In both cases, the analysis should make clear what the next productive move in the debate would be.

When completing the dialectical consequences section, bear in mind that the value of a parity analysis often lies less in its verdict than in its articulation of what is at stake. A parity dispute that appears to be about whether two cases are similar is often, at a deeper level, about whether a particular philosophical principle or framework should be accepted. The dialectical consequences section should surface this deeper level of disagreement when it is present, identifying not merely who wins the parity battle but what turns on winning it.

### Operational Guidance

When executing the seven steps above, adhere to the following operational practices:

- **Iterate, do not finalize prematurely**: The output of later steps may reveal features that were missed in earlier steps. The difference inventory (Step 3) may need to be expanded after the relevance assessment (Step 4) reveals that a candidate difference, though initially overlooked, is potentially relevant. The relevance assessment may need to be revisited after the suppressed disanalogy check (Step 5) uncovers disanalogies that, once considered, cast earlier relevance judgments in a new light. The dialectical consequences (Step 7) may reveal that the parity claim, as initially extracted in Step 1, is too weak or too strong for the argument's purposes, requiring a revision of the extraction. The steps are presented sequentially but should be executed iteratively, with cross-checking between adjacent and non-adjacent steps.

- **Document interpretive choices**: Wherever the analysis makes an interpretive choice---about how to restate the parity claim, about which features belong in the respect of comparison, about whether a difference is relevant---document the choice and the alternatives. A parity analysis that hides its interpretive choices behind a veneer of objectivity is less useful than one that makes its choices transparent, because the user can evaluate whether the choices are defensible and can substitute different choices if needed. The analysis should read as a critical engagement with the parity argument, not as an algorithmic computation.

- **Use explicit hedging for uncertain judgments**: Where the analysis is uncertain about a relevance determination, say so explicitly and explain the source and degree of uncertainty. Is the uncertainty due to missing information (the materials do not specify whether feature F is present)? Due to theoretical indeterminacy (philosophers disagree about whether F is relevant)? Due to conceptual ambiguity (it is unclear what F means in this context)? Different sources of uncertainty call for different responses, and the analysis should distinguish them.

- **Flag argumentative vulnerabilities for both parties**: A parity analysis that identifies only the arguer's vulnerabilities is incomplete. The opponent's position may also have vulnerabilities---differences they rely on that are not clearly relevant, principles they endorse that would, if consistently applied, support the parity claim, prior admissions that constrain their ability to claim a relevant difference. A balanced analysis surfaces vulnerabilities on both sides, enabling the user to see not only whether the parity claim succeeds but also what each party's argumentative alternatives are.

### Analytical Considerations

In addition to the seven steps above, the following analytical considerations should inform the parity analysis at multiple stages:

- **Same-Property vs. Same-Degree Parity**: A parity claim may assert that two cases share a property (binary parity: "both involve suffering") or that they share it to the same degree (scalar parity: "both involve the same amount of suffering"). Many parity arguments that appear compelling at the binary level are vulnerable at the scalar level. The arguer may have shown that both cases involve suffering, but not that they involve it to a comparable degree, and the degree may be what matters for the conclusion. The analysis should flag binary-to-scalar equivocations when the conclusion requires scalar parity but the arguer has only established binary parity.

- **Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Features**: The arguer may focus on intrinsic features of the cases while ignoring extrinsic or relational features that are relevant to the conclusion. Two cases can share all intrinsic features and differ in relational features (historical trajectory, institutional context, effects on third parties), and those relational features may be what matters. The difference inventory should explicitly include relational and extrinsic features, and their relevance should be tested alongside intrinsic features.

- **Context-Sensitivity of Relevance**: A difference that is irrelevant in one argumentative context may be relevant in another, because the operative principles and the stakes of the conclusion differ. The analysis must be sensitive to the specific conclusion being extended. A difference that is irrelevant to whether something *exists* may be highly relevant to whether something *matters morally*, and vice versa. The relevance assessment in Step 4 should always state the conclusion to which relevance is being tested, to prevent context-insensitive relevance judgments.

- **Defeasibility of Parity**: Parity claims are typically defeasible: the arguer claims that, *absent further considerations*, the cases should be treated alike. A parity analysis that judges "parity holds" is not claiming that no conceivable consideration could break parity; it is claiming that no consideration *available in the dialectical context* breaks it. The verdict should be qualified by noting that it is relative to the materials supplied and could be overturned by additional considerations. This qualification is especially important for parity analyses performed early in a philosophical investigation, where the full range of relevant considerations may not yet have been articulated.

- **Asymmetric Parity**: Sometimes parity is claimed in only one direction. An arguer might claim that if you accept X about A, you must accept X about B, without claiming the converse (if you reject X about B, you must reject X about A). Asymmetric parity claims typically rely on the idea that one case is a "more compelling" instance of the relevant property, so that if it fails to compel the conclusion, the other case certainly will not. The analysis should identify asymmetric parity claims and assess whether the asymmetry is justified by a principled difference between the cases or is merely rhetorical.

## 6. Quality Criteria

Apply the following criteria to evaluate the quality of the parity analysis:

1. **Completeness of Difference Inventory**: The analysis should enumerate differences that a competent philosophical interlocutor in the relevant domain would recognize as distinguishing the cases. Omissions of obvious, commonly cited differences undermine the credibility of the verdict. The inventory should be generous---include differences that the analyst suspects are irrelevant, and explain their irrelevance, rather than prejudging relevance by omitting them. An opponent reading the analysis should recognize their best objections in the inventory, even if those objections are ultimately judged to fail. A useful test: if a philosopher well-versed in the debate would, upon reading the analysis, think "they missed X," where X is a difference widely recognized in the literature, the inventory is incomplete. The inventory should also distinguish between differences the arguer acknowledges and differences the arguer does not acknowledge but has not ruled out; this distinction matters for burden-of-proof assignments.

2. **Principle-Governed Relevance Assessment**: Relevance judgments must be supported by reference to identifiable principles, not bare intuition or the analyst's personal philosophical sympathies. Each assessment of a difference as relevant or irrelevant must cite the principle that makes it so. For example: "This difference is relevant because of the principle that only causal differences bear on ontological commitment, and this difference is causal; the principle is accepted by both parties, as evidenced by the opponent's concession that causal differences distinguish concrete from abstract objects." Or: "This difference is irrelevant because, while real, it does not bear on the ground of moral status; both parties agree that the ground of moral status is sentience, and this difference concerns species membership, which is orthogonal to sentience." Statements of the form "this difference is obviously irrelevant" without citation of a principle fail this criterion. Where no established principle clearly covers the case, the analysis should construct a plausible candidate principle and note that it is not uncontroversial, flagging this as a point of potential dispute.

3. **Dialectical Fairness**: The analysis must evaluate the parity claim as it functions in its argumentative context, not as it would function in a different debate. The relevance standard should be the one the parties to the debate would recognize (or could reasonably be expected to accept), not an external standard imposed by the analyst. This means the analyst must exercise interpretive charity toward both parties: the arguer's parity claim should be reconstructed in its strongest defensible form, and the opponent's basis for resistance should be reconstructed in its strongest defensible form. The analysis should not declare parity defeated on the basis of a difference the opponent could adduce in principle but has not in fact adduced, unless that difference is independently motivated by the opponent's other commitments. Where the parties disagree about the relevance standard itself---where the arguer and opponent have different views about what makes a difference relevant---the analysis should identify this as a deeper disagreement requiring resolution before the parity claim can be adjudicated, rather than assuming one party's standard and declaring victory. Dialectical fairness also requires that the analysis not exploit ambiguities in the opponent's position that a charitable interpreter would resolve in the opponent's favor.

4. **Sensitivity to Domain Norms**: Relevance criteria differ systematically across philosophical subfields. The analysis must explicitly state the domain-specific criteria it employs and justify their applicability to the case at hand. Criteria that are appropriate for metaphysics (causal profile, ontological parsimony, explanatory power, modal robustness) may be inappropriate for ethics (capacity for welfare, autonomy, dignity, relational or contractual standing) and vice versa. In philosophy of mind, the relevant criteria may concern functional role, multiple realizability, and explanatory exclusion. In epistemology, they may concern reliability, sensitivity to truth, and the structure of justification. The analysis must not import the relevance standards of one domain into another without argument, and it must not assume that "relevance" means the same thing across domains. Where the parity claim spans domains (e.g., a companions-in-guilt argument that compares moral facts with epistemic facts), the analysis must address the cross-domain relevance problem directly: what reason is there to think that the relevance criteria of domain A apply to domain B? This is often the deepest question raised by cross-domain parity arguments, and the analysis should not sidestep it.

5. **Recognition of Burden-Shifting**: A central function of many parity arguments is not to settle an issue but to shift the argumentative burden. A parity argument that fails to establish its conclusion on the merits may nonetheless succeed in showing that the opponent's position is inconsistent unless they identify a relevant difference. The analysis must distinguish between (a) establishing parity, (b) shifting the burden to identify a relevant difference, and (c) failing even to shift the burden. A parity argument that achieves (b) without (a) has still accomplished something dialectically significant. The verdict section should reflect this nuance.

6. **Resistance to False Precision**: The analysis must not pretend to more precision than the subject matter admits. Relevance is often a matter of degree, not a binary property. The relevance assessment should use the tripartite classification (clearly irrelevant / potentially relevant / genuinely relevant) rather than forcing a binary distinction. Where the relevance of a difference is genuinely indeterminate on the available materials, the analysis must say so, and must identify what additional information or argument would resolve the indeterminacy. A parity analysis that confidently declares "parity holds" where reasonable philosophers disagree is not a good analysis; a parity analysis that explains *why* reasonable philosophers disagree, and what turns on the resolution, is.

7. **Constructive Diagnosis**: A parity analysis is not merely critical; it is diagnostic. When parity fails, the analysis should indicate what revisions would make a parity argument succeed: what respect of comparison would need to be specified, what differences would need to be shown irrelevant, what principles would need to be conceded, what additional premises would need to be established. The analysis should distinguish between failures that are repairable (the arguer can narrow the respect of comparison, qualify the conclusion, or supply a missing premise) and failures that are fatal (the parity claim is structurally defective or relies on premises incompatible with the arguer's broader position). When parity holds, the analysis should indicate the shape of the opponent's options: what they must concede, what they must reject, what prior commitments they must revise, or whether they must accept an unwelcome expansion of their position. The analysis should enable the arguer (or the opponent) to see what needs to happen next in the dialectic, not merely to register that the current move succeeded or failed. A good parity analysis is a roadmap for the next stage of the argument, not merely a verdict on the current stage.

## 7. Relationships to Other Skills

- **Depends on `phil-argument-reconstruct`**: The parity check presupposes a properly reconstructed argument. The input parity claim must be embedded in a premise-conclusion structure that makes explicit:
  - What is being asserted about case A (the source case).
  - What is being claimed or presupposed about the similarity between A and B.
  - What principle or rule licenses the extension from A to B.
  - What conclusion is asserted about B (the target case).
  A parity analysis performed on a poorly reconstructed argument risks misidentifying the parity claim, the respect of comparison, or the conclusion being extended. For best results, run `phil-argument-reconstruct` on the source text before invoking `phil-parity-check`, and pass the reconstructed argument as input.

- **Sibling to `phil-analogy-check`**: Both skills evaluate similarity-based reasoning, but they differ in scope, methodology, and normative standard.
  - `phil-analogy-check` assesses the *substantive strength* of analogical arguments: how well does the source domain map onto the target domain? Are the mapped features structurally central or merely superficial? Does the analogy support the inference to a novel conclusion, or is it merely illustrative? `phil-analogy-check` evaluates analogies as positive support for conclusions.
  - `phil-parity-check` assesses the *dialectical consistency* of treating two cases differently: given the arguer's existing commitments, can they consistently affirm X of A but deny X of B? The question is not whether A and B are similar in some absolute sense, but whether the arguer's *own principles* compel treating them alike.
  - The two skills complement each other: an argument may involve both analogical inference (A is like B, so what we know about A supports a conclusion about B) and parity reasoning (consistency demands that we treat A and B the same). Use `phil-analogy-check` for the substantive evaluation and `phil-parity-check` for the consistency evaluation. When an argument employs both strategies, run both skills and cross-reference the results.

- **Feeds into `phil-counterexample`**: A negative parity verdict frequently licenses the construction of a counterexample. If the arguer claims there is no relevant difference between A and B, but the parity analysis identifies one, then one of the cases (depending on the dialectical context) can be formulated as a counterexample to the arguer's position. The structured difference inventory produced by `phil-parity-check` can be passed directly to `phil-counterexample` for counterexample construction. Specifically, the "genuinely relevant" differences can serve as the basis for a counterexample that shows the arguer's principle fails to carve the intended distinction.

- **References `fallacy-catalog.md`**: The fallacy of **false equivalence** (treating two things as relevantly similar when they differ in respects that matter for the conclusion) is the most frequent mode of parity-argument failure. When the parity analysis returns a "parity fails" verdict because of a clearly relevant difference the arguer has overlooked or suppressed, the analysis should reference the false equivalence entry in the project's fallacy catalog. Distinguish false equivalence from the subtler failure mode where differences exist but their relevance is genuinely contested rather than clearly decisive; only the former warrants classification as a fallacy. The catalog reference provides the arguer with a label for what went wrong and a framework for avoiding the error in revised arguments.

- **Complements `phil-distinction-track`**: When a parity argument is resisted by drawing a distinction between the two cases, `phil-distinction-track` assesses whether the proffered distinction is principled or ad hoc. The relationship between the two skills is sequential and complementary: `phil-parity-check` determines whether a distinction is *needed* (i.e., whether the parity claim would otherwise succeed), and articulates the respects in which the cases must be shown to differ for the distinction to work. `phil-distinction-track` then evaluates whether candidate distinctions meet those requirements, and whether they generalize appropriately to other cases. The difference inventory from `phil-parity-check` provides the raw material for `phil-distinction-track`'s analysis.

- **Complements `phil-conceptual-analysis`**: Parity arguments often turn on whether two cases fall under the same concept. `phil-conceptual-analysis` can be used to determine whether the concept's application conditions are satisfied by both cases, or whether one case is a borderline instance. This is especially useful for parity arguments involving vague concepts (where the question is whether both cases are within the concept's extension) and for parity arguments about thick concepts (where the question is whether both cases share the evaluative and descriptive components of the concept). The conceptual analysis output can be passed to `phil-parity-check` as domain context, enriching the difference inventory with concept-specific distinctions.

- **Feeds into `phil-dialectical-map`**: The dialectical consequences section of the parity analysis report maps naturally onto the nodes and edges of a dialectical map. If parity holds, the opponent's position acquires a new commitment (the extended conclusion) that can itself become the target of further argument. If parity fails, the arguer's attempted extension is blocked, and the dialectical tree forks depending on whether the arguer revises the parity claim, narrows the principle, or abandons the extension. The structured parity analysis report can be passed to `phil-dialectical-map` to update the dialectical map with the outcome of the parity dispute.

## 8. Boundary Conditions

The skill is not designed to handle the following edge cases, which require supplementary or alternative approaches:

1. **Trivial Parity**: When the respect of comparison is so thin that nearly any pair of cases satisfies it (e.g., "both are events," "both involve causation in some sense," "both are objects of some intentional attitude"), the parity claim is formally true but substantively vacuous. A parity argument with a trivial respect of comparison does no philosophical work because it fails to connect the respect of comparison to the conclusion. The problem is not that the claims are false---they are true, and that is precisely the problem. They are true of too many things to constrain the conclusion in the way the arguer needs. The skill flags trivial parity cases and declines to proceed further with the standard analysis, because the relevance test cannot be applied without a substantive respect of comparison. The remedy is to demand that the arguer specify a thicker respect of similarity---one that is genuinely shared by the cases, not shared by a vast range of cases to which the conclusion manifestly does not apply, and demonstrably connected to the conclusion. The skill's output in such cases explains why the respect is trivial, provides examples of clearly dissimilar cases that would also satisfy the respect, and indicates what kind of specification would be needed to make the parity argument non-trivial. Trivial parity is not merely a failed parity argument; it is a failure to make a parity argument in the first place.

2. **Incommensurable Cases**: When the two cases are drawn from incommensurable domains (e.g., one from aesthetics, one from ethics; or one from abstract ontology, one from social epistemology; or one from mathematics, one from normative theory), the question of whether differences are "relevant" may lack a common currency of adjudication. The concept of a "relevant difference" presupposes a shared framework of relevance criteria---a common set of considerations that both parties recognize as bearing on the conclusion. When the cases are drawn from domains with different relevance frameworks, the parity argument may founder on framework-incommensurability rather than on any specific disanalogy. The arguer may claim that because both cases lack property P, they must be treated alike, but the opponent may respond that P's absence matters in one domain but not the other, and this response is not a refusal to identify a relevant difference but a challenge to the very idea that the two domains share relevance criteria. The skill flags incommensurability and identifies what bridging premises would be needed to make relevance assessment possible---for example, an argument that the two domains share a common normative structure (as in companions-in-guilt arguments that claim ethics and epistemology are both normative domains), or that one domain's relevance criteria are properly applicable to the other. The skill does not supply these bridging premises itself, because doing so would require substantive philosophical argument outside the scope of parity analysis. It does, however, assess whether the bridging premises the arguer does supply (if any) are adequate to the task.

3. **Pragmatic and Contextual Relevance**: Some differences are irrelevant to the *truth* of the transferred conclusion but highly relevant to the *pragmatics* of asserting it, the *practical consequences* of accepting it, or the *rhetorical force* with which it is advanced. For example, a parity argument might establish that two claims are logically on a par, but the opponent might resist on the grounds that asserting one of them in a particular context would have harmful social consequences that asserting the other would not. The skill's framework treats relevance strictly as bearing on the truth or justification of the transferred conclusion. Pragmatic and contextual considerations of this kind are noted in the analysis (typically under "Dialectical Consequences") but do not determine the verdict. When the arguer's point is primarily pragmatic rather than alethic (e.g., a *reductio ad absurdum* that is meant to be practically unsettling rather than logically refutative, or a parity argument deployed as a political intervention rather than a truth-directed philosophical argument), the skill may mischaracterize the argument by treating it as a truth-directed parity claim. Analysts should be alert to this possibility and flag it when relevant. The skill can still contribute to the analysis of pragmatic parity arguments by identifying the truth-directed substructure, but the ultimate evaluation of the argument will depend on pragmatic considerations the skill's framework does not adjudicate.

4. **Fully Specified vs. Underdescribed Cases**: When one or both cases are described only schematically---as frequently happens in philosophical thought experiments or idealized models---the difference inventory is constrained by the thinness of the description. The analysis distinguishes between two kinds of missing differences:
   - **Absent-by-design differences**: features that are not included in the case description because they are irrelevant given the purpose for which the case was constructed. For example, a thought experiment about personal identity may deliberately omit details about the subject's height, weight, and hair color because these are not plausibly relevant to identity. Filling in these details would not break parity, because the details are orthogonal to the respect of comparison. The analysis should note that these absences are benign and do not threaten the parity claim.
   - **Dialectically significant absences**: features whose absence from the case description is exploited by the arguer. The arguer may rely on the fact that the opponent cannot point to a *stated* difference to argue that no relevant difference exists, when the real issue is that the case is too thinly described to determine whether a relevant difference is present. For example, a parity argument about moral considerability may involve a case described only as "a being with cognitive capacity C," without specifying whether the being has other morally relevant features (relationships, projects, self-conception). The arguer claims that since no relevant difference is stated, none exists, but the thinness of the description prevents the opponent from identifying differences that would be apparent in a fully specified case. The analysis must call out this dialectical move when it occurs and distinguish it from genuine parity: "no stated difference" is not the same as "no relevant difference."
   The skill handles underdescribed cases by generating the suppressed-disanalogy catalogue more aggressively (including features that are not ruled out by the thin description), but it acknowledges in the verdict that underdescription makes the analysis more tentative. If the input contains only schematic case descriptions, the skill recommends fleshing them out before proceeding and, where possible, suggests how to flesh them out in a way that is neutral between the disputing parties. The analysis should also assess whether the arguer's reliance on schematic description is itself a dialectical liability---whether the argument would lose force if the cases were fully specified.

## 9. ARS Interface

This skill integrates with the Academic Research System (ARS) pipeline as follows. The integration is designed to be modular: each phase of the ARS pipeline can be invoked independently, and the skill can be used with or without ARS support depending on the user's needs and the complexity of the parity argument. For simple parity claims (e.g., a student exercise or a clearly fallacious parity argument), the ARS interface may be unnecessary; for complex, literature-embedded parity claims (e.g., a companions-in-guilt argument in a professional journal article), the full ARS pipeline is recommended.

- **Pre-analysis phase**: Before invoking this skill on a parity argument drawn from the scholarly literature, use `ars-lit-review` to survey the secondary literature on the target argument. Many canonical parity arguments in philosophy have generated extensive literatures cataloguing proposed relevant differences. The companions-in-guilt literature in metaethics alone contains dozens of proposed disanalogies between moral and epistemic reasons; the argument from marginal cases contains hundreds of proposed differences between humans and non-human animals. The difference inventory benefits substantially from consultation of the secondary literature, which prevents the analysis from "reinventing" distinctions that have already been proposed and assessed. If the `ars-lit-review` output is available, incorporate it into the difference inventory with citations. Where the literature reaches consensus on the status of certain differences, the analysis can rely on that consensus; where the literature is divided, the analysis should reflect the division rather than taking one side as settled.

- **Relevance-calibration phase**: The relevance assessment in Step 4 requires domain-specific criteria for what counts as a relevant difference. Use `ars-plan` to identify the relevant normative, metaphysical, or epistemological principles operative in the subfield, and `ars-lit-review` to survey how those principles have been applied in parity disputes within that subfield. For example, if the parity argument concerns moral status, `ars-lit-review` on the moral status literature can identify the candidate grounds of moral status (sentience, agency, relationships, species membership, etc.) and their dialectical standing.

  This calibration ensures the analysis does not import relevance criteria alien to the debate. Different subfields have different dominant relevance criteria, and a calibration that works for a parity argument in epistemology (where reliability, sensitivity, and safety are central) may fail for one in applied ethics (where capacity for welfare, autonomy, and relational properties are central). The calibration should identify not only the accepted relevance criteria but also the excluded ones---criteria that participants in the debate have explicitly rejected as irrelevant. Run the relevance criteria identified through `ars-citation-check` to verify they are accurately represented in the scholarly literature.

  Additionally, use `ars-abstract` on key primary sources to confirm that the relevance criteria attributed to specific authors are correctly understood. Attribution errors are a common source of flawed parity analyses: if the analysis attributes a relevance criterion to an author that the author does not in fact hold, the entire relevance assessment is compromised.

- **Post-analysis phase**: After producing the parity analysis report, several verification steps are recommended:

  First, run `ars-rebuttal-audit` on the analysis to search for objections to each relevance determination. The rebuttal audit searches for counterarguments in the literature and flags relevance assessments that are contradicted by published philosophical work. Adjust the analysis in light of audit findings, or flag the disagreement and note that it is contested in the literature. The rebuttal audit is especially important for parity analyses that return a determinate verdict (holds or fails), because determinate verdicts are more likely to be contested than indeterminate ones.

  Second, run `ars-citation-check` on any substantive philosophical claims made in the relevance assessment that are not obviously common ground in the discipline. Claims about what "most philosophers" hold, about the state of the literature, about the implications of particular arguments, or about what a specific author has argued should be citation-supported. Unsupported claims of this kind undermine the authority of the analysis and leave it vulnerable to rebuttal on grounds of factual error.

  Third, if the parity analysis identifies suppressed disanalogies that are serious threats to the parity claim, use `ars-lit-review` to check whether those disanalogies have been developed in the literature. An independently plausible disanalogy that has been defended in print is more dialectically weighty than one that merely "could be" defended. The analysis should note when a suppressed disanalogy has scholarly support.

  Fourth, if the parity analysis is destined for inclusion in a manuscript (e.g., as part of a reply article or a critical notice), use `ars-format-convert` to transform the structured report into prose suitable for the target venue's style guidelines.

- **Archival phase**: After the analysis is complete and verified, use `ars-cache-invalidate` to clear any cached literature-search results that informed the analysis but may have been superseded by more recent publications. Mark the primary sources consulted during the analysis using `ars-mark-read` to maintain the research pipeline's state for future literature-review tasks.

  If the analysis identifies open questions (as it frequently will, given that many canonical parity disputes remain unresolved in the literature), add them to the research plan via `ars-plan` for future investigation, and use `ars-disclosure` to note the open questions in any output destined for publication. Open questions arising from parity analysis typically take the form: "Is feature F genuinely relevant to conclusion C, and if so, what is the principle that makes it relevant?" or "Do the two domains share relevance criteria such that parity reasoning across them is legitimate?" These are research-worthy questions that can ground further inquiry.

- **Output formatting**: The structured parity analysis report produced by this skill is designed to map cleanly onto the sections of a philosophical critique: the Parity Claim Restatement corresponds to the argument summary; the Difference Inventory and Relevance Assessment correspond to the critical evaluation; the Verdict and Dialectical Consequences correspond to the concluding assessment. Use `ars-format-convert` to transform the report into narrative prose for integration into a broader research document (e.g., a literature review section that critically evaluates an argument, or a standalone reply to a parity-based objection). For publication-oriented output, consider using `ars-reviewer` to preemptively identify likely objections from referees, and `ars-revision-coach` to strengthen the analysis in response to those objections.
