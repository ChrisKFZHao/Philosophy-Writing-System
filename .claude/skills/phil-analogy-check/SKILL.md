# phil-analogy-check

## 1. Overview

The phil-analogy-check skill provides a rigorous, systematic framework for evaluating analogical arguments — one of the most pervasive, contested, and philosophically significant forms of non-deductive reasoning. Analogical arguments operate by identifying structural or relational similarities between two domains — a familiar or better-understood source domain and a less familiar or contested target domain — and inferring, on the basis of those similarities, that a property, relation, or normative status present in the source also holds in the target. Philosophers have deployed analogical arguments in nearly every subfield: the argument from design (artifacts : universe), the argument from other minds (my behavior–mind connection : others' behavior–mind connection), Parfit's teletransportation cases (personal identity across fission : personal identity across time), Thomson's violinist analogy (unplugging the violinist : terminating a pregnancy), and countless arguments in ethics, metaphysics, philosophy of mind, and philosophy of science. Despite their ubiquity, analogical arguments are frequently misanalyzed — either dismissed too quickly as "mere analogies" or accepted uncritically on the strength of a vivid mapping — and this skill exists to replace such impressionistic judgments with methodical, philosophically grounded assessment.

The core method draws on three foundational figures in the study of analogical reasoning.
Mary Hesse (1966) introduced the critical distinction between positive analogy (known shared properties), negative analogy (known differences), and neutral analogy (properties not yet known to be shared or not), and argued that analogical reasoning in science is not a heuristic crutch but a genuine engine of theory extension when the shared properties are "material" — that is, substantively connected to the projected property rather than merely formal.
Paul Bartha (2010) developed the articulation model, which imposes two necessary conditions on any good analogical argument: prior association (there must be a clear, non-trivial reason connecting the shared properties to the projected property in the source domain) and potential for generalization (there must be no defeating disanalogy that blocks the extension of that association to the target).
John Stuart Mill, in *A System of Logic* (1843), characterized analogical arguments as inferences from known resemblances to further resemblances, distinct from both induction and deduction, and identified the key weakness of analogical reasoning — namely, that its probabilistic force depends on the ratio of known shared properties to the total unknown properties of the target, a criterion that has shaped every subsequent treatment.

This skill operationalizes these theoretical resources into a concrete analytical procedure. It classifies analogies across multiple informative dimensions (illustrative vs. argumentative; a priori vs. empirical; formal vs. material; positive, negative, and neutral), maps every structural correspondence between source and target domains, evaluates whether the shared properties are relevant to the conclusion through an identifiable connecting principle, systematically detects and assesses the severity of disanalogies, constructs counter-analogies to test robustness, and assigns a calibrated strength grade reflecting the overall argumentative force of the analogical inference. The result is not a simple thumbs-up or thumbs-down but a nuanced, multidimensional assessment that clarifies exactly what the analogy does and does not achieve, equipping both the proponent and the respondent with actionable dialectical guidance.

Philosophically, the skill occupies a distinctive methodological niche. Unlike purely formal approaches that assess analogical arguments by their fit to a logical schema, and unlike purely psychological approaches that treat analogy as a cognitive heuristic studied through experiment, this skill integrates formal, epistemological, and dialectical perspectives into a unified evaluative framework. It recognizes that analogical arguments are not static logical objects but dynamic moves in argumentative exchanges, and that their force cannot be assessed without attention to the dialectical context — what is being argued for, against whom, with what shared background assumptions, and under what standards of proof. The skill thus treats the evaluation of analogical arguments as itself a philosophical practice, one that requires sensitivity to the content of the specific argument, the theoretical resources of the relevant subfield, and the canons of good reasoning that the philosophical community has developed over centuries of reflection on analogical inference.

The skill also serves a pedagogical function within the broader skills ecosystem. By making the evaluative criteria for analogical arguments explicit and operational, it enables users who are not specialists in informal logic or argumentation theory to produce analyses that meet professional standards of rigor. Conversely, by grounding its criteria in canonical philosophical sources, it ensures that the analyses it produces are defensible within the relevant scholarly community — that they do not rest on ad hoc intuitions about what makes an analogy "good" but on a well-developed theoretical framework that has been refined through decades of philosophical debate. This dual commitment — to accessibility for non-specialists and to scholarly defensibility — is a guiding design principle of the entire phil-analogy-check skill.

## 2. Input

**Required input:**

- An argument reconstruction report, typically produced by the phil-argument-reconstruct skill, that identifies and formalizes an analogical argument within a philosophical text. The reconstruction should isolate the argument's explicit premises, any implicit or suppressed premises, the conclusion being argued for, and an initial classification of the argument as analogical in logical form. Ideally — and for the analysis to be maximally informative — the reconstruction will already have extracted the following structural elements: (a) the source domain, including its key objects, properties, and relations; (b) the target domain, similarly characterized; (c) the set of properties or relations asserted to be shared between source and target; and (d) the projected property — the property or relation that the argument infers to hold in the target domain on the basis of its holding in the source domain. If the reconstruction does not explicitly identify these elements, the analyst will extract them in Step 1, but the quality of the input reconstruction directly constrains the quality of the output analysis.

**Optional inputs:**

- **The original philosophical text** from which the analogical argument is drawn. While the reconstructed argument provides the formal structure, the original text supplies contextual cues — tone, hedging language, surrounding dialectical moves — that bear on classification (e.g., whether the analogy is offered tentatively or asserted forcefully, whether it is the main argument or a supplement, whether the author anticipates and addresses certain disanalogies).

- **A conceptual analysis report** from phil-conceptual-analysis, especially when the analogical argument bears on the extension or intension of a contested concept (e.g., arguments by analogy about whether a novel entity — artificial intelligence, a fetus, a non-human animal, a corporation — falls under a given moral or legal concept). The intensional structure of the concept constrains which shared properties can count as relevant to the projected classification.

- **The fallacy-catalog entry for false analogy** (see `fallacy-catalog.md`). This entry provides the diagnostic criteria for when an analogical argument crosses from merely weak reasoning into genuinely fallacious reasoning — including the subcategories of faulty comparison, suppressed disanalogy, and the fallacy of the single similar instance. The analyst uses this as a checklist in the fallacy cross-reference section of the output.

- **A dialectical map** from phil-dialectical-map, situating the analogical argument within its broader argumentative context. Knowledge of whether the analogy is being deployed offensively (to establish a positive conclusion), defensively (to block a counterargument), or as a parity move (to demonstrate inconsistency in an opponent's position) informs the assessment of its dialectical burden and the formulation of dialectical recommendations in Step 7.

- **The argument form reference for analogy** (see `analogy.md`), which defines the canonical logical form of analogical arguments and distinguishes them from adjacent argument types such as argument from example, argument from precedent, and metaphorical argument. This reference is particularly useful when there is ambiguity about whether the text under analysis genuinely constitutes an analogical argument.

When multiple optional inputs are provided, the analyst should integrate them in a specific order of priority. The argument form reference (`analogy.md`) should be consulted first, to confirm that the reconstructed argument is indeed analogical in form. The original text should be examined second, to resolve ambiguities in the reconstruction and to gather contextual cues. The conceptual analysis (if relevant) and the fallacy-catalog entry should be consulted during the evaluative steps (Steps 3 and 4, respectively). The dialectical map should be consulted last, during Step 7, to ensure that the dialectical recommendations are contextually appropriate. If the analyst is working without some or all of the optional inputs — as will often be the case in rapid or preliminary analyses — the report should note which inputs were unavailable and how their absence may have affected the analysis (e.g., "without the original text, the classification as illustrative vs. argumentative is provisional; the reconstructed argument alone does not settle whether the author intended the analogy to carry argumentative weight").

## 3. Output

The output of phil-analogy-check is a detailed, multi-section report that traces a complete analytical arc from descriptive mapping through evaluative testing to dialectical synthesis. The report is designed to be self-standing — a reader unfamiliar with the original text should be able to understand the analogical argument, the grounds on which it has been evaluated, and the practical implications of that evaluation for further argument. The report is also designed to be modular: each section answers a specific analytical question, and sections can be read independently or in sequence depending on the reader's needs. The following subsections describe each component of the report in detail.

The skill produces a structured **Analogy Analysis Report** organized into the following sections. Each section is self-contained but builds cumulatively on the preceding sections, so that the full report traces a complete analytical arc from descriptive mapping to evaluative judgment to dialectical recommendation. The report is intended to be useful to multiple audiences: the proponent of the analogy (who can use it to identify weaknesses and strengthen the argument), the respondent (who can use it to identify the most effective lines of critique), and third-party analysts (who can use it as a model for evaluating similar analogical arguments or as a benchmark for comparative assessment across multiple analogies in a debate).

**3.1. Analogy Summary.** A concise, one-paragraph restatement of the analogical argument under analysis. This summary identifies the source domain, the target domain, the conclusion being argued for, and the key relational or property mapping that drives the inference. It also notes any explicit qualifications or scope restrictions that the argument's proponent has placed on the analogy.

**3.2. Source–Target Mapping Table.** A systematic tabulation of every correspondence between the source and target domains asserted or implied by the argument, organized into four categories: (a) object/entity mappings — the individual entities in the source and their counterparts in the target; (b) property/attribute mappings — the monadic properties said to be shared; (c) first-order relation mappings — dyadic or polyadic relations among entities that recur in both domains (e.g., causal relations, part–whole relations, functional relations); and (d) higher-order relation mappings — relations among relations that exhibit structural isomorphism (e.g., "the causal structure in S mirrors the normative structure in T"). The mapping table also identifies the projected property and notes any correspondences that the argument asserts but that the analyst judges to be contested or undersupported. Following Gentner's structure-mapping theory, relational correspondences carry more argumentative weight than mere attribute correspondences, and the mapping table explicitly flags this distinction.

**3.3. Analogy Type Classification.** A multidimensional classification of the analogy: (a) *illustrative* (the source is used merely to clarify or explain a feature of the target, with no new knowledge claimed) vs. *argumentative* (the source is used to support an inference to a novel conclusion about the target); (b) *a priori* (the inference depends only on conceptual or logical relations and can be evaluated from the armchair) vs. *empirical* (the inference depends on contingent facts about the world and requires empirical evidence to evaluate); (c) *formal* (an abstract structural isomorphism, as in mathematical analogies, where the mapping is purely syntactic) vs. *material* (substantive property overlap with a causal or nomological connection to the projected property); and (d) Hesse's tripartite taxonomy of *positive analogy* (enumerating the known shared properties), *negative analogy* (enumerating the known differences), and *neutral analogy* (identifying the properties whose status in the target is unknown and which therefore constitute the epistemic risk of the analogy).

**3.4. Relevance Assessment.** An evaluation of whether the properties and relations that the source and target share are genuinely relevant to the conclusion. This assessment is structured around Bartha's prior-association condition: is there an identifiable connection in the source domain between the shared properties and the projected property? The connection may take one of four forms: *causal* (the shared properties cause, or are part of a causal mechanism that produces, the projected property in the source); *definitional/conceptual* (the projected property is analytically or conceptually entailed by the shared properties); *functional* (the shared properties subserve the same function that the projected property realizes, such that the projection is warranted by functional isomorphism); or *statistical/correlational* (the shared properties are robustly correlated with the projected property in the source domain, warranting an inductive projection). If no connection of any of these four types can be plausibly identified — if the shared properties are merely "alongside" the projected property in the source without any documented dependence, or if the connection is itself the very thing at issue in the debate — then the analogy fails the prior-association condition, and its argumentative force is severely diminished regardless of how many shared properties are enumerated or how vividly the mapping is described. For illustrative analogies, strict prior association is not required; the question is instead whether the mapping is accurate (the source genuinely has the cited features) and illuminating (the mapping does not distort the target by importing features that are artifacts of the source).

**3.5. Disanalogy Detection and Impact Analysis.** A systematic catalogue of every relevant difference between source and target — that is, every difference that plausibly bears on the inference from shared properties to projected property. Each disanalogy is assessed for its severity and classified into one of three categories: *defeating* (the difference severs the inferential link — even if the shared properties hold in the target, the disanalogy blocks the specific causal, conceptual, or probabilistic mechanism that licenses the projection, such that the argument no longer supports the conclusion); *weakening* (the difference reduces the probability of the conclusion or narrows its scope, but does not completely sever the inferential link; the analogy retains some probative value); or *irrelevant* (the difference, however striking or rhetorically salient, does not interact with the inferential pathway and therefore does not bear on the argument's force). For each defeating or weakening disanalogy, the analysis explains exactly how the disanalogy disrupts the inferential mechanism — it is not enough to note that source and target differ in some respect; the analyst must trace the disanalogy through the causal, conceptual, or probabilistic pathway that the analogy relies upon. Where the argument's proponent has anticipated and addressed known disanalogies, the analysis notes this as a mark of argumentative sophistication and evaluates the adequacy of the response.

**3.6. Counter-Analogy Audit.** A construction and evaluation of one or more counter-analogies — alternative source domains that share the same cited properties with the target but that lack the projected property (or that possess a property inconsistent with the projected property). For each counter-analogy, the analysis assesses its plausibility (how well does the alternative source actually share the cited properties?) and its relevance (does the counter-analogy genuinely track the same inferential pathway, or does it introduce its own disanalogies that undermine its force?). The purpose of the counter-analogy audit is to test the robustness of the original analogy: a strong analogical argument is one for which plausible counter-analogies either fail to share the relevant properties or are themselves undermined by disanalogies, while a weak analogical argument is one for which a plausible, structurally parallel counter-analogy can be constructed without much contrivance.

**3.7. Argumentative Force Assessment.** A holistic, calibrated evaluation of the analogical argument's overall strength, expressed on a five-tier scale:
- **Compelling:** The analogy approaches deductive force. The shared properties, together with an established connecting principle, entail or near-entail the projected property. Defeating disanalogies are absent, and plausible counter-analogies cannot be constructed.
- **Strong:** The analogy provides substantial rational support. The prior-association condition is satisfied by a well-established causal or conceptual connection, weakening disanalogies are minor or manageable, and counter-analogies are either implausible or readily distinguished.
- **Moderate:** The analogy carries some weight but is substantially defeasible. The prior-association condition is partially satisfied (the connection is plausible but not well-established), there are weakening disanalogies that significantly reduce the force of the inference, or a plausible counter-analogy introduces reasonable doubt.
- **Weak:** The analogy provides minimal rational support; it is suggestive at most. The prior-association condition is not clearly satisfied, or there are disanalogies that come close to being defeating. The analogy may have heuristic or pedagogical value but does not, by itself, give a rational agent reason to update their credence.
- **Merely Rhetorical:** The analogy has no genuine argumentative force and functions only as a persuasive or literary device. Either the mapping is too vague to support any determinate inference, the shared properties are irrelevant to the conclusion, or a defeating disanalogy is present and unaddressed. The argument would carry no less rational weight if the analogy were simply omitted.

The strength grade is justified by explicit reference to the evidence gathered in the preceding sections, and the relative weighting of the factors (prior association, disanalogy severity, relational depth, number and variety of shared properties, vulnerability to counter-analogies) is made transparent. The analyst avoids both grade inflation — labeling weak analogies as "moderate" because the mapping is clever or vivid — and excessive skepticism — dismissing strong analogies for minor or irrelevant disanalogies.

**3.8. Fallacy Cross-Reference.** A comparison of the weaknesses detected in the analysis against the diagnostic criteria for false analogy as documented in `fallacy-catalog.md`. The cross-reference determines whether the argument crosses the threshold from merely weak analogical reasoning into genuinely fallacious reasoning. In particular, the analysis checks for: (a) the *fallacy of the single similar instance* (inferring a broad conclusion from a single, atypical source case with no demonstration that the source is representative); (b) *suppressed disanalogy* (failing to acknowledge or address a relevant and significant difference between source and target that, if acknowledged, would undermine the inference); and (c) *faulty comparison* (asserting a similarity that does not in fact obtain between source and target, or asserting a similarity in a respect that is not probative of the conclusion). The analysis also checks whether the argument commits the *fallacy of the misleading vividness* — relying on the rhetorical or emotional salience of the source domain to obscure the weakness of the inferential link.

**3.9. Dialectical Impact and Recommendations.** An assessment of how the analogy functions within its argumentative context and what practical moves are available to the proponent and respondent. For the proponent: identifying which weaknesses can be shored up — by providing evidence of prior association, by restricting the scope of the conclusion, by acknowledging and accounting for disanalogies, by reformulating an argumentative analogy as an illustrative one, or by supplementing the analogy with independent arguments that converge on the same conclusion. For the respondent: identifying the most promising lines of attack — pressing a specific defeating disanalogy, constructing a superior counter-analogy, challenging the relevance of the shared properties by questioning the connecting principle, or arguing that the analogy, even if sound in its own terms, does not establish the conclusion because the projected property is not sufficient for the ultimate claim. The analysis also notes any burden-shifting dynamics: a strong analogical argument may shift the burden of proof to the respondent to demonstrate a defeating disanalogy, while a weak one leaves the burden squarely on the proponent and can be dismissed without further argument.

## 4. Methodological Foundations

The analytical framework deployed by this skill is grounded in the following canonical sources, each of which contributes essential concepts, distinctions, and evaluative criteria.

**1. Hesse, Mary. (1966). *Models and Analogies in Science*. University of Notre Dame Press.**

Hesse's work is the indispensable starting point for any rigorous treatment of analogical reasoning.
She introduced the tripartite classification of analogies into *positive analogy* (the set of properties known to be shared between source and target), *negative analogy* (the set of properties known to differ), and *neutral analogy* (the set of properties whose status in the target is unknown — the epistemic frontier that analogical reasoning seeks to push outward). This taxonomy structures the mapping table and the type classification in the output report (Sections 3.2 and 3.3).
More importantly, Hesse argued that the argumentative force of an analogy depends not on the sheer number of shared properties but on whether the shared properties are "material" — that is, whether they belong to a cluster of properties that are nomologically or causally connected, such that the presence of some members of the cluster licenses the inference to others. This concept of material analogy is the direct precursor to Bartha's prior-association condition and informs the relevance assessment (Section 3.4).
Hesse also articulated the crucial distinction, developed throughout the first half of her book, between analogies that function as logical arguments and analogies that function as models — a distinction that maps onto the illustrative vs. argumentative classification in this skill. Her treatment of the "neutral analogy" as the locus of creative scientific inference — the unknown properties whose status the analogy is invoked to settle — provides the theoretical rationale for treating the projected property as the central object of analysis rather than treating all shared properties as equally significant.
Hesse's analysis of the role of analogical models in scientific theory change also informs the distinction, operationalized throughout this skill, between an analogy's logical structure (which can be reconstructed and evaluated) and its heuristic function (which may be valuable even when the logic is weak). Her extended case study of the billiard-ball model of gases — in which the positive, negative, and neutral analogies are systematically charted — serves as an exemplar of the kind of exhaustive mapping that this skill demands in Step 1.

**2. Bartha, Paul. (2010). *By Parallel Reasoning: The Construction and Evaluation of Analogical Arguments*. Oxford University Press.**

Bartha's articulation model is the most fully developed contemporary theory of analogical argument evaluation and serves as the primary evaluative framework for this skill. The model imposes two individually necessary and jointly sufficient conditions on a good analogical argument. The *prior-association condition* requires that there exist, in the source domain, a clear and non-trivial reason to think that the shared properties are connected to the projected property — that the project is not an arbitrary leap from one set of features to another but is anchored in an established relationship (causal, conceptual, functional, or probabilistic). The *potential-for-generalization condition* requires that there be no defeating disanalogy — no critical difference between source and target that blocks the extension of the prior association from the source to the target. This skill operationalizes both conditions: the prior-association condition drives the relevance assessment in Step 3, and the potential-for-generalization condition drives the disanalogy detection and impact analysis in Step 4. Bartha also provides a sophisticated treatment of the role of formal constraints — symmetry, one-to-one mapping, and structural consistency — in analogical reasoning, which informs the mapping procedure in Step 1. His extended case studies (including the analogical argument for the existence of other minds, Darwin's analogical reasoning in the *Origin of Species*, and the use of analogy in mathematical reasoning) serve as benchmarks for calibrating strength judgments. Bartha's distinction between "horizontal" analogies (between coordinate domains at the same level of generality) and "vertical" analogies (between a model and the phenomena it models) is adopted in the type classification. Crucially, Bartha also provides a sophisticated account of *why* analogical arguments can be rationally persuasive even when they are not reducible to enumerative induction or deduction. His answer — that good analogies track "prior associations" grounded in the structure of the source domain, which function as epistemic constraints on what can reasonably be projected — supplies the philosophical justification for treating the prior-association condition as the linchpin of analogical evaluation rather than as one consideration among many.

**3. Mill, John Stuart. (1843). *A System of Logic, Ratiocinative and Inductive*. Book III, Chapter XX ("Of Analogy").**

Mill's chapter on analogy is the canonical nineteenth-century treatment and remains philosophically fertile. Mill characterized analogical reasoning as inference from "a certain number of points of agreement" between two things to "an indefinite number of others" — a formulation that captures both the ampliative character of analogy (it goes beyond the evidence) and its inherent fallibility (the number of unexamined properties is indefinite, and any one of them may be a point of difference). Mill's key insight, which has shaped every subsequent analysis, is that the strength of an analogical argument is not a function of the absolute number of shared properties but of the *proportion* of shared properties to the total properties of the things compared — and, critically, of the proportion of *known* shared properties to the *unknown* properties. This proportional criterion is integrated into the strength-grade assessment (Section 3.7) as one axis of evaluation, alongside Bartha's more demanding prior-association condition. Mill also connected analogical reasoning to his methods of experimental inquiry: an analogy is strengthened when the shared properties survive variation across multiple, diverse source cases (the Method of Agreement applied to analogical reasoning), and an analogy is weakened or defeated when a relevant difference between source and target is identified (the Method of Difference applied to analogical reasoning). This connection between analogy and Mill's inductive methods informs the counter-analogy audit (Step 5), which tests whether the analogy survives the introduction of alternative source cases. Mill's sober assessment of the weakness of analogical reasoning — he regarded it as the weakest form of inductive inference, inferior to the methods of agreement, difference, and concomitant variation — provides a benchmark against which claims of analogical strength should be measured. Yet Mill also recognized that analogical reasoning is, in many domains, all we have: when direct experimental control is impossible (as in historical explanation, moral reasoning, and large-scale scientific theorizing), analogy is the only ampliative inference form available, and its weakness is a fact about our epistemic situation rather than a defect of the inference form itself. This tension — between the rational indispensability of analogical reasoning and its intrinsic fallibility — runs through all subsequent philosophical treatments and is reflected in this skill's calibrated strength scale, which reserves the "Compelling" grade for analogies that overcome Mill's default skepticism.

**4. Gentner, Dedre. (1983). "Structure-Mapping: A Theoretical Framework for Analogy." *Cognitive Science*, 7(2): 155–170.**

Gentner's structure-mapping theory, developed within cognitive science, provides the most rigorous available framework for distinguishing between different kinds of similarity and for determining which similarities contribute argumentative weight to an analogy. The central claim of structure-mapping theory is that analogical reasoning is fundamentally about mapping *systems of relations* rather than isolated attributes. Mere attribute similarity — "the source is red and round, and the target is red and round" — contributes little to analogical inference; what matters is relational similarity — "x causes y in the source, and a causes b in the target" — and, even more importantly, *higher-order relational similarity* — "the causal structure that explains the behavior of the source also explains the behavior of the target." Gentner's systematicity principle states that analogical mappings prefer connected systems of relations governed by higher-order constraining relations (like causality or mathematical dependency) over isolated relational matches. This skill applies Gentner's framework in the mapping procedure: the source–target mapping table explicitly distinguishes object mappings, attribute mappings, first-order relational mappings, and higher-order relational mappings, and the strength assessment weights higher-order relational correspondences more heavily than first-order relational correspondences, which in turn are weighted more heavily than mere attribute correspondences. Gentner's work also grounds the distinction between analogy (relational mapping), literal similarity (both relational and attributive mapping), and mere-appearance matching (attributive mapping only) — a distinction that informs the classification of the analogy type and the boundary condition concerning non-analogical comparisons. Subsequent experimental work in the structure-mapping tradition (e.g., Gentner & Markman, 1997; Holyoak & Thagard, 1995) has confirmed that human reasoners spontaneously privilege relational over attributive similarity when evaluating analogies, and that analogies rich in higher-order relational structure are both more memorable and more persuasive. This empirical finding provides independent corroboration for the weighting scheme used in this skill's strength assessment, which assigns the greatest argumentative weight to higher-order relational correspondences.

**5. Walton, Douglas; Reed, Chris; and Macagno, Fabrizio. (2008). *Argumentation Schemes*. Cambridge University Press. Chapter on "Argument from Analogy" (pp. 48–58).**

Walton, Reed, and Macagno formalize analogical reasoning within the broader framework of argumentation schemes — stereotypical patterns of reasoning that carry presumptive weight and that are evaluated through a standardized set of critical questions. Their scheme for argument from analogy is as follows: *Premise 1*: Generally, case A1 is similar to case A2. *Premise 2*: Proposition P is true in case A1. *Conclusion*: Therefore, proposition P is true in case A2. The associated critical questions are: (CQ1) Is A1 relevantly similar to A2 in the respect cited? (CQ2) Is A1 true — i.e., does the source case genuinely have property P? (CQ3) Are there differences between A1 and A2 that would undermine the force of the similarity? (CQ4) Could another case be cited, equally similar to A2, in which P is false? These four critical questions map directly onto the analytical steps of this skill: CQ1 corresponds to the relevance assessment (Step 3), CQ2 to a basic factual check on the source description (incorporated into the mapping table), CQ3 to the disanalogy detection (Step 4), and CQ4 to the counter-analogy audit (Step 5). Walton et al. also provide a dialectical profile for analogical arguments, tracing how the burden of proof shifts between proponent and respondent as each critical question is raised and answered — a profile that informs the dialectical recommendations in Step 7. Their treatment of the distinction between argument from analogy and argument from example (where the source is a particular instance of a generalization rather than a distinct domain) is incorporated into the boundary conditions. Walton et al.'s framework is especially valuable for this skill because it bridges the gap between the normative evaluation of analogical arguments (are they good arguments?) and their dialectical management (how should participants in an argumentative exchange respond to them?). This bridging function is realized in the progression from Step 4 (disanalogy detection, corresponding to CQ3) through Step 7 (dialectical recommendations, which operationalize the burden-shifting profile that Walton et al. describe).

**6. Keynes, John Neville. (1884). *Studies and Exercises in Formal Logic*. 4th ed. (1906), Part IV, Chapter VIII ("Analogy").**

Keynes's treatment of analogy, while less well-known than Mill's, provides a set of precise and practically useful criteria for evaluating analogical strength that complement and refine Mill's proportional approach. Keynes argued that the force of an analogical argument depends on three factors: (a) the *number* of points of resemblance — more shared properties increase the probability that the projected property is among them, though with diminishing returns once the properties are non-independent; (b) the *variety* of points of resemblance — shared properties drawn from different "orders" or aspects of the things compared (e.g., morphological, functional, historical) carry more weight than multiple properties drawn from a single tightly clustered set, because variety reduces the probability that the shared properties are artifacts of a common cause irrelevant to the projected property; and (c) the *character of the inferred property* — inferences to properties that are "determinable a priori" (i.e., conceptual consequences of the shared properties) are stronger than inferences to properties that can only be established empirically, because the former do not depend on the contingent association between the shared properties and the projected property in the source domain. Keynes's variety criterion is particularly useful for diagnosing analogies that list many shared properties but all within a single narrow category (e.g., listing ten morphological similarities while ignoring functional or ecological differences) — a pattern that this skill flags as a weakness even if the absolute number of shared properties seems impressive. Keynes's distinction between a priori determinable and empirically determinable projected properties informs the classification of analogies as a priori vs. empirical in the type classification (Section 3.3).

**Interrelations Among the Foundations.** These six sources, though drawn from different disciplines and historical periods, form a coherent and mutually reinforcing analytical framework. Hesse's material analogy provides the metaphysical grounding for Bartha's prior-association condition: a prior association is, in Hesse's terms, a material connection among properties in the source domain. Mill's proportional criterion provides the probabilistic scaffolding that justifies why prior associations, once established, license projection — the more properties are shared, and the fewer remain unknown, the higher the probability that the target will share the projected property. Gentner's structure-mapping theory supplies the cognitive mechanism: prior associations are tracked not through isolated property lists but through systematic relational mappings, and the systematicity of the mapping is what makes analogical inference cognitively natural and epistemically reliable. Walton et al.'s critical questions operationalize the Bartha conditions within a dialectical framework, specifying exactly what a respondent must do to challenge an analogy and what a proponent must do to defend one. Keynes's variety criterion adds a dimension of epistemic robustness: an analogy whose shared properties are drawn from multiple independent dimensions is more likely to survive disanalogy detection because a single disanalogy is less likely to undermine all dimensions simultaneously. Together, these sources provide not merely a checklist for evaluating analogical arguments but an integrated theory of what analogical reasoning is, why it sometimes works, and when it fails.

## 5. Steps

Execute the following steps in order when analyzing an analogical argument. Each step produces findings that serve as inputs to the subsequent steps, so the analyst should resist the temptation to leap to a strength judgment before completing the full sequence. The steps are designed to enforce a disciplined progression from descriptive mapping through evaluative testing to dialectical synthesis. The seven-step sequence is not merely a procedural checklist; it embodies a specific methodological commitment — namely, that the evaluation of analogical arguments requires descriptive adequacy (correctly identifying what the analogy claims), explanatory depth (understanding why the inference would or would not go through), and dialectical sensitivity (assessing how the analogy functions in an argumentative exchange), and that these three desiderata are best served by addressing them in strict sequence rather than simultaneously.

The analyst should allocate time and attention across the steps in proportion to their importance for the specific argument under analysis. For an analogy whose primary weakness is a failure of prior association (no connecting principle links shared properties to projected property), Step 3 will be the most labor-intensive and the most consequential. For an analogy that clearly satisfies prior association but faces a potentially defeating disanalogy, Step 4 will be the focal point of analysis. The analyst is not required to treat all steps as equally demanding in every case; the framework is designed to channel analytical effort toward the evaluative dimensions that are genuinely contested.

**Step 1: Map Source–Target Correspondences.**

Construct an exhaustive table of correspondences between the source domain S and the target domain T. For every entity, property, or relation in S that is cited by the argument as part of the analogical basis, identify its counterpart in T — or note explicitly when the argument asserts a correspondence without specifying a counterpart (a gap that may indicate an incomplete or sloppy analogy). Categorize each correspondence using Gentner's taxonomy:

- *Object/entity mappings (O-mappings):* Individual entities in S and their counterparts in T (e.g., "the watchmaker : the divine creator"). O-mappings are the least informative type of correspondence and carry minimal argumentative weight.
- *Attribute/property mappings (A-mappings):* Monadic properties said to be shared (e.g., "both the watch and the universe exhibit complexity"). A-mappings carry more weight than O-mappings but less than relational mappings.
- *First-order relational mappings (R1-mappings):* Dyadic or polyadic relations among entities that recur in both domains (e.g., "the parts of the watch are arranged to serve a function : the parts of the universe are arranged to serve a function"). R1-mappings are the primary carriers of analogical inference.
- *Higher-order relational mappings (R2-mappings):* Relations among relations that exhibit structural isomorphism (e.g., "functional arrangement explains complexity in the source : functional arrangement explains complexity in the target"). R2-mappings carry the greatest argumentative weight because they capture the systematic, principled nature of the analogy.

Identify the *projected property* (P*) — the specific property or relation that the argument infers to hold in T on the basis of its holding in S. The projected property must be clearly distinguished from the shared properties; if the argument conflates them (treating the projected property as if it were already established as shared), note this as a potential circularity or question-begging move. Consult `analogy.md` to verify that the mapping respects the formal structure of analogical argumentation and to distinguish the argument from adjacent forms such as argument from example or argument from precedent.

**Step 2: Classify the Analogy Type.**

Determine the kind of analogy at work by applying multiple independent classification schemes. First, apply Hesse's taxonomy: catalogue the *positive analogy* (the full set of properties known to be shared between S and T), the *negative analogy* (the full set of properties known to differ), and the *neutral analogy* (the properties whose status in T is unknown — including the projected property, which is the target of the inference). Hesse's classification is exhaustive: every property of S falls into one of these three categories.

Second, classify the analogy as *illustrative* or *argumentative*. An illustrative analogy uses the source merely to clarify, explain, or render intuitive a feature of the target that is already known or established on independent grounds; it makes no claim to extend knowledge. An argumentative analogy uses the source to support an inference to a conclusion about the target that is not already known or established; it claims to provide a *reason* to believe something about the target. This distinction is crucial because illustrative analogies are not subject to the prior-association condition in the same way: an illustrative analogy can be good (clear, accurate, illuminating) without satisfying Bartha's criteria, but it carries zero argumentative force by definition. When an argument is presented as if it were argumentative but upon analysis functions only illustratively, flag this as a mismatch between presentation and logical function.

Third, classify the analogy as *a priori* or *empirical*. An a priori analogy is one in which the inference from shared properties to projected property can be evaluated from the armchair — the connection is conceptual, definitional, or logical, and empirical investigation of the target domain is not required to assess the analogy's strength. An empirical analogy is one in which the inference depends on contingent facts about the world — causal laws, statistical regularities, or nomological connections — and its evaluation requires (or would be strengthened by) empirical evidence. This classification follows Keynes's distinction between properties determinable a priori and those determinable only empirically.

Fourth, classify the analogy as *formal* or *material*. A formal analogy is one in which the mapping is purely structural or syntactic — the source and target share an abstract relational pattern, but the relata are of entirely different kinds and there is no substantive connection between the shared structure and the projected property (e.g., an analogy between the structure of a mathematical proof and the structure of a legal argument, where the mapping is purely formal). A material analogy (in Hesse's sense) is one in which the shared properties belong to a cluster of properties that are nomologically or causally connected, such that the presence of some members of the cluster licenses the inference to others. Material analogies carry greater argumentative weight than formal analogies because they are anchored in substantive dependencies rather than mere structural isomorphism.

Record all four classifications in the report. If the analogy resists clean classification along one of these dimensions (e.g., it has features of both a priori and empirical reasoning), note the ambiguity and explain its source. The analyst should be alert to several common classification pitfalls. First, do not classify an analogy as "argumentative" simply because it appears in an argumentative context — the question is whether the analogy itself is doing argumentative work or merely illustrating a conclusion reached on other grounds. A philosopher may deploy a vivid analogy within a broader argument without the analogy itself bearing any inferential weight. Second, do not classify an analogy as "a priori" merely because no empirical evidence is cited — the question is whether the inferential link is *in principle* evaluable without empirical investigation. An analogy may be empirical (its connecting principle is causal or statistical) even if the proponent has not provided the relevant empirical evidence, and this gap is itself a finding worth reporting. Third, do not classify an analogy as "formal" merely because the source and target belong to different ontological categories — the question is whether the shared properties are substantively connected to the projected property. An analogy between a biological system and a social system may be material (in Hesse's sense) if the shared functional properties are nomologically connected to the projected functional property in both domains. The formal/material distinction tracks the epistemic grounding of the inference, not the ontological distance between the domains.

**Step 3: Evaluate the Relevance of Shared Properties.**

This is the core evaluative step. Determine whether the properties and relations that S and T share are genuinely relevant to the conclusion — that is, whether there exists a plausible *connecting principle* that links the shared properties to the projected property. Apply Bartha's prior-association condition by asking: in the source domain S, is there an established connection between the shared properties and the projected property, such that knowing that something possesses the shared properties gives one reason (even a defeasible reason) to think it also possesses the projected property?

Identify the type of connection, if any: *causal* (the shared properties are causes, or part of a causal mechanism, of the projected property in the source, or vice versa); *definitional/conceptual* (the projected property is analytically or conceptually entailed by the shared properties — if something has the shared properties, it eo ipso has the projected property); *functional* (the shared properties constitute or realize a function, and the projected property is the characteristic manifestation of that function, such that functional isomorphism between S and T warrants projecting the manifestation); or *statistical/correlational* (in the class of things to which S belongs, the shared properties are robustly correlated with the projected property, and S is representative of that class).

If no connection of any of these four types can be identified — if the shared properties are merely co-present with the projected property in S without any documented dependence, or if the proposed connection is speculative, ad hoc, or itself the very thing at issue in the debate — then the analogy fails the prior-association condition. In this case, the analogy's argumentative force is severely diminished regardless of the number of shared properties or the vividness of the mapping. Note, however, that the failure of prior association does not automatically render the analogy a fallacy; it may still function as an illustrative device, or it may gesture toward a connection that, while not yet established, could be established by further investigation. The analysis should distinguish between "no known connection" (which may be remediable) and "no conceivable connection" (which is fatal).

For argumentative analogies that *satisfy* the prior-association condition, the analysis should specify exactly what the connecting principle is and assess its strength — is it a well-established law, a robust but defeasible generalization, a plausible but contested hypothesis, or a merely conceivable mechanism? The strength of the connecting principle directly constrains the strength of the analogy. For illustrative analogies, the relevance assessment asks a different question: does the mapping accurately represent the target, or does it distort the target by importing features that are artifacts of the source and that do not hold in the target? An illustrative analogy fails if it is misleading — if it suggests features that the target does not possess — even if the mapping is vivid and intuitive.

**Step 4: Detect and Assess Disanalogies.**

Systematically catalogue every relevant difference between source and target — that is, every difference that plausibly bears on the inference from shared properties to projected property. The keyword is *relevant*: not every difference is a disanalogy in the argumentatively significant sense. A difference is relevant if and only if it interacts with the connecting principle identified in Step 3 in a way that undermines, blocks, or calls into question the projection from source to target. Differences that are orthogonal to the connecting principle — however striking or rhetorically salient — are irrelevant and should be noted as such but not allowed to influence the strength assessment.

For each relevant disanalogy, determine its severity:

- *Defeating:* The disanalogy severs the inferential link. Even if the shared properties hold in T, the difference between S and T blocks the specific causal, conceptual, or probabilistic mechanism that licenses the projection. A defeating disanalogy renders the analogy argumentatively worthless — it does not support the conclusion at all. The proponent cannot repair a defeating disanalogy by qualifying the conclusion (unless the qualification effectively abandons the original conclusion); they must reformulate the analogy with a different source or a different inferential pathway.
- *Weakening:* The disanalogy reduces the probability of the conclusion or narrows its scope, but does not completely sever the inferential link. The analogy retains some probative value, but less than it would have in the absence of the disanalogy. The proponent can respond by qualifying the conclusion (e.g., "probably" rather than "certainly," or "in most cases" rather than "in all cases"), by providing independent evidence that mitigates the disanalogy's impact, or by acknowledging the disanalogy and arguing that the remaining analogical force is sufficient for the dialectical purpose.
- *Irrelevant:* The disanalogy, however striking, does not interact with the connecting principle. It may be noted for completeness, but it should not affect the strength assessment.

For each defeating or weakening disanalogy, trace exactly how it disrupts the inferential pathway. A vague claim that "S and T differ in important ways" is insufficient; the analysis must identify the specific link in the causal, conceptual, or probabilistic chain that the disanalogy breaks. Where the argument's proponent has anticipated and addressed known disanalogies (e.g., "one might object that X differs from Y in respect Z, but this difference is irrelevant because..."), the analysis evaluates the adequacy of that response.

Consult `fallacy-catalog.md` for each disanalogy classified as defeating: if the disanalogy is one that a competent reasoner should have recognized, and the proponent has suppressed or ignored it, then the argument may additionally commit the fallacy of suppressed disanalogy. Note this in the fallacy cross-reference section of the report.

When detecting disanalogies, the analyst should adopt a systematic search strategy rather than relying on casual inspection. Begin by enumerating all properties and relations of the source domain that the argument does *not* claim are shared with the target — the negative analogy in Hesse's taxonomy. From this set, filter for those that are potentially relevant to the connecting principle. Then, for each candidate disanalogy, ask the counterfactual question: if the target *did* share this property with the source (contrary to fact), would the inference to the projected property be stronger or unchanged? If the answer is yes, the difference is a genuine disanalogy. This counterfactual test distinguishes real disanalogies — differences whose elimination would strengthen the analogy — from mere dissimilarities that are irrelevant to the inference regardless of how they are resolved. The analyst should also be alert to *structural* disanalogies — differences not in individual properties but in the pattern of relations that the analogy maps. A structural disanalogy occurs when a higher-order relation present in the source (e.g., a feedback loop, a hierarchical dependency, a symmetry constraint) has no counterpart in the target, and the projected property depends on that higher-order relation. Structural disanalogies are typically more severe than property-level disanalogies because they undermine the systematicity of the mapping.

**Step 5: Construct a Counter-Analogy Audit.**

Following Walton's fourth critical question ("Could another case be cited, equally similar to the target, in which the projected property is false?"), generate one or more counter-analogies. A counter-analogy is an alternative source domain S' that shares the same cited properties with the target T but that lacks the projected property P* (or possesses a property inconsistent with P*). The purpose of the counter-analogy audit is to test whether the original analogy's inference is robust or whether the inference can be shown to be unreliable by producing a structurally parallel case in which the same inferential move would lead to a false conclusion.

For each counter-analogy, assess:
- *Plausibility:* Does S' genuinely share the properties cited in the original analogy? If the counter-analogy stretches or misdescribes the shared properties, it is not a genuine counter-analogy and should be dismissed. A counter-analogy is only as good as the accuracy of its mapping.
- *Relevance:* Does the counter-analogy track the same inferential pathway as the original? If the counter-analogy introduces a disanalogy of its own that explains why P* does not hold in S' (a disanalogy not present between the original S and T), then the counter-analogy does not undermine the original analogy — it is merely a different analogy with different properties. The proponent of the original analogy can respond that the counter-analogy is disanalogous in a relevant respect.
- *Dialectical burden:* Does the counter-analogy put pressure on the original proponent to explain what distinguishes their preferred analogy from the counter-analogy? If the proponent cannot articulate a principled, non-ad-hoc distinction between the two source domains, the original analogy's inference is suspect.

If the original argument's proponent has explicitly considered and rejected competing analogies, the analysis evaluates the adequacy of that response. A proponent who can explain why their analogy succeeds where a prima facie similar analogy fails demonstrates command of the underlying connecting principle and strengthens their case.

Several additional considerations guide the construction and evaluation of counter-analogies. First, the counter-analogy should be constructed at the same level of abstraction as the original analogy. If the original maps specific entities and relations, a counter-analogy that operates at a much higher or lower level of generality is not genuinely parallel and its force should be discounted. Second, the analyst should consider whether the counter-analogy is *symmetric* — that is, whether the original analogy would serve as an equally plausible counter-analogy to the counter-analogy. If the two analogies are symmetric (each undermines the other equally), then neither provides a decisive consideration, and the dispute reduces to which analogous case better captures the relevant features of the target — a question that often requires independent argument about what those relevant features are. Third, the analyst should consider the *diversity* of counter-analogies: a single counter-analogy may be dismissed as idiosyncratic, but multiple, independently constructed counter-analogies that all share the cited properties with the target while lacking the projected property constitute a much stronger challenge. If the original analogy's inference would lead to false conclusions across a range of structurally similar cases, the inference pattern itself is unreliable.

**Step 6: Assess Argumentative Force.**

Synthesize the findings from Steps 1 through 5 into an overall strength judgment on the five-tier scale defined in Section 3.7 of the Output specification. The factors to be weighed, in descending order of importance, are:

1. *Satisfaction of the prior-association condition* (from Step 3). This is the single most important factor. An analogy that fails the prior-association condition cannot rise above "Weak" regardless of other virtues.
2. *Presence and severity of defeating or weakening disanalogies* (from Step 4). A single defeating disanalogy reduces the analogy to "Merely Rhetorical" or "Weak" depending on the degree to which the disanalogy was foreseeable and addressable.
3. *Relational depth of the mapping* (from Step 1). Analogies rich in higher-order relational correspondences (R2-mappings) are stronger than analogies that map only first-order relations, which are in turn stronger than analogies that map only attributes. Apply Gentner's systematicity principle: connected systems of relations carry more weight than isolated relational matches.
4. *Number and variety of shared properties* (from Steps 1 and 2). Apply Mill's proportional criterion (what fraction of the target's total properties do the known shared properties represent, and how does this bear on the probability that the projected property is among them?) and Keynes's variety criterion (are the shared properties drawn from multiple, independent aspects of the source and target, or are they clustered in a single dimension?). A large number of shared properties drawn from a single narrow category is weaker than a smaller number drawn from diverse categories.
5. *Vulnerability to counter-analogies* (from Step 5). An analogy that is undermined by a plausible counter-analogy is weaker than one that withstands the counter-analogy audit, even if the other factors are comparable.

The assigned strength grade must be justified by explicit reference to these factors, and the relative contribution of each factor must be made transparent. The analyst should resist the temptation to average across factors: a single catastrophic failure (e.g., a defeating disanalogy) can overwhelm multiple positive indicators. The grade represents an all-things-considered judgment, not a mechanical sum.

If the analogy is classified as illustrative in Step 2, it receives no argumentative force grade (or is explicitly marked as "N/A – illustrative"). Illustrative analogies are evaluated along a separate dimension: clarity, accuracy, and freedom from distortion. An illustrative analogy can be good or bad, but it cannot be "strong" or "weak" in the argumentative sense.

**Step 7: Formulate Dialectical Recommendations.**

Translate the strength assessment into practical, actionable guidance for both the proponent and the respondent of the analogical argument. The recommendations should be specific to the argument under analysis and grounded in the findings of the preceding steps; generic advice ("the proponent should provide more evidence") is insufficient.

*For the proponent:*
- Identify which weaknesses can be shored up with additional argumentative work. If the prior-association condition is unsatisfied, recommend the specific kind of evidence or argument that would establish the required connection (e.g., a causal study, a conceptual argument, a statistical analysis). If the analogy is undermined by a weakening disanalogy, recommend how the conclusion could be qualified to accommodate the disanalogy without abandoning the core inference.
- If the analogy is illustrative but presented as argumentative, recommend reformulating it explicitly as illustrative and supplementing it with independent arguments, or, alternatively, developing the analogy into a genuinely argumentative form by establishing the missing prior association.
- If the analogy is strong, recommend that the proponent explicitly articulate the connecting principle and address known disanalogies preemptively, converting the analogy from an intuitive gesture into a fully explicit argument.
- If the analogy is weak or merely rhetorical, recommend that the proponent either abandon it and seek alternative argumentative strategies or dramatically restrict its scope and acknowledge its limitations.

*For the respondent:*
- Identify the most promising lines of attack, prioritized by their dialectical efficiency. A defeating disanalogy is the most efficient attack if one exists, because it requires only that the respondent demonstrate one critical difference rather than addressing the entire mapping. If no defeating disanalogy is available, the next most efficient strategy is to challenge the prior association — to argue that even in the source domain, the shared properties do not support the projected property.
- If the analogy is weak, recommend that the respondent can legitimately dismiss it without extensive engagement: point out the specific failure (lack of prior association, presence of a defeating disanalogy, etc.) and note that the analogy does not shift the burden of proof.
- If a counter-analogy surfaced in Step 5 that is both plausible and relevant, recommend deploying it as the primary response, placing the burden on the proponent to articulate what distinguishes their preferred analogy from the counter-analogy.

*Burden-shifting analysis:*
- A strong or compelling analogy shifts at least some dialectical burden to the respondent: the respondent cannot simply deny the conclusion but must engage with the analogy by identifying a specific disanalogy or challenging the connecting principle. The analysis should specify exactly what the respondent must do to discharge this burden.
- A weak or merely rhetorical analogy imposes no burden on the respondent: the respondent can dismiss it with a brief explanation of its failure. The proponent retains the full burden of providing a different argument.

Situate these recommendations within the broader argumentative context, referencing any available dialectical map (from phil-dialectical-map). If the analogy is part of a larger argumentative strategy — e.g., one prong of a multi-pronged case, or a response to a specific objection — the recommendations should address how the fate of the analogy affects the larger strategy.

## 6. Quality Criteria

A high-quality Analogy Analysis Report satisfies the following criteria. These criteria are designed to be applied by peer reviewers, automated quality-checking tools, or self-assessment by the analyst.

1. **Exhaustiveness of Mapping.** Every correspondence asserted or implied by the analogical argument is captured in the source–target mapping table. No significant shared property, relation, or structural feature is omitted, and no correspondence is attributed to the argument that it does not support. The mapping table should be sufficiently complete that a reader unfamiliar with the original text could reconstruct the argument's analogical structure from the table alone. Gaps in the mapping — where the argument gestures toward a similarity without specifying a counterpart — are explicitly flagged rather than silently filled in by the analyst.

2. **Discrimination of Analogy Types.** The four-dimensional classification in Step 2 (illustrative/argumentative, a priori/empirical, formal/material, Hesse's positive/negative/neutral) is precise, non-overlapping, and justified by reference to the text. The analyst does not conflate illustrative with argumentative analogies — a common error that inflates the perceived argumentative force of vivid but merely explanatory analogies. When the analogy exhibits features of multiple types (e.g., it is partly formal and partly material), these are disaggregated rather than blurred into an ambiguous hybrid category.

3. **Mechanistic Specificity in Relevance Assessment.** The prior-association analysis in Step 3 does not rest on vague gestures toward "similarity" or "plausibility" but specifies the exact mechanism — causal, conceptual, functional, or statistical — connecting the shared properties to the projected property in the source domain. Assertions of relevance without mechanistic backing are flagged as incomplete, and the report distinguishes between "the connection is established," "the connection is plausible but not established," "the connection is speculative," and "no connection has been proposed." The analyst's own judgment about the plausibility of the connection is clearly separated from the proponent's claims about it.

4. **Rigor in Disanalogy Assessment.** Every detected disanalogy is traced through its specific impact on the inferential pathway. The defeating/weakening/irrelevant classification is justified with reasoning, not merely asserted. The analyst does not treat the mere existence of differences between source and target as problematic — a ubiquitous feature of all analogies — but only those differences that interact with the connecting principle. The severity assessment reflects a careful calibration: a disanalogy is not classified as "defeating" unless it genuinely blocks the inference, and a disanalogy is not classified as "irrelevant" merely because the analyst finds the analogy otherwise compelling. A high-quality disanalogy assessment also considers whether the disanalogy can be *repaired* — that is, whether the proponent could modify the description of the source, the target, or the inferential pathway in a way that neutralizes the disanalogy without abandoning the core of the analogy. If a disanalogy is defeating but repairable, the analysis should note the repair strategy, because this distinguishes analogies that are fundamentally flawed from those that are merely incomplete or imprecise.

5. **Calibrated Strength Judgments.** The five-tier strength grade is justified by explicit, transparent reference to the evidence gathered in the preceding steps, with the relative weighting of factors made clear. The analyst avoids both grade inflation — labeling weak analogies as "moderate" because the mapping is clever, vivid, or rhetorically effective — and excessive skepticism — dismissing strong analogies for minor or irrelevant disanalogies. The justification should be sufficiently detailed that a second analyst, applying the same framework to the same evidence, would arrive at the same or a neighboring grade. The analyst should also note the *confidence* of the grade: some analyses admit of a clear, unambiguous judgment (e.g., a clear defeating disanalogy makes the grade unambiguously "Merely Rhetorical"), while others involve trade-offs among factors where reasonable analysts could disagree (e.g., whether a particular disanalogy is defeating or merely weakening). In the latter case, the report should indicate the range of reasonable disagreement and the considerations that could resolve it.

6. **Constructive Dialectical Guidance.** The recommendations in Step 7 are actionable, argument-specific, and grounded in the analysis rather than generic. For the proponent: the recommendations identify the weakest link and propose concrete steps to strengthen it. For the respondent: the recommendations identify the most efficient line of attack and explain what must be shown to succeed. The recommendations reflect an understanding of the argument's strategic role within its dialectical context, including any burden-shifting dynamics.

7. **Scholarly Grounding.** The analysis demonstrates familiarity with the canonical sources cited in Section 4 and applies their frameworks correctly. Where a source's framework directly informs a judgment — Bartha's prior-association condition in the relevance assessment, Hesse's material analogy in the type classification, Gentner's systematicity principle in the strength weighting, Walton's critical questions in the dialectical recommendations — the connection is made explicit, either by direct citation or by recognizable application of the framework's concepts. The analyst does not simply name-drop sources but uses them as analytical tools.

8. **Fallacious Reasoning Identification.** The report correctly determines whether the argument crosses the line from weak analogical reasoning into genuine fallacious reasoning, applying the diagnostic criteria from `fallacy-catalog.md` for false analogy and its subcategories. The fallacy determination is not a mechanical consequence of a low strength grade — a weak analogy is not automatically a fallacy — but reflects an independent assessment of whether the argument exhibits the specific structural flaws (single similar instance, suppressed disanalogy, faulty comparison) that characterize fallacious analogical reasoning.

## 7. Relationships to Other Skills

The phil-analogy-check skill is embedded within the broader suite of philosophical analysis skills and entertains dependency, feed-forward, and coordination relationships with other skills. Understanding these relationships is essential for integrating analogy analysis into multi-stage analytical workflows.

**Upstream dependencies (inputs that phil-analogy-check requires or strongly benefits from):**

- **phil-argument-reconstruct.** This is the primary upstream dependency. A complete and well-structured argument reconstruction is the necessary starting point for analogy analysis. The reconstruction isolates the analogical inference from surrounding text, identifies explicit and implicit premises, and extracts the structural elements (source domain, target domain, shared properties, projected property) that the mapping procedure requires. Without a prior reconstruction, the analyst must perform reconstruction work before beginning the analogy-specific analysis, which introduces the risk of misidentifying what the analogy is actually being used to argue for. The quality of the reconstruction directly constrains the quality and reliability of the analogy analysis.

- **`analogy.md` (argument form reference).** This reference document defines the canonical logical form of analogical arguments and distinguishes them from adjacent argument types. The analyst consults it in Step 1 to verify that the argument under analysis genuinely has analogical form and to ensure that the mapping respects the formal structure (premises about shared properties, conclusion about projected property). The reference also provides a checklist for identifying when an argument that uses analogical language does not in fact have analogical logical structure — a boundary condition elaborated in Section 8.

- **`fallacy-catalog.md` (false analogy entry).** This reference provides the diagnostic criteria for false analogy and its subcategories. The analyst consults it in Step 4 (to determine whether unaddressed disanalogies constitute suppressed-disanalogy fallacies) and in the Fallacy Cross-Reference section of the output (to render a final determination). The fallacy catalog also provides historical examples of false analogies that can serve as benchmarks for calibrating the severity assessment.

**Downstream dependencies (skills that consume or build upon the output of phil-analogy-check):**

- **phil-counterexample.** When a disanalogy detected in Step 4 meets the structural criteria for a counterexample — that is, when the source and target differ in a way that makes the conclusion false of the target even if true of the source — the analogy analysis can be passed to phil-counterexample for a formal assessment of whether the counterexample is genuine, whether it meets relevant modal constraints, and whether it targets the conclusion in its strongest form. The analogy analysis identifies the candidate counterexample; phil-counterexample adjudicates its status.

- **phil-parity-check.** When two competing analogical arguments are advanced for opposing conclusions about the same target (e.g., "abortion is like killing an innocent adult" vs. "abortion is like unplugging a life-support machine"), each analogy is first analyzed independently by phil-analogy-check. The resulting reports are then passed to phil-parity-check, which compares their strength grades, identifies any asymmetries in the analyses, and determines whether rational adjudication between the two analogies requires further argument or whether one analogy clearly dominates the other. This is a common pattern in applied ethics and legal reasoning, where competing analogies function as the primary vehicles of normative argument.

- **phil-distinction-track.** When a disanalogy is found to be defeating, the philosophical work of articulating the precise nature of the relevant distinction — what exactly makes the source and target different in the respect that matters — falls within the remit of phil-distinction-track. The analogy analysis identifies the *site* of the distinction (the specific point in the mapping where the disanalogy operates) and the *type* of distinction (causal, conceptual, normative); phil-distinction-track articulates its *nature*, *grounds*, and *implications*, often by situating it within a broader theoretical framework. The two skills are complementary: phil-analogy-check diagnoses the problem; phil-distinction-track provides the positive account of the distinction.

- **phil-fallacy-check.** If the analogy analysis determines that the argument commits a fallacy (based on the `fallacy-catalog.md` criteria), the report can be passed to phil-fallacy-check for a formal fallacy diagnosis, including classification within the broader fallacy taxonomy, analysis of the dialectical context of the fallacy, and assessment of whether the fallacy is remediable or fatal to the argument.

**Coordination relationships (skills that provide contextual enrichment):**

- **phil-conceptual-analysis.** When the analogical argument bears on the extension or intension of a contested concept, the intensional structure of the concept — identified by phil-conceptual-analysis — constrains which shared properties can count as relevant. For example, if the analogy argues that a novel entity falls under a concept C, the conceptual analysis of C determines whether the shared properties are among the necessary and sufficient conditions for C-application. Conversely, an analogy analysis may reveal that the proponent's concept of C is ambiguous between two interpretations, one of which makes the analogy work and the other of which does not — a finding that feeds back into conceptual analysis.

- **phil-dialectical-map.** When a dialectical map is available, it situates the analogical argument within its broader argumentative context — showing what the analogy is responding to, what it is meant to establish, and what countermoves have already been made. This context is essential for the dialectical recommendations in Step 7, which are only actionable if they address the argument's actual strategic role. The dialectical map also reveals whether the proponent has already addressed certain disanalogies or counter-analogies in the surrounding text.

- **phil-charity-check.** Before finalizing the analysis, the analyst may wish to run phil-charity-check on the interpretation of the analogical argument, to ensure that the mapping does not inadvertently attribute to the proponent a weaker analogy than the one they intended. This is especially important when the reconstructed argument is ambiguous about which properties are being mapped or what the projected property is.

## 8. Boundary Conditions

The following edge cases define the boundary of this skill's proper application and identify situations in which the analyst should defer to or coordinate with other skills, or in which the standard analytical procedure requires modification. Boundary conditions serve a dual purpose: they prevent the misapplication of the analogy analysis framework to texts and arguments that it was not designed to handle, and they alert the analyst to special cases that, while still within the skill's scope, require methodological adjustments. The analyst should review these boundary conditions at the outset of any analysis, before beginning Step 1, to determine whether the argument under examination is a suitable candidate for analogical analysis or whether it should be redirected to a more appropriate skill.

1. **Non-Analogical Comparisons.** Not every statement that compares two things constitutes an analogical argument. Comparisons of the form "X is larger than Y," "X is more complex than Y," or "X resembles Y in color" that do not support an inference to a further, unobserved similarity are comparative observations, not analogical arguments, and fall outside the scope of this skill. Before applying the full analytical framework, the analyst must confirm that the argument under examination genuinely has analogical inferential structure — that it proceeds from a set of known similarities to an inferred similarity (the projected property) that is not already known to hold in the target. If the text employs the language of analogy without the inferential structure — e.g., "just as a captain steers a ship, so too does a general command an army" offered merely as a stylistic flourish with no conclusion being argued for — flag this as *pseudo-analogical* and defer to phil-argument-reconstruct for reclassification as a non-argumentative rhetorical device. If the comparative statement uses analogical language but the conclusion is not an inference to a further similarity but rather a claim about degree or proportion, reclassify the argument accordingly (e.g., as an a fortiori argument or an argument from proportion).

2. **Metaphors vs. Analogies.** Metaphors and analogies are related but distinct linguistic and cognitive phenomena, and philosophical texts frequently blur the boundary between them. A metaphor asserts or implies an identity or fusion between domains ("Juliet is the sun," "the mind is a computer") without necessarily mapping discrete, structured correspondences or drawing an explicit inference. An analogy, by contrast, maps discrete correspondences between source and target and uses those correspondences to support a conclusion. When a philosophical text employs extended metaphor that blurs into analogical argument — as is common in, e.g., the use of computer metaphors in philosophy of mind or the use of organic metaphors in political philosophy — the analyst must first disambiguate: extract the propositional content that can be rendered as an analogical argument (object mappings, property mappings, relational mappings, and a projected property), separate it from the merely figurative, expressive, or heuristic content, and analyze only the former. If the metaphor cannot be extracted into a determinate analogical structure — if it resists being paraphrased into a set of specific, evaluable similarity claims — then it falls outside the scope of this skill and should be deferred to phil-conceptual-analysis (if the metaphor is shaping the concept itself) or to a literary/rhetorical analysis. Note that Hesse's work is directly relevant to this boundary condition: she argued that metaphor and analogy in science form a continuum, with theoretical models functioning as both metaphorical redescriptions of phenomena and as analogical inference licenses. The analyst should be guided by whether the text is making a truth-apt claim that can be evaluated.

3. **Trivial or Tautological Analogies.** An analogy whose projected property is analytically entailed by the shared properties — such that the inference from shared properties to projected property is deductive rather than ampliative — is not a genuine analogical argument but a deductive argument in analogical disguise. For example: "Triangle A is like triangle B in having three sides; therefore, triangle A has three angles summing to 180 degrees" — where the shared property (having three sides) conceptually or mathematically entails the projected property (having angles summing to 180 degrees) for the relevant class of objects. In such cases, the analysis should note that the analogical form is logically superfluous: the argument does not depend on the analogy and can be restated as a straightforward deduction from the definition of a triangle. The analogy is not harmful (it does not introduce error), but it contributes no independent argumentative weight and should not be assessed as if it were a genuine analogical inference. Reclassify the argument as deductive and recommend that the proponent present it as such, reserving the analogical language for pedagogical purposes if desired.

4. **Analogies with No Identifiable Source Domain.** Some philosophical texts deploy what might be called "free-floating" or "elliptical" analogies — they gesture toward similarity using analogical language ("it is like when something happens, but for reasons," "it is analogous to the case we all know") without specifying a concrete, describable source domain. In these cases, the analogy is too indeterminate to analyze: without a specifiable source domain, the mapping procedure cannot get off the ground, and there is nothing against which to test disanalogies or assess prior association. The output should report that the analogy lacks a specifiable source domain and therefore cannot be evaluated. The analysis should: (a) flag this as a potential instance of merely rhetorical analogizing or of the fallacy of vagueness; (b) identify what minimal specification of the source domain would be required to render the analogy evaluable; and (c) recommend that the proponent specify the source domain before the analogy can be assessed. If the proponent responds to the request for specification with a determinate source domain, the analysis can proceed.

5. **Legal and Moral Analogies with Normative Force.** Analogies in legal and moral reasoning often carry a distinctive normative dimension that descriptive analogies lack. The claim is not merely that the target resembles the source in descriptive respects, but that the target *ought to be treated like* the source in virtue of those resemblances — that the descriptive similarities are normatively significant and ground a normative conclusion (a legal ruling, a moral judgment, a policy prescription). When analyzing such analogies, the standard procedure requires modification. The relevance assessment (Step 3) must identify not only a descriptive connecting principle linking shared properties to the projected property, but also a *normative principle* that licenses the move from descriptive similarity to normative conclusion. This normative principle is typically a principle of consistency (like cases should be treated alike), fairness, universalizability, or respect for precedent, and it may be either explicit or implicit in the argument. The absence of an articulated normative principle does not automatically defeat the analogy — the principle of treating like cases alike is often taken to be a default norm of practical reasoning — but the analyst should note its presence or absence, because normative analogies without a discernible grounding principle are vulnerable to challenge on the grounds that the descriptive similarity, however well-established, does not entail or support the normative conclusion without an additional normative premise. When the analogy is deployed in a legal context, the analysis should also consider whether the source case is a precedent — that is, whether it carries institutional authority beyond its analogical force — and, if so, whether the authority of the precedent is being conflated with the analogical strength of the similarity. Coordinate with phil-normativity if the normative dimension requires specialized analysis beyond what the standard analogy framework provides.

6. **Analogies in Mathematical and Formal Reasoning.** Analogical arguments in mathematics — where the source and target are both formal structures (e.g., an analogy between the behavior of integers under addition and the behavior of polynomials under multiplication) — present a special case that requires careful adaptation of the standard framework. In these contexts, the prior-association condition may be satisfied by formal isomorphisms that license inference in ways that have no counterpart in empirical or normative analogies. The analyst should recognize that mathematical analogies operate under different epistemic constraints: the "connecting principle" may be a proof that the two structures share a given formal property, and the "projected property" may be a conjecture that the two structures share a further formal property, with the strength of the analogy depending on the extent of the known isomorphism and the nature of the conjecture. The standard criteria (prior association, disanalogy detection) still apply, but they must be interpreted in terms of formal structure rather than causal or conceptual connection. A key difference is that in mathematical analogies, a "disanalogy" is not a difference in contingent properties but a structural feature present in one domain and absent in the other — and whether this disanalogy is defeating depends on whether the projected property depends on that structural feature. Mathematical analogies also admit of a unique form of confirmation: if the projected property can be proven in the source domain from axioms that are also satisfied by the target domain (under the mapping), then the analogy's conclusion follows deductively. The analyst should flag when a mathematical analogy crosses this threshold from analogical to deductive reasoning. Consult Bartha's (2010) extended treatment of mathematical analogy (Chapters 8–9) for a detailed account of how the articulation model applies in formal contexts.

7. **Analogies Across Ontological Categories.** A special class of analogical arguments maps relations between entities that belong to fundamentally different ontological categories — for example, analogies between physical systems and abstract systems, between minds and machines, or between biological organisms and social institutions. These cross-categorical analogies present distinctive analytical challenges. The mapping of entities (Step 1) is often indeterminate because there is no natural one-to-one correspondence between entities at different ontological levels. The relevance assessment (Step 3) is complicated by the difficulty of establishing a connecting principle that operates uniformly across ontological boundaries — a causal law that governs biological systems may have no straightforward analogue in the social domain, even if the functional description is identical. The analyst should flag cross-categorical analogies as requiring special care in the mapping and relevance-assessment steps. In particular, the analyst should be alert to the risk of *ontological projection* — the inadvertent importation of features from the source domain's ontological category into the target domain, where those features may not apply. For example, an analogy between computation and cognition that maps algorithms to mental processes must be careful not to smuggle in assumptions about the discreteness, seriality, or determinacy of mental processes that are features of the computational source rather than established facts about the cognitive target. When analyzing cross-categorical analogies, coordinate with phil-ontological-commitment to identify the ontological categories involved and to assess whether the cross-categorical mapping respects the modal and mereological constraints appropriate to each category.

## 9. ARS Interface

The phil-analogy-check skill integrates with the Academic Research System (ARS) through the interface points described below. This integration ensures that analogy analyses are reproducible, citable, cacheable, and chainable within larger analytical workflows.

**Invocation.** This skill is invoked as part of the `/phil:evaluate` command or directly via the Skill tool. It expects, as its primary input, an argument reconstruction produced by `phil-argument-reconstruct`. Optional inputs include: source text for contextual enrichment; a `phil-conceptual-analysis` report when the analogy bears on a contested concept; a `phil-dialectical-map` for contextualizing the analogy within a broader debate; and a pre-existing set of counter-analogies to be evaluated alongside those generated in Step 5. A `--depth` flag controls the thoroughness of the analysis (values: `quick`, `standard`, `deep`), which adjusts the exhaustiveness of the mapping and the number of counter-analogies generated.

**ARS-3W protocol integration.** Upon completion, the analysis report is automatically registered with the ARS-3W (What, Why, What-If) tracking system. The "What" is the analogical argument under analysis, identified by its source–target structure and projected property. The "Why" is the set of evaluative judgments rendered in the report: the strength grade, the relevance assessment (including the type and strength of the connecting principle), the disanalogy impact analysis, and the fallacy determination. The "What-If" is the array of dialectical recommendations, including the counter-analogies generated in Step 5, the proposed revisions for the proponent, and the recommended lines of attack for the respondent. This 3W registration enables downstream tools to query the analysis by argument, to compare analyses of the same argument performed at different times or by different analysts, and to track how the assessment of an analogy evolves as the surrounding dialectic develops.

**Citation resolution and bibliographic integrity.** When the analyst cites canonical sources from the methodological foundations (Section 4) — whether to justify a classification using Hesse's taxonomy, to apply Bartha's prior-association condition, or to invoke Keynes's variety criterion — the ARS citation-check module (ars-citation-check) can be invoked to verify the accuracy of citations and to retrieve full bibliographic metadata in the configured citation style. This is especially important when the analysis grounds a strength judgment in a specific passage from one of the canonical sources, because the accuracy of the citation directly bears on the auditability of the analysis. The report includes a references section (not shown in the output specification above but appended automatically) listing all cited works with full bibliographic data, and ARS can cross-reference these against its internal citation database to flag discrepancies.

**Cache and session management.** Completed analogy analysis reports are cached within the ARS session using the ars-cache-invalidate and ars-mark-read protocol. When this skill is invoked, ARS checks the session cache for an existing analysis of the same reconstructed argument. If a cached report is found, the analyst is prompted with three options: (a) reuse the cached report as-is, if the argument reconstruction has not changed and the original analysis is judged to still be sound; (b) supplement the cached report with additional findings — e.g., new disanalogies that have emerged in the literature, new counter-analogies proposed by respondents, or new empirical evidence bearing on the connecting principle — without redoing the entire analysis; or (c) supersede the cached report with a fresh analysis, which may be appropriate if the argument reconstruction has been revised or if the analyst's methodological approach has changed. Cached reports are versioned, so supersession creates a new version while preserving the old one for audit trails.

**Pipeline chaining and automation.** Within the ARS academic pipeline, phil-analogy-check functions as a midstream analysis node. It receives a reconstructed argument from phil-argument-reconstruct (upstream). Depending on the results of the analysis, it may feed its output to one or more downstream skills: phil-counterexample (if a disanalogy meets counterexample criteria), phil-parity-check (if competing analogies need adjudication), phil-fallacy-check (if the argument is determined to be fallacious), phil-distinction-track (if a defeating disanalogy requires precise articulation), or phil-normativity (if the analogy has a normative dimension requiring specialized analysis). The pipeline scheduler can be configured to automatically invoke phil-analogy-check whenever an argument is classified as analogical by phil-argument-reconstruct, reducing the need for manual workflow orchestration.

**Format conversion and export.** The output report is generated in structured Markdown as its native format. However, ARS provides format conversion (via ars-format-convert) to LaTeX for inclusion in academic manuscripts, to JSON for programmatic consumption by downstream ARS tools and external applications, and to plain text for integration into text-based workflows or for submission to venues with plain-text requirements. The source–target mapping table is emitted as both a rendered Markdown table (for human readers) and a machine-readable YAML block embedded in the Markdown source (for automated extraction and computational analysis). The JSON export includes a structured representation of all correspondences, the disanalogy catalogue, the strength grade, and the dialectical recommendations, enabling programmatic comparison of multiple analogy analyses across a corpus.

**Disclosure and reproducibility metadata.** The report includes a disclosure section, appended automatically below the main report body, recording: the skill version (from the YAML frontmatter), the date and time of analysis, the canonical sources consulted during the analysis, the depth setting used, the identity of any optional inputs supplied, and any interpretive choices or assumptions made by the analyst that could affect the results (e.g., "the analysis assumes interpretation A of the projected property; under interpretation B the strength grade would be 'Moderate' rather than 'Weak'"). This disclosure metadata is consumed by ars-disclosure for transparency auditing and by the reproducibility infrastructure to determine whether a given analysis can be independently replicated. The disclosure section is designed to satisfy the transparency norms of peer review: a reviewer should be able to understand exactly how the analysis was produced and to identify any points at which alternative interpretive choices could yield different results.

**Integration with other ARS modules.** Beyond the specific interface points described above, phil-analogy-check is designed to interoperate with the broader ARS ecosystem. Analysis reports can be tagged with domain-specific metadata (e.g., "ethics," "philosophy of mind," "philosophy of science") to enable domain-filtered search and comparative analysis across analogical arguments within a single philosophical subfield. The skill also supports batch processing via the ars-pipeline scheduler, allowing a corpus of argument reconstructions to be automatically screened for analogical arguments and analyzed in sequence — useful for systematic literature surveys or for mapping the analogical structure of an entire philosophical debate.

**Limitations of automated analysis.** While this skill provides a rigorous, theoretically grounded framework for evaluating analogical arguments, the analyst should be aware of its limitations. The framework cannot fully automate the judgment of relevance — the determination that a connecting principle links shared properties to the projected property requires substantive domain knowledge and philosophical judgment that resists algorithmic formalization. The framework also cannot anticipate every possible disanalogy or counter-analogy; its value lies in structuring the search for these defeating conditions, not in guaranteeing their exhaustive discovery. Finally, the strength-grade scale, while calibrated, admits of borderline cases where reasonable analysts applying the same framework may disagree. These limitations are not defects of the skill but reflections of the inherent complexity of analogical reasoning — a form of inference that, as Mill recognized, is at once indispensable and irreducibly judgment-dependent. The skill's primary contribution is to make that judgment explicit, structured, and defensible, not to eliminate it.
