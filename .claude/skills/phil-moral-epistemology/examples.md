## Example 1: Moral Intuitionism and the Perceptual Analogy (Huemer, Audi)

### Input

> We know that torturing innocent children for fun is wrong. How do we know this? Not through deductive argument or empirical observation — we simply "see" it. This intellectual seeming is relevantly analogous to perceptual experience. Just as it is reasonable to believe that there is a table in front of you when you have a perceptual experience as of a table, it is reasonable to believe that torturing children is wrong when you have an intellectual seeming that it is wrong. In both cases, the belief is non-inferentially justified. Just as perceptual justification is defeasible — it can be overridden by evidence that you are hallucinating — moral intuition is also defeasible: it can be overridden by evidence that your intuition is distorted by bias, self-interest, or cultural conditioning. But absent such defeaters, the default rational attitude is to trust your intuitions. And since nearly everyone shares the intuition that torturing innocent children for fun is wrong, and there is no plausible defeater for this intuition, we are justified in believing it.

This passage presents an intuitionist epistemology with a perceptual analogy. It makes claims about the nature, justification, and reliability of moral intuition. The epistemological analysis follows.

### Output

```
## Moral Epistemology Analysis Report

### Basic Information
- **Analysis ID**: MEEX001
- **Justification Pathway**: Intuitionist (Moderate — Audi-style)
- **Source**: Composite intuitionist argument (Huemer/Audi synthesis)
- **Related Analysis**: Argument-reconstruction report required

### Justification Pathway Classification

**Primary Pathway: Intuitionist — Moderate Intuitionism (Audi)**

The argument treats at least some moral beliefs as non-inferentially justified through intellectual seeming. The analogy with perception is central: moral intuitions are intellectual seemings that provide prima facie justification without requiring inference. Key sub-type indicators:
- *Defeasibility:* The argument explicitly acknowledges that moral intuitions can be defeated (bias, self-interest, cultural conditioning), which distinguishes it from classical self-evidence intuitionism (Moore) and places it firmly in the moderate intuitionist camp (Audi).
- *No special faculty:* The argument does not posit a sui generis faculty of moral intuition; it appeals to "intellectual seeming" — the same faculty that delivers mathematical and logical intuitions.
- *Fallibilism:* The argument does not claim that moral intuitions are infallible or indubitable; it claims only that they provide default justification absent defeaters.

**Sub-type justification:** The perceptual analogy, the emphasis on defeasibility, and the appeal to intersubjective convergence all point to Audi's moderate intuitionism rather than Moore's classical intuitionism or Ross's pluralistic intuitionism.

### Epistemic Access Mechanism

**Mechanism:** Rational intuition / intellectual seeming — a non-inferential cognitive state in which a proposition appears true to the thinker upon reflection, with a distinctive "seeming" phenomenology.

**Phenomenological character:**
- Immediate: the wrongness of torturing children "just seems" true, without inferential mediation.
- Intellectual rather than sensory: the seeming is not perceptual or emotional but cognitive — it is an "its seeming to one that p" in the domain of moral truth.
- Compelling: the seeming carries a sense of obviousness that makes disbelief psychologically difficult.

**Scope:** The argument claims that basic moral propositions (e.g., "torturing innocent children for fun is wrong") are known through intuition. It does not claim that all moral truths are known through intuition — complex moral judgments may require inference from basic intuitions plus empirical premises.

**Reliability conditions:**
- The intuition must not be subject to a defeater (bias, self-interest, cultural conditioning, emotional distortion).
- Intersubjective convergence provides corroboration (nearly everyone shares the intuition).
- The argument claims these conditions are met for the specific case.

**Reliability assessment:** The reliability of moral intuition is contested. Empirical evidence from moral psychology suggests that moral intuitions are influenced by factors (framing effects, order effects, emotional salience) that are not truth-tracking. However, the argument's moderate intuitionism already accommodates this by treating intuitions as defeasible. The key question is whether the defeaters that affect some moral intuitions (e.g., those involving ingroup/outgroup distinctions) also affect the specific intuition at issue (the wrongness of torturing children). The argument's appeal to intersubjective convergence provides some support for reliability in this case, but convergence alone does not establish truth-tracking — convergence could be explained by shared evolutionary or cultural factors.

### Skeptical Challenge Assessment

**Evolutionary Debunking: MODERATE VULNERABILITY**

The evolutionary debunking challenge targets the reliability of moral intuition: if our moral intuitions were shaped by evolutionary pressures that favored fitness-enhancing beliefs rather than truth-tracking beliefs, then the fact that a moral proposition seems intuitively true provides no reason to think it is true. This is an undercutting defeater for intuition-based justification.

For the specific intuition that torturing innocent children is wrong, the evolutionary debunking challenge has some force: evolutionary pressures plausibly favored tendencies to care about the welfare of conspecifics (especially the young), which could explain the intuition's content without reference to moral truth. However, the argument can respond:
- The intuition's intersubjective convergence and apparent obviousness do not, by themselves, establish truth-tracking — but they raise the bar for debunking. The debunker must show not merely that evolution could explain the intuition but that it does explain it and that the truth-tracking hypothesis is less plausible.
- The third-factor response may be available: if survival and harm-avoidance are genuinely valuable, then evolution may have favored truth-tracking intuitions about these values.

**Disagreement-Based Skepticism: LOW VULNERABILITY**

The argument appeals to near-universal convergence on the specific intuition (torturing innocent children for fun is wrong). If this convergence claim is empirically accurate, disagreement-based skepticism does not undermine this particular intuition. However, widespread moral disagreement on other issues (abortion, capital punishment, distributive justice) may undermine the general reliability of moral intuition — if intuition delivers incompatible outputs on many issues, we should be cautious about trusting intuition even in apparently convergent cases.

**Causal Skepticism: LOW-TO-MODERATE VULNERABILITY**

The causal origins of the specific intuition (socialization, emotional development, evolution) may be non-truth-tracking. The argument's defeasibility clause partially addresses this — it acknowledges that cultural conditioning can defeat intuitions — but the question is whether the actual causal origins of this intuition involve truth-tracking factors. If the intuition is primarily shaped by emotional responses to suffering (which are themselves evolutionarily selected for social bonding rather than truth-tracking), the causal skeptic has a point.

**Agrippan Trilemma: MODERATE VULNERABILITY**

The argument is foundationalist: basic moral intuitions are stopping points that are non-inferentially justified. The foundationalist horn of the Agrippan trilemma requires that the stopping points be genuinely self-justified, not arbitrary. The argument's perceptual analogy is meant to address this: just as perceptual seemings provide non-inferential justification (we do not demand a further argument for believing our eyes), intellectual seemings provide non-inferential justification. However, the analogy may be challenged: perceptual seemings are connected to a causal mechanism (light hitting the retina) that is known to be generally reliable, whereas moral seemings have no analogous mechanism — or if they do (evolution), that mechanism is not known to be truth-tracking.

### Evolutionary Debunking Vulnerability

**Street's Darwinian Dilemma applied:**
- Horn 1 (no tracking): The intuition that torturing children is wrong could be explained entirely by evolutionary pressures favoring care for conspecifics. If this explanation is correct and no tracking relation holds, the intuition does not provide justification.
- Horn 2 (tracking): If the realist claims that evolution favored truth-tracking moral capacities, they must explain how and why. The answer — that survival and harm-avoidance are genuinely valuable — is the third-factor response.
- The argument's moderate intuitionism, with its emphasis on defeasibility, is actually well-positioned to absorb the evolutionary debunking challenge: the argument does not claim that moral intuitions are infallible or immune to evolutionary shaping. It claims only that, absent specific defeaters, intuitions provide prima facie justification. The evolutionary debunking challenge is itself a candidate defeater that must be assessed case by case.

**Third-factor viability for this argument:** HIGH. The intuition that torturing innocent children is wrong concerns harm-avoidance, which is plausibly linked to survival and well-being — states that are genuinely valuable independent of our attitudes toward them. If survival and well-being are genuinely good, and evolution favored intuitions that track what promotes survival and well-being, then the intuition that harming innocents is wrong may track moral truth after all.

### Reflective Equilibrium Structure

This argument does not primarily employ reflective equilibrium. It is foundationalist (basic intuitions provide non-inferential justification) rather than coherentist (justification depends on coherence relations). However, the argument's defeasibility clause implicitly acknowledges a role for coherence: intuitions can be overridden by countervailing considerations, which is a coherentist element within a broadly foundationalist framework. This is a hybrid structure: intuitionist foundationalism with a coherentist superstructure for non-basic beliefs.

### Overall Epistemic Assessment

**Rating: JUSTIFIED BELIEF IS POSSIBLE**

The argument successfully establishes that the specific intuition (torturing innocent children for fun is wrong) provides prima facie justification for believing the corresponding moral proposition. The moderate intuitionist framework, with its emphasis on defeasibility and the absence of specific defeaters, is well-designed to handle many skeptical challenges. However, knowledge-level warrant is not fully achieved, because:
- The evolutionary debunking challenge raises a general doubt about the reliability of moral intuition that has not been decisively answered.
- The perceptual analogy has limitations (no known truth-tracking mechanism for moral intuition comparable to the visual system's known reliability).
- The argument's appeal to intersubjective convergence does not fully address the truth-tracking question.

**Strongest element:** The defeasibility structure, which allows the argument to absorb skeptical challenges without claiming infallibility.
**Weakest element:** The perceptual analogy, which relies on a contested analogy between sensory perception and intellectual seeming.
```

## Example 2: Evolutionary Debunking (Street) — Darwinian Dilemma for Realism

### Input

> Realist theories of value hold that there are evaluative facts that hold independently of our attitudes. But consider the evolutionary origins of our evaluative attitudes. The best explanation of why we have the evaluative attitudes we do — why we value survival, cooperation, harm-avoidance, and so on — is that these attitudes enhanced our ancestors' reproductive fitness. Natural selection favored organisms that cared about their own survival and the welfare of their kin, not organisms that accurately detected the evaluative truth. So the realist faces a dilemma. Either there is no relation between the evolutionary forces that shaped our evaluative attitudes and the evaluative truth — in which case it would be a massive coincidence if our evaluative attitudes happened to track the truth, and we have no reason to trust them. Or there is a relation — in which case the realist must explain how natural selection favored truth-tracking evaluative capacities. But this explanation is not forthcoming: evolutionary biology explains our evaluative attitudes in terms of fitness enhancement, not truth detection. Realism thus leads to skepticism about evaluative knowledge; the only way to avoid skepticism is to reject realism.

This is Street's Darwinian dilemma argument against moral realism. The epistemological analysis focuses on the skeptical force of the evolutionary explanation and its implications for the possibility of moral knowledge.

### Output

```
## Moral Epistemology Analysis Report

### Basic Information
- **Analysis ID**: MEEX002
- **Justification Pathway**: Not applicable — this is a debunking argument that challenges the possibility of moral knowledge under realism, not an argument that itself makes positive epistemological claims about a justification pathway.
- **Source**: Sharon Street, "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value" (2006)
- **Argument Type**: Dilemma-based skeptical challenge

### Justification Pathway Classification

**Not a positive epistemology.** This argument does not propose a justification pathway for moral knowledge. It is a skeptical argument: it claims that if realism is true, then moral knowledge is impossible (or radically unlikely). The argument's epistemological import is negative — it challenges the possibility of moral knowledge on realist assumptions, not by identifying a specific flaw in a specific justification pathway but by showing that any justification pathway faces a generic undercutting defeater.

The argument's structure is: (1) Under realism, our evaluative attitudes are explained by evolutionary forces that do not track evaluative truth. (2) Therefore, under realism, our evaluative attitudes are not justified. (3) Therefore, realism leads to skepticism. (4) Since skepticism is unacceptable (or since we do have evaluative knowledge), realism should be rejected.

**Epistemic status of the argument's own claims:** The argument makes empirical claims about evolutionary psychology (what the "best explanation" of our evaluative attitudes is) and philosophical claims about justification (the tracking requirement). The epistemological analysis must assess the justification of these claims as well.

### Epistemic Access Mechanism

**The argument's target:** The argument targets ALL realist justification pathways. It does not single out intuitionism, coherentism, or reliabilism — it claims that any realist justification pathway is undermined because the evolutionary origins of our evaluative attitudes provide an undercutting defeater for moral beliefs irrespective of the specific justification structure.

**The debunker's own epistemic mechanism:** The argument's central empirical premise — that evolutionary forces shaped our evaluative attitudes without tracking evaluative truth — is itself justified by empirical evidence from evolutionary biology and moral psychology. The argument also relies on a philosophical premise about justification: that beliefs are unjustified if the best explanation of why we hold them does not involve their truth. This premise is contested in epistemology (e.g., by proponents of causal accounts of knowledge, or by those who distinguish the context of discovery from the context of justification).

### Skeptical Challenge Assessment

**The argument IS a skeptical challenge.** Rather than evaluating skeptical challenges against the argument, we evaluate the argument's skeptical force.

*Force of the Darwinian dilemma:*
- Horn 1 (no tracking): The claim that, absent a tracking relation, our evaluative beliefs would be unjustified even if true. This is an undercutting defeater: the problem is not that the beliefs are false but that they are not appropriately connected to the truth. This horn is powerful because it does not require showing that our evaluative beliefs are false — merely that their causal origins are insensitive to their truth.
- Horn 2 (tracking): The claim that the realist cannot provide a plausible account of how natural selection tracked evaluative truth. This horn depends on how demanding the tracking requirement is. If the realist only needs to show that truth-tracking and fitness-enhancement are not in conflict (rather than that fitness-enhancement selected for truth-tracking), this horn is weaker.

*Dialectical force:* The Darwinian dilemma is most damaging to non-naturalist realism, which posits sui generis moral properties that are not causally efficacious in the natural world. It is harder to see how natural selection could track such properties. The dilemma is less damaging to naturalist realism, which identifies moral properties with natural properties — survival and harm-avoidance are natural properties, and natural selection could plausibly track them.

### Evolutionary Debunking Vulnerability

**Evaluation of the argument's own vulnerability:** The debunking argument itself makes claims about evolutionary psychology. Is it vulnerable to a meta-debunking challenge? Our belief in evolution and our belief that evolutionary forces explain our evaluative attitudes are themselves products of cognitive processes shaped by evolution. If evolutionary debunking undermines all beliefs with evolutionary origins, it undermines itself. Street's response: the debunking argument targets evaluative beliefs specifically — beliefs about what is valuable — not factual beliefs (like beliefs about evolution). The asymmetry claim is that evolutionary pressures directly shape evaluative attitudes (organisms that value survival outcompete those that do not) but not factual beliefs about the world (organisms with accurate perceptual systems outcompete those without them). This asymmetry is crucial to the argument's coherence.

**Third-factor viability against Street's argument:** The third-factor response is the primary realist reply to Street. The question is whether the third-factor response successfully defuses the dilemma rather than merely relocating it.

### Reflective Equilibrium Structure

Not applicable. Street's argument is a skeptical challenge, not a constructive epistemological proposal. It does not employ reflective equilibrium methodology.

### Overall Epistemic Assessment

**Dialectical status: SIGNIFICANTLY FAVORS SKEPTICISM under non-naturalist realism; MODESTLY FAVORS SKEPTICISM under naturalist realism.**

The Darwinian dilemma is a powerful skeptical argument against non-naturalist moral realism. The non-naturalist's commitment to sui generis, causally inert moral properties makes the tracking relation especially mysterious. Naturalist realists have more resources to respond (moral properties are natural properties, and natural selection can track natural properties), but they still face the challenge of explaining why natural selection would favor accurate moral cognition specifically (as opposed to merely fitness-enhancing cognition that happens to coincide with moral truth).

The third-factor response partially defuses the dilemma for naturalist realism but does not fully eliminate the skeptical worry: even if a third factor explains the correlation between evolved attitudes and moral truth, the correlation may be imperfect (some evolved attitudes are not truth-tracking) and the believer has no way to distinguish truth-tracking from non-truth-tracking attitudes without independent moral knowledge.
```

## Example 3: Coherentism and Reflective Equilibrium in Moral Justification

### Input

> We do not arrive at moral knowledge by deducing conclusions from self-evident first principles. Nor do we simply "see" moral truths through a faculty of intuition, independent of all other beliefs. Rather, moral justification is a matter of bringing our moral judgments into reflective equilibrium. We start with our considered moral judgments about particular cases — judgments we make under conditions favorable to good reasoning: when we are calm, informed, and not swayed by self-interest. We then formulate general moral principles that would explain and systematize these judgments. When a principle conflicts with a firmly held particular judgment, we must decide which to revise — sometimes we revise the principle, sometimes the judgment. We work back and forth, adjusting principles and judgments until we reach a coherent set in which principles and particular judgments mutually support each other. This is wide reflective equilibrium, not narrow, because we also bring to bear relevant background theories: what we know about human psychology, about the conditions of human flourishing, about the nature of practical reason. A set of moral beliefs in wide reflective equilibrium is justified — not because it rests on foundations but because it is the set of beliefs we would hold after ideal deliberation.

This is a coherentist epistemology centered on Rawlsian reflective equilibrium. The analysis focuses on the coherence criterion, the independence constraint, and the isolation objection.

### Output

```
## Moral Epistemology Analysis Report

### Basic Information
- **Analysis ID**: MEEX003
- **Justification Pathway**: Coherentist — Wide Reflective Equilibrium (Rawls)
- **Source**: Composite reflective equilibrium argument (Rawls/Daniels synthesis)
- **Related Analysis**: Argument-reconstruction report required

### Justification Pathway Classification

**Primary Pathway: Coherentist — Wide Reflective Equilibrium**

The argument explicitly denies both foundationalism (no self-evident first principles) and pure intuitionism (no isolated moral "seeing"). Justification is holistic: a moral belief is justified in virtue of its coherence with the entire system of moral and non-moral beliefs. Sub-type indicators:
- *Wide, not narrow:* The argument explicitly incorporates background theories (psychology, human flourishing, practical reason), which distinguishes wide reflective equilibrium from narrow reflective equilibrium (which considers only moral judgments and principles).
- *No foundational stopping points:* Considered judgments are "starting points" but not foundations — they are fully revisable in light of principles and background theories.
- *Ideal deliberation model:* Justification is defined counterfactually — justified beliefs are those that would survive ideal deliberation, not those that are currently held.

**Key difference from intuitionism:** Both intuitionists and coherentists appeal to "intuitions" (considered judgments), but they assign different epistemic roles. The intuitionist treats some intuitions as non-inferentially justified foundations. The coherentist treats all intuitions as revisable inputs to a coherence-generating process — no intuition is epistemically prior or immune from revision. The present argument is clearly coherentist: "sometimes we revise the principle, sometimes the judgment" — the considered judgment has no privileged epistemic status.

### Epistemic Access Mechanism

**Mechanism:** The process of reflective equilibrium — iterative adjustment of moral judgments and principles until coherence is achieved, informed by background theories.

**Phenomenological character:**
- Deliberative and inferential: unlike intuitionism, justification is not immediate but is achieved through a process of reasoning.
- Dialectical: the process involves moving back and forth between particular judgments and general principles.
- Holistic: the justification of any single belief depends on its relations to the entire system.

**Scope:** The mechanism potentially delivers justification for the full range of moral beliefs, from particular case judgments to abstract principles. However, the output depends entirely on the inputs (the initial considered judgments) and the coherence-generating process.

**Reliability conditions:**
- The initial considered judgments must be made under favorable conditions (calm, informed, not self-interested).
- The background theories must be independently well-supported (psychology, biology, etc.).
- The coherence-generating process must constrain the output — it must not be the case that any set of moral beliefs can be made coherent given sufficient adjustments.
- The reflective equilibrium must be stable — it should not be the case that further deliberation would upset it.

**Reliability assessment: MODERATE.** Wide reflective equilibrium is the dominant methodology in contemporary moral and political philosophy, which provides some institutional support for its reliability. However, the reliability of coherentist justification in general is contested. The central worry is the isolation objection: if coherence is the sole criterion of justification, a coherent but systematically false set of moral beliefs — a moral "fairy tale" — would count as justified. The argument attempts to address this by incorporating background theories (wide reflective equilibrium), which provide an external constraint: moral beliefs must cohere not only with each other but with independently supported factual claims about human psychology, biology, and social organization. This reduces (but does not eliminate) the threat of isolation.

### Skeptical Challenge Assessment

**Evolutionary Debunking: MODERATE VULNERABILITY**

The coherentist faces a distinctive version of the evolutionary debunking challenge. If the initial considered judgments are themselves products of evolutionary pressures that did not track moral truth, then the entire coherentist edifice rests on a distorted input base. Coherence can amplify or systematize distortion; it cannot correct it if the distortion is systematic and the coherence process does not include external truth-tracking constraints. The wide reflective equilibrium partially addresses this by incorporating background theories — but these background theories include empirical claims about human psychology that may themselves reveal the evolutionary origins of our moral intuitions. If the background theories tell us that our considered judgments are unreliable (because evolutionarily shaped for fitness, not truth), then wide reflective equilibrium may undermine itself.

**Disagreement-Based Skepticism: HIGH VULNERABILITY**

The coherentist methodology is especially vulnerable to disagreement-based skepticism. If different deliberators, starting from different considered judgments (shaped by different cultural backgrounds, moral traditions, or life experiences), arrive at different reflective equilibria, then reflective equilibrium does not uniquely determine a single justified moral system. The argument does not address this — it assumes that all reasonable deliberators would converge on the same reflective equilibrium, which is empirically questionable given the persistence of deep moral disagreement among reasonable, informed people.

**Causal Skepticism: MODERATE VULNERABILITY**

The causal origins of considered judgments are relevant to the coherentist. If the considered judgments are caused by factors (culture, upbringing, emotion) that are not truth-tracking, the coherentist cannot appeal to coherence to solve the problem — coherence among non-truth-tracking judgments does not make them truth-tracking. Wide reflective equilibrium helps by incorporating background theories that may reveal and correct for distorting causal influences, but the effectiveness of this correction depends on the quality of the background theories.

**Agrippan Trilemma: MODERATE VULNERABILITY**

The argument occupies the coherentist horn of the Agrippan trilemma. The standard objection to coherentism is that coherence is circular — each belief is justified by its relations to other beliefs, which are justified by their relations to still other beliefs, eventually circling back to the original belief. The argument's response is implicit in the reflective equilibrium model: justification is not linear (premise-conclusion chains) but holistic (the entire system is justified as a whole). Whether this response is adequate depends on whether holistic coherence can provide a genuine constraint on truth — the isolation objection above.

### Evolutionary Debunking Vulnerability

**Special considerations for coherentism:** The coherentist has a distinctive response to evolutionary debunking that the foundationalist lacks. The coherentist can acknowledge that our initial considered judgments are shaped by evolution and may not individually track moral truth — but claim that the process of reflective equilibrium, especially wide reflective equilibrium that incorporates empirical knowledge about human evolution and psychology, can correct for evolutionary distortion. The idea is that we can "debias" our moral judgments by understanding their evolutionary origins and adjusting accordingly. (Compare: we can correct for perceptual illusions by understanding the visual system's heuristics.) This response is promising but faces the challenge that the debiasing itself requires moral judgments (to determine which evolved intuitions to trust and which to distrust), and these meta-level moral judgments may themselves be evolutionarily shaped.

**Third-factor viability for this argument:** The third-factor response is compatible with coherentism but does not protect it from the specific concerns above. Even if a third factor establishes a correlation between evolved attitudes and moral truth, the coherentist still needs to show that the coherence process amplifies truth-tracking rather than merely systematizing a set of inputs that track truth imperfectly and unsystematically.

### Reflective Equilibrium Structure

**Type:** Wide reflective equilibrium (explicit incorporation of background theories from psychology, biology, and practical reason).

**Independence constraint:** The argument's considered judgments (particular case judgments) are the inputs to the equilibrium process. The independence constraint requires that these inputs be independent of the principles they are used to justify — otherwise the process is circular (principles are "derived" from judgments that already presuppose the principles). The argument does not explicitly address this constraint. The risk is that considered judgments about particular cases (e.g., "this act of lying is wrong") are already theoretically laden — they reflect implicit commitments to general principles (e.g., a principle against deception). If so, the reflective equilibrium process may merely rationalize pre-existing theoretical commitments rather than genuinely testing principles against independent data.

**Isolation objection:** Wide reflective equilibrium partially addresses the isolation objection by incorporating external constraints (background theories), but the background theories are themselves revisable within the equilibrium, which limits their constraining power. If a background theory conflicts with a firmly held moral judgment, the theory may be revised rather than the judgment — which means the moral judgments are not genuinely answerable to empirical evidence.

### Overall Epistemic Assessment

**Rating: JUSTIFIED BELIEF IS POSSIBLE (with qualifications)**

Wide reflective equilibrium is a defensible coherentist epistemology for moral beliefs. It avoids the most serious objections to narrow reflective equilibrium (isolation, lack of external constraint) by incorporating background theories. It also has the advantage of accurately describing how moral reasoning actually proceeds — philosophers do work back and forth between judgments and principles, adjusting both. However, the independence constraint and the disagreement challenge remain serious problems. The method may deliver justification for moral beliefs for those who share a common set of considered judgments, but it does not resolve deep moral disagreements among those with different starting points.

**Strongest element:** The incorporation of background theories provides genuine external constraint and distinguishes wide reflective equilibrium from mere coherence.
**Weakest element:** The assumption of convergence — that all reasonable deliberators starting from considered judgments would arrive at the same reflective equilibrium — is empirically and philosophically questionable.
```

## Example 4: Third-Factor Responses to Debunking (Enoch, Skarsaune)

### Input

> The evolutionary debunker claims that our moral beliefs are explained by natural selection, which favors fitness-enhancing beliefs rather than truth-tracking ones. So if our moral beliefs are true, it is a massive coincidence — and this undermines their justification. But consider: survival is good. That is not just something we happen to believe — it is a moral truth. Natural selection favored organisms that pursue survival and avoid death. So natural selection favored organisms that pursue something that is, in fact, objectively good. This is the third factor: the goodness of survival explains both (a) why natural selection favored survival-pursuing organisms and (b) why those organisms' evaluative attitudes tend to track the truth about what is good. The coincidence is not inexplicable — it is explained by the value of survival itself. The debunker's premise that natural selection does not track evaluative truth is therefore false. Evolution favors the pursuit of what is in fact valuable.

This is a third-factor response to evolutionary debunking, associated with David Enoch. The epistemological analysis focuses on whether the third factor genuinely defuses the debunking challenge.

### Output

```
## Moral Epistemology Analysis Report

### Basic Information
- **Analysis ID**: MEEX004
- **Justification Pathway**: Reliabilist (implicit) — the third-factor response functions as a reliabilist defense: evolution produced a mechanism (survival-pursuit) that reliably tracks a subset of evaluative truths (the goodness of survival).
- **Source**: Composite third-factor argument (Enoch 2010 / Skarsaune 2011 synthesis)
- **Related Analysis**: Argument-reconstruction report required; evolutionary debunking analysis (MEEX002) provides the challenge context.

### Justification Pathway Classification

**Primary Pathway: Reliabilist (Third-Factor Reliabilism)**

The argument does not explicitly classify itself as reliabilist, but its structure is reliabilist: it claims that our evaluative attitudes are justified because they are produced by a process (natural selection for survival-pursuit) that tracks evaluative truth (the goodness of survival). Justification is a matter of reliable production, not of accessibility to the believer — the believer need not know about the third factor for their beliefs to be justified.

**Sub-type justification:** The argument is most naturally classified as process reliabilism — the relevant process is the evolutionary selection of survival-oriented evaluative tendencies, and the claim is that this process is generally reliable with respect to a certain class of evaluative truths (those related to survival and well-being).

**Important note:** The argument does not provide a full epistemology. It responds to a specific skeptical challenge (evolutionary debunking) by showing that a tracking relation can be established. It does not address other skeptical challenges or provide a general account of moral knowledge.

### Epistemic Access Mechanism

**Mechanism:** Evolved evaluative tendencies (survival-pursuit, harm-avoidance, kin-altruism) that were selected for their fitness-enhancing effects but that also track genuine evaluative truths (because survival and well-being are genuinely valuable).

**Phenomenological character:** The mechanism is not accessible to introspection. The believer experiences moral intuitions as compelling seemings; they do not experience the evolutionary origins of those seemings or the tracking relation. This is consistent with reliabilism — justification does not require reflective access to the justifying mechanism.

**Scope:** The third factor covers evaluative attitudes related to survival and well-being (harm-avoidance, care for kin, cooperation). It does not obviously cover all evaluative attitudes — for example, attitudes about fairness to strangers, about abstract principles of justice, or about moral desert may not be directly linked to survival value. The argument's scope is limited: it establishes reliability for a core subset of evaluative attitudes but not for the full range of moral beliefs.

**Reliability conditions:**
- The third factor must genuinely establish a correlation between evolved attitudes and moral truth.
- The correlation must be sufficiently strong and systematic — it cannot be merely occasional or accidental.
- The moral truths in question must be naturalistically respectable (survival is good because survival is a natural property of organisms; if the goodness of survival is a non-natural property, the tracking relation is harder to establish).

**Reliability assessment: MODERATE-TO-HIGH for survival-related attitudes; LOW for non-survival-related attitudes.** The third factor plausibly establishes a tracking relation for a core set of evaluative attitudes. However, it does nothing to establish reliability for moral beliefs about, e.g., distributive justice, retributive punishment, or moral status beyond the in-group — all of which are central to human moral cognition and are not obviously linked to individual survival.

### Skeptical Challenge Assessment

**Evolutionary Debunking: PARTIALLY DEFUSED**

The third-factor response challenges the debunker's claim that natural selection does not track evaluative truth. If survival is genuinely good, then natural selection — which favors survival-pursuit — does track (some) evaluative truth. The response is most effective when combined with a naturalist realism on which evaluative properties are natural properties that can enter into causal explanations (survival is good, and survival is a natural property of organisms).

However, the response faces a scope problem: it covers only those evaluative attitudes that directly concern survival and well-being. Many human moral beliefs — about justice, rights, desert, purity, and sacredness — do not obviously reduce to survival value. The debunker can accept the third-factor response for a narrow range of evaluative attitudes while maintaining the debunking challenge for the rest.

The response also faces a strength problem: even if natural selection tracks evaluative truth for survival-related attitudes, the correlation may be weak (natural selection favors survival-pursuit in general, but particular evaluative attitudes may be imperfectly calibrated to genuine value). A weak correlation may not be sufficient for justification in the reliabilist sense.

**Disagreement-Based Skepticism: UNAFFECTED**

The third-factor response does not address disagreement-based skepticism. The fact that evolution may have produced reliable survival-oriented evaluative attitudes does not explain why those attitudes take different forms in different cultures (e.g., different norms about when killing is permitted). If the mechanism is reliable, why does it produce variable outputs?

**Causal Skepticism: PARTIALLY ADDRESSED**

The third-factor response directly addresses causal skepticism by showing that the causal origins of (some) evaluative attitudes are truth-tracking. This transforms the causal skeptic's challenge: instead of showing that causal origins are non-truth-tracking, the skeptic must show that they are insufficiently truth-tracking for justification.

**Agrippan Trilemma: MODERATE VULNERABILITY**

The third-factor response is externalist: justification depends on the reliability of the belief-forming process, not on the believer's ability to articulate the justification. This avoids the regress problem — the believer does not need to know that the process is reliable for their beliefs to be justified. However, externalism faces the "normativity problem" — if the believer has no access to the fact that their beliefs are reliably produced, in what sense are they epistemically justified in holding them? The reliabilist must explain why reliability, independently of accessibility, constitutes epistemic justification.

### Evolutionary Debunking Vulnerability

**Dialectical impact of the third-factor response:** The third-factor response, if successful, does not fully defeat the evolutionary debunking challenge — it limits its scope. The debunker can still maintain that many moral beliefs are not covered by the third factor, or that the correlation established by the third factor is too weak for justification. The debate shifts from "does evolution track moral truth at all?" to "does evolution track moral truth sufficiently for the range of moral beliefs we actually hold?"

**Unresolved tension:** The third-factor response works best for naturalist realism (where evaluative properties are natural properties) and is strained for non-naturalist realism (where evaluative properties are sui generis and not part of the causal order). This creates a tension: the third-factor response, if fully developed, pushes toward naturalism, which may be an unwelcome result for those who are attracted to non-naturalist realism on independent grounds.

### Reflective Equilibrium Structure

Not applicable. The third-factor argument is an externalist, reliabilist response to a skeptical challenge. It does not employ reflective equilibrium methodology.

### Overall Epistemic Assessment

**Rating: JUSTIFIED BELIEF IS POSSIBLE for survival-related moral beliefs; MERELY PERMISSIBLE BELIEF for other moral beliefs.**

The third-factor response provides a plausible reliabilist defense for a limited range of moral beliefs — those directly concerned with survival, harm-avoidance, and basic well-being. It shows how evolution could produce truth-tracking evaluative attitudes for this subset. However, it does not establish justification for the full range of human moral beliefs, and it does not address other skeptical challenges (disagreement, the normativity problem for externalism). The third-factor response is best understood as a partial defense of moral knowledge, not a complete epistemology.

**Strongest element:** The identification of a plausible tracking mechanism for survival-related moral beliefs — this is a genuine advance over the bare claim that moral intuitions are reliable.
**Weakest element:** The scope limitation — the vast majority of interesting moral beliefs (about justice, rights, desert) are not covered by the survival-based third factor.

## Example 5: Moral Testimony Pessimism — Can We Gain Moral Knowledge from Others?

### Input

> Can I come to know that an action is wrong simply by being told so by a reliable moral informant? There is something puzzling about the idea of moral testimony. If I ask someone whether abortion is wrong, and they say yes, and I form the belief that abortion is wrong on the basis of their say-so — have I genuinely come to know that abortion is wrong? Many philosophers think not. Moral knowledge, unlike factual knowledge, seems to require that I "see for myself" — that I grasp the moral reasons that make the action wrong, not merely that I trust someone who has grasped them. If moral testimony cannot transmit moral knowledge, this reveals something important about the nature of moral understanding: moral knowledge essentially involves understanding, and understanding cannot be transmitted by testimony. This might be because moral reasons are essentially first-personal in a way that factual reasons are not. Or it might be because moral knowledge is a kind of practical knowledge — knowledge of how to act — rather than theoretical knowledge of propositions. Either way, the limits of moral testimony pose a challenge to any epistemology that treats moral knowledge on the model of perceptual or testimonial knowledge.

This argument develops moral testimony pessimism — the view that moral knowledge cannot be acquired through testimony. The epistemological analysis assesses the implications for various justification pathways.

### Output

```
## Moral Epistemology Analysis Report

### Basic Information
- **Analysis ID**: MEEX005
- **Justification Pathway**: Rationalist / Understanding-Based (hybrid — the argument does not propose but critiques a specific pathway; its own implicit positive view is that moral knowledge requires first-personal grasp of reasons)
- **Source**: Composite moral testimony argument (Hills, Hopkins, McGrath synthesis)
- **Related Analysis**: Argument-reconstruction report required

### Justification Pathway Classification

**The argument's target: Testimonial models of moral knowledge.** The argument challenges the view that moral knowledge can be acquired through testimony in the way that factual knowledge can. If successful, it eliminates testimony as a source of moral knowledge and puts pressure on any epistemology that cannot accommodate first-personal understanding as a requirement for moral knowledge.

**The argument's implicit positive view: Understanding-based rationalism.** The argument implies that moral knowledge requires "seeing for oneself" — grasping the moral reasons that make an action wrong. This is a form of rationalism: moral knowledge requires understanding the justificatory relations between moral facts and the reasons that support them. Testimony can at best provide information that another person has this understanding; it cannot transmit the understanding itself.

**Sub-type of the implicit positive view:** This is akin to a Kantian rationalist view on which moral knowledge requires the exercise of practical reason. It is not intuitionist (the grasping is inferential — one sees the reasons, not just the conclusion) and not purely coherentist (understanding is not merely coherence — it involves grasping explanatory relations).

### Epistemic Access Mechanism

**Mechanism (implicit in the argument):** First-personal rational grasp — the agent must themselves appreciate the moral reasons that bear on the action, understand how those reasons support the moral judgment, and recognize the normative force of those reasons.

**Phenomenological character:**
- Active rather than passive: understanding is something the agent achieves, not something that happens to them.
- Inferential: understanding involves seeing the relationship between reasons and conclusions.
- First-personal: the understanding is essentially indexed to the agent's own rational capacities.

**Scope:** The argument claims this requirement applies to all moral knowledge, but it is most plausible for non-trivial moral judgments (e.g., judgments about the permissibility of abortion, the justice of distributive schemes) and least plausible for basic moral judgments (e.g., that torturing children is wrong — a child can know this without being able to articulate the reasons).

**Reliability conditions:** The mechanism is reliable when the agent's rational capacities are functioning properly, when the agent has access to relevant moral reasons, and when the agent is not subject to distorting influences (bias, self-interest, emotional interference). These conditions are demanding and may not be met in many actual cases of moral judgment.

**Reliability assessment: UNCERTAIN.** The understanding-based model has the advantage of connecting moral knowledge to moral agency — moral knowledge is something the agent actively achieves through the exercise of reason. However, the model faces the challenge that many people seem to have moral knowledge without being able to articulate the reasons (moral "expertise" may operate below the level of explicit reasoning). The model also raises the bar for moral knowledge very high — if understanding requires grasping the full structure of reasons, few people may qualify as having moral knowledge about any non-trivial issue.

**Evolutionary Debunking: UNAFFECTED BY THE TESTIMONY ARGUMENT; POTENTIALLY AGGRAVATED**

The testimony pessimism argument does not engage with evolutionary debunking. However, if moral knowledge requires first-personal rational grasp, and our rational capacities are themselves products of evolution (and may be shaped for fitness rather than truth), then the evolutionary debunking challenge applies to the rational capacities the understanding-based model requires. This may actually make evolutionary debunking more damaging: it is not just our moral intuitions that are under suspicion, but our very capacity for grasping moral reasons.

**Disagreement-Based Skepticism: POTENTIALLY AGGRAVATED**

If moral knowledge requires first-personal rational grasp, and different rational agents, exercising their rational capacities under favorable conditions, arrive at different moral conclusions, then the disagreement suggests that rational grasp is not reliable. The understanding-based model makes the problem of moral disagreement more acute, not less — it is harder to dismiss disagreeing others as "not seeing the reasons" if they appear to be exercising their rational capacities just as we are.

**Causal Skepticism: MODERATE VULNERABILITY**

The understanding-based model is vulnerable to causal skepticism about rational capacities. If the agent's rational grasp of moral reasons is causally shaped by cultural upbringing, emotional dispositions, and evolved cognitive heuristics, then the "first-personal" character of the grasp may be an illusion — the agent experiences the reasons as compelling, but the compulsion is caused by non-rational factors.

**Agrippan Trilemma: MODERATE VULNERABILITY**

The understanding-based model is rationalist and may be foundationalist or coherentist depending on how it is developed. If the "grasping of reasons" terminates in self-evident moral principles, it is foundationalist. If the understanding is holistic (grasping the entire set of reasons and their relations), it is coherentist. The argument does not specify which, so the Agrippan assessment is indeterminate.

**The testimony argument's relationship to evolutionary debunking:** The two arguments are largely independent but have an interesting interaction. The testimony pessimism argument says: "you cannot get moral knowledge from others." The evolutionary debunking argument says: "your moral beliefs are not justified because of their evolutionary origins." Together, they seem to leave moral knowledge with no source: you cannot get it from others, and you cannot generate it yourself. This suggests a deep tension in moral epistemology that has not been fully resolved.

### Reflective Equilibrium Structure

Not explicitly employed. The understanding-based model is more naturally allied with rationalism than with coherentism. However, the process of "grasping reasons" and seeing how they support conclusions may involve something structurally similar to reflective equilibrium — moving back and forth between particular judgments and general principles to achieve understanding.

### Overall Epistemic Assessment

**Rating: The argument's own claims are PRO TANTO JUSTIFIED but raise the bar for moral knowledge to a level that may be unattainable.**

The testimony pessimism argument identifies an important disanalogy between moral testimony and factual testimony: moral understanding does seem to require a kind of first-personal grasp that factual knowledge does not. However, the argument may prove too much — if moral knowledge requires the kind of understanding the argument describes, very few people would qualify as having moral knowledge, and this may conflict with the common-sense view that people do have moral knowledge about at least some moral matters.

**Strongest element:** The identification of a genuine asymmetry between moral and non-moral testimony — it is puzzling to defer to others on moral matters in a way that is not puzzling for factual matters.
**Weakest element:** The argument may set an unrealistically high standard for moral knowledge that would count against most actual moral beliefs, including the argument's own.
