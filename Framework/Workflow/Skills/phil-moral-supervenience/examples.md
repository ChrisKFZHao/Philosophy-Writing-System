# Moral Supervenience Analysis Examples

> This document demonstrates the practical application of moral supervenience analysis across different types of supervenience claims, explanatory challenges, and realist/expressivist responses. Each example shows the complete input-to-output process.

---

## Example 1: Blackburn's Supervenience Challenge

### Input

> Let two possible worlds be identical in all natural respects — the same distribution of physical particles, the same biological organisms, the same actions performed, the same psychological states. Could these two worlds differ in their moral properties? Could an act of killing be wrong in one world but not wrong in the other, even though the acts are identical in every natural respect? Almost everyone agrees that this is impossible — moral properties supervene on natural properties. But the realist who treats moral properties as distinct, irreducible properties owes us an explanation: why must the moral covary with the natural in this way? No such explanation is forthcoming. The expressivist, by contrast, has a ready explanation: moral judgments are expressions of attitudes, and the function of moral language — to guide action and coordinate practical attitudes — imposes a consistency requirement. We cannot coherently express different moral attitudes toward natural duplicates without undermining the practical point of moral evaluation. Supervenience is thus a conceptual constraint arising from the nature of moral judgment, not a mysterious metaphysical correlation requiring explanation. The failure of realism to explain supervenience, and the success of expressivism in doing so, gives us reason to prefer expressivism.

This is the core Blackburnian supervenience challenge, formulated as an inference to the best explanation. The analysis focuses on specifying the supervenience relation, reconstructing the explanatory challenge, and assessing its dialectical force.

### Output

```
## Moral Supervenience Analysis Report

### Basic Information
- **Analysis ID**: MSEX001
- **Supervenience Relation**: Strong global supervenience of the moral on the natural
- **Argument Type**: Inference to the best explanation (abductive)
- **Source**: Composite Blackburnian argument (Blackburn 1971/1984/1993 synthesis)

### Supervenience Relation Specification

Using Kim's taxonomy, the supervenience relation claimed is:

- **Strength: Strong** — the covariance holds across possible worlds, not merely within worlds. Any two individuals (actions, states of affairs) in any possible worlds that are natural duplicates are also moral duplicates. This is maximally demanding.
- **Scope: Global** — the supervenience base is the entire natural profile of a world. Any possible world naturally identical to the actual world must be morally identical to it.
- **Base properties: All natural properties** — the broadest class. The argument does not restrict the base to physical properties, psychological properties, or any proper subset. This makes the supervenience claim less vulnerable to counterexample (because the base is so rich) but also makes it harder to explain (because the relation is so unconstrained).
- **Supervening properties: All moral properties** — both thin concepts (right, wrong, good, bad) and thick concepts (brave, cruel, generous) are included.
- **Modal force: The argument is ambiguous between metaphysical necessity and conceptual necessity.** Blackburn treats supervenience as a conceptual constraint (it arises from the nature of moral judgment), which suggests conceptual necessity. But the realist can accept the same modal claim and treat it as metaphysical necessity. This ambiguity is central to the dialectic.

**Textual support:** The argument explicitly states "let two possible worlds be identical in all natural respects" and asks whether they could differ morally — the strong, global formulation.

### Blackburn's Challenge Assessment

**Formulation:** The argument is presented as an inference to the best explanation (Formulation 1 in the SKILL.md):

1. Moral properties strongly supervene on natural properties. (Premise — near-consensus)
2. Realism treats moral properties as distinct, irreducible properties. (Premise — characterization of non-naturalist realism)
3. Expressivism treats moral judgments as expressions of attitudes. (Premise — characterization of expressivism)
4. Expressivism explains supervenience as a conceptual constraint on attitudes; realism cannot explain it at all, or can explain it only by positing a brute, unexplained necessity. (Key explanatory premise)
5. Therefore, supervenience favors expressivism over realism. (Abductive conclusion)

**Dialectical force: MODERATE.** The argument's force depends on the comparative plausibility of (4). If expressivism genuinely explains supervenience better than realism, the argument provides a significant (though not necessarily decisive) reason to prefer expressivism. However, the argument is only as strong as the comparative explanatory claim in (4).

**Critical assessment of premise (4):**
- *Does realism fail to explain supervenience?* The non-naturalist realist who treats moral properties as distinct from natural properties cannot explain why the moral must covary with the natural — the necessity is brute. However, the realist can argue: (a) brute necessities are not always objectionable (consider the necessity that nothing is both red and green all over); (b) the realist can explain supervenience through grounding (moral properties are grounded in natural properties) or conceptual analysis (supervenience is analytic).
- *Does expressivism genuinely explain supervenience?* The expressivist says that the consistency requirement on moral attitudes explains supervenience. But this explanation may merely redescribe the phenomenon: saying "we cannot consistently apply different moral predicates to natural duplicates" is not obviously different from saying "moral properties supervene on natural properties" — it is a paraphrase, not an explanation. The expressivist must show that the consistency requirement follows from the nature of moral attitudes in a way that is not question-begging.

**Vulnerability to reductivist responses:** The argument assumes that the realist treats moral properties as distinct from natural properties — i.e., it targets non-naturalist realism. If the realist is a naturalist reductionist (moral properties are identical to natural properties), supervenience is trivially explained by identity. The argument has no force against naturalist realism. The dialectical significance of the argument depends on whether non-naturalist realism is the most plausible form of realism — if it is not, the argument only eliminates a position that realism's defenders were not committed to in the first place.

### Realist Response Evaluation

**Conceptual Truth Response: MODERATELY PROMISING**

If supervenience is an analytic or conceptual truth — it follows from the meaning of moral terms — then realism does not need to posit a mysterious metaphysical correlation. It is part of our concept of wrongness that two acts cannot differ in wrongness without differing in some natural respect. This response is available to the realist and (if successful) neutralizes the explanatory burden: supervenience is explained by semantic facts, not metaphysical ones. However, the response faces the objection that conceptual analysis of moral terms has proven difficult (the failure of the Canberra Plan), and that the supervenience intuition may not be analytic in the relevant sense — it could be a substantive metaphysical truth, not a semantic one.

**Grounding Response: PROMISING**

If moral properties are grounded in natural properties — moral facts obtain in virtue of natural facts — then supervenience follows from grounding: any difference in moral facts requires a difference in grounding facts, which (if the grounding base is natural) entails supervenience. This response has the advantage of explaining the modal force of supervenience (grounding is a necessitation relation) while preserving a distinction between moral and natural properties (grounded properties are not reducible to their grounds). However, the response shifts the explanatory burden from supervenience to grounding: why is the grounding relation itself necessary? And does grounding respect non-naturalism, or does it collapse moral properties into natural ones?

**Reductive Identification Response: TRIVIALLY SUCCESSFUL BUT COSTLY**

If moral properties are identical to natural properties (wrongness = causing unnecessary suffering; goodness = maximizing utility), supervenience is a trivial consequence of identity: if A=B, then A trivially supervenes on B. This response is available only to naturalist realists and requires accepting specific reductive identifications of moral properties — a significant metaphysical commitment that many realists reject.

**Brute Necessity Response: DEFENSIBLE**

The realist can accept that supervenience is a brute necessity and argue that not all necessities require explanation. Some modal facts — logical truths, some metaphysical truths — are brute. The question is whether moral-natural supervenience is relevantly similar to other unexplained necessities, or whether its status as a "cross-categorical" necessity (connecting the normative to the natural) makes it distinctively mysterious. The brute necessity response is not obviously defeated, but it concedes that the realist cannot provide the kind of explanation the expressivist claims to offer.

### Expressivist Response Evaluation

**Explanatory power: MODERATE.** The expressivist explanation has intuitive appeal: moral judgment involves a commitment to consistency in the application of moral predicates, and this commitment generates the supervenience constraint. However, the explanation may be too thin — the realist can also claim that moral judgment involves a commitment to consistency, and this does not distinguish expressivism from realism. The distinctive expressivist claim — that supervenience is nothing over and above this conceptual constraint — requires an argument that has not been fully provided.

**Dreier's triviality challenge: SIGNIFICANT.** What the expressivist explains may be trivial. We are committed to applying any predicate consistently to natural duplicates — this is the principle that like cases should be treated alike, which applies to all predicates, not just moral ones. If expressivism only explains why we treat moral predicates like all other predicates, it does not explain anything distinctively moral. Blackburn's response — that moral consistency is a specific, substantive constraint arising from the action-guiding role of moral language — partially answers this but requires spelling out why moral consistency is more demanding than generic predicate consistency.

**Supervenience of attitudes: UNRESOLVED.** The expressivist must also explain why moral attitudes supervene on natural attitudes — why we cannot have different moral attitudes toward natural duplicates. This may be no easier to explain than the realist's problem of explaining why moral properties supervene on natural properties. If the expressivist faces a parallel explanatory challenge, the comparative advantage is lost.

### Overall Dialectical Status

**Rating: MODESTLY FAVORS ANTI-REALISM against non-naturalist realism; DIALECTICALLY NEUTRAL against naturalist realism.**

The supervenience challenge is a genuine explanatory problem for non-naturalist moral realism. The non-naturalist's commitment to distinct, irreducible moral properties makes the covariance of the moral on the natural mysterious — a cross-categorical modal correlation with no clear explanation. The expressivist can offer an explanation rooted in the nature of moral judgment. However:
- The explanation is at best partial — it faces Dreier's triviality challenge and the supervenience of attitudes problem.
- The realist has responses available (grounding, conceptual truth, brute necessity) that may neutralize or reduce the explanatory burden.
- The challenge has no force against naturalist realism, where supervenience is explained by identity or reduction.
- The challenge is best understood as a consideration that counts against non-naturalist realism to some degree, not as a decisive objection.
```

## Example 2: Dreier's Triviality Objection

### Input

> Blackburn claims that supervenience is a problem for realism and that expressivism can explain it. But every property supervenes on the class of all properties — trivially, since any property supervenes on itself. So the existence of a supervenience relation is not surprising. What the realist needs explained is a specific, non-trivial supervenience relation: that moral properties supervene on a restricted set of natural properties — say, the physical or the descriptive. But is this restricted supervenience necessary? It is a contingent feature of our world that the wrongness of an act is tied to the physical properties of that act, but there are possible worlds where the supervenience base is different — maybe in some worlds, wrongness covaries with the distribution of ectoplasm. If the supervenience relation is contingent rather than necessary, the explanatory burden is reduced. The realist does not need to explain why moral properties must supervene on these particular natural properties; she only needs to explain why they do in fact supervene on them, which may be a much easier task. The supervenience argument against realism thus depends on a strong necessity claim that may not be defensible.

This is Dreier's triviality objection. The analysis assesses whether the triviality challenge defuses the supervenience argument or merely relocates the debate.

### Output

```
## Moral Supervenience Analysis Report

### Basic Information
- **Analysis ID**: MSEX002
- **Supervenience Relation**: Contested — Dreier argues the supervenience claim must be reformulated as restricted and contingent
- **Argument Type**: Objection to the supervenience argument against realism
- **Source**: Composite Dreier argument (Dreier 1992 synthesis)

### Supervenience Relation Specification

Dreier's argument challenges the specification of the supervenience relation used in Blackburn's argument:

- **The original specification (Blackburn):** Strong global supervenience of the moral on the natural — any possible world naturally identical to the actual world is morally identical.
- **Dreier's reformulation:** The interesting question is whether moral properties supervene on a *restricted* set of natural properties — e.g., the physical, the non-normative descriptive. If the base is "all properties" (including moral properties themselves), the supervenience claim is trivial and uninformative.
- **The contingency point:** Even if we specify a restricted base (e.g., the physical), the supervenience relation on that base may be contingent rather than necessary — true in the actual world and nearby worlds, but not in all metaphysically possible worlds. If so, the explanatory burden is reduced: the realist does not need to explain a necessary connection, only a contingent regularity.

**Modal force at issue:** Dreier's challenge turns on the modal force of the supervenience claim. If supervenience is only nomologically necessary (or merely actual), the explanatory burden is much lighter than if it is metaphysically necessary.

### Blackburn's Challenge Assessment

**Impact of Dreier's objection on the challenge:** Dreier's objection does not show that Blackburn's challenge fails; it shows that the challenge must be reformulated with greater precision. The challenge is not: "why does the moral supervene on everything?" (trivial) but "why does the moral supervene on the non-moral (or on a restricted set of non-moral properties) with modal force greater than mere contingency?"

Blackburn can respond:
- The supervenience intuition is modal — we think it is not just actually true but necessarily true that there can be no moral difference without a natural difference. The contingency Horn of Dreier's dilemma conflicts with the intuitive modal force of supervenience.
- Even if supervenience on a restricted base is contingent, the realist still owes an explanation of why the actual world exhibits this regularity — and the expressivist still has a candidate explanation (the practical function of moral judgment imposes consistency constraints that generate the regularity).

**Dialectical effect:** Dreier's objection sharpens the debate rather than ending it. The question becomes: what is the modal force of supervenience on a restricted, non-trivial base? If it is metaphysically necessary, the explanatory burden remains heavy. If it is contingent, the burden is lighter but not eliminated. The anti-realist needs the necessity for the strongest version of the challenge; the realist benefits from weakening the modal claim.

### Realist Response Evaluation

**Dreier's objection AS a realist response:** Dreier's objection itself functions as a partial realist response — it weakens the explanatory burden by questioning the modal force of the supervenience claim. However, this is a defensive move that concedes that if the modal force were stronger, the challenge would be more serious. The realist who deploys Dreier's objection must be willing to accept that moral-natural supervenience is contingent rather than necessary, which may itself be an uncomfortable position (it would mean that there are possible worlds where an act of killing is wrong in one world and not wrong in a naturally identical world — a "queer" possibility that many realists would want to reject).

**The "restricted base" problem:** The realist who accepts Dreier's reformulation must specify which restricted set of natural properties the moral supervenes on. This is a version of the "location problem" for moral properties — the realist must delineate the natural properties that moral properties covary with. This is a significant task that realists have not fully discharged. The expressivist, by contrast, does not need to solve the location problem because she is not committed to there being moral properties with determinate supervenience bases.

### Expressivist Response Evaluation

**Expressivism and Dreier's challenge:** Dreier's objection is primarily aimed at Blackburn's expressivist explanation — Dreier argues that expressivism explains only the trivial supervenience relation, not the interesting one. The expressivist must show that her explanation covers the restricted, non-trivial supervenience claim. This requires the expressivist to explain why moral attitudes specifically must be consistent across natural duplicates, where "natural" is understood in a restricted sense (not merely "non-moral in general"). This is a harder task than Blackburn's original explanation, and it is not clear that expressivism has the resources to discharge it.

### Overall Dialectical Status

**Rating: DIALECTICALLY NEUTRAL — Dreier's objection weakens the anti-realist argument but does not decisively support realism.**

Dreier's triviality objection shows that the supervenience argument must be reformulated with precision — it cannot rely on the bare fact that moral properties supervene on something (which is true of any properties). The reformulated argument must specify a non-trivial supervenience base and defend the modal force of the supervenience claim. This is a genuine constraint on the anti-realist argument, and meeting it requires work. However, the reformulated challenge is still viable: the anti-realist can argue that moral properties supervene on the natural (or the descriptive, or the physical) with metaphysical necessity, and that explaining this necessity is a problem for realism. Dreier's objection shifts the terrain of the debate but does not settle it.
```

## Example 3: Realist Response — Supervenience as Grounding

### Input

> The supervenience challenge assumes that the realist is committed to a "brute" correlation between moral and natural properties. But the realist has a better option: moral properties are *grounded* in natural properties, and grounding explains supervenience. When we say that an act is wrong *because* it causes unnecessary suffering, the "because" expresses a grounding relation — the wrongness of the act is grounded in its natural property of causing unnecessary suffering. If moral properties are grounded in natural properties, then any difference in moral properties requires a difference in the grounding natural properties — which is exactly the supervenience claim. The grounding relation also explains the modal force of supervenience: grounding is a necessitation relation (if A grounds B, then necessarily, if A obtains then B obtains), so the covariance across possible worlds follows from the nature of grounding. The realist does not need to treat supervenience as a brute, unexplained modal fact — it is a consequence of the grounding of moral properties in natural properties. And grounding is not mysterious: we appeal to grounding relations throughout philosophy (the mental is grounded in the physical; the legal is grounded in the social; the semantic is grounded in the intentional). Moral grounding is just another instance of a familiar metaphysical relation.

This is the grounding-based realist response. The analysis assesses whether grounding genuinely explains supervenience without collapsing into reductionism.

### Output

```
## Moral Supervenience Analysis Report

### Basic Information
- **Analysis ID**: MSEX003
- **Supervenience Relation**: Strong global supervenience, explained by grounding
- **Argument Type**: Realist response to the supervenience challenge (explanatory)
- **Source**: Composite grounding response (Fine / Rosen synthesis)

### Supervenience Relation Specification

- **Strength: Strong** — the grounding relation is a necessitation relation, so supervenience holds across possible worlds.
- **Scope: Global** — if the grounding base is the totality of natural facts and the grounded is the totality of moral facts, supervenience is global.
- **Base properties: Natural properties** — the grounding base is the class of natural properties that ground moral properties.
- **Supervening properties: All moral properties** — each moral property is grounded in some natural property or complex of natural properties.
- **Modal force: Metaphysical necessity** — grounding generates metaphysical necessity; the covariance across possible worlds follows from the nature of the grounding relation itself.

**Crucial distinction:** The argument distinguishes supervenience (a modal correlation claim) from grounding (a non-causal determination relation). Supervenience is the symptom; grounding is the underlying metaphysical structure that explains the symptom.

### Blackburn's Challenge Assessment

**The grounding response directly addresses Blackburn's challenge:** Blackburn said realism cannot explain why moral properties must covary with natural properties. The grounding response says: moral properties are grounded in natural properties, and any grounded property necessarily covaries with its grounds. The explanation is not circular (it does not assume supervenience to explain supervenience) — it posits a more fundamental metaphysical relation (grounding) from which supervenience follows.

**Residual challenge:** The grounding response shifts the explanatory burden from supervenience to grounding. The question "why do moral properties supervene on natural properties?" becomes "why are moral properties grounded in natural properties?" If the grounding relation itself is unexplained, the explanatory burden has been relocated but not discharged. The realist must either:
- Accept the grounding relation as itself brute (which is analogous to accepting supervenience as brute — not a decisive objection if some metaphysical relations must be brute), or
- Explain the grounding relation further (e.g., by appeal to the essence of moral properties, or by conceptual analysis of moral terms).

**Dialectical assessment:** The grounding response is a genuine advance over the brute necessity response. It provides a model of how supervenience could be explained — through a more fundamental metaphysical relation — rather than merely denying the need for explanation. The question is whether the grounding relation itself is sufficiently well-understood and whether it is compatible with the realist's other commitments (especially non-naturalism).

### Realist Response Evaluation

**The grounding response evaluated on its own terms:**

*Does grounding genuinely explain supervenience?* YES, with a qualification. If grounding is a necessitation relation (if A grounds B, then necessarily, if A then B), then supervenience follows from grounding. The explanation is valid: from the premise that moral properties are grounded in natural properties and the nature of grounding as a necessitation relation, the supervenience of the moral on the natural follows. The qualification: this explanation works only if the notion of grounding is sufficiently well-defined and its modal profile is agreed upon. The metaphysics of grounding is a contested field, and grounding skeptics may deny that the notion is sufficiently clear to do explanatory work.

*Does grounding preserve non-naturalism?* CONTESTED. The non-naturalist wants moral properties to be distinct from and irreducible to natural properties. If moral properties are grounded in natural properties, the grounding relation imposes a dependence of the moral on the natural. Some argue that this dependence compromises non-naturalism — a property that is fully grounded in natural properties is not genuinely sui generis. Others argue that grounding is consistent with non-naturalism because grounded properties can have features (normativity, categorical reason-giving force) that their grounds lack. The resolution of this issue depends on the precise nature of grounding and the precise formulation of non-naturalism.

*Does grounding collapse into reduction?* POSSIBLY. If grounding is sufficiently "tight" (e.g., if the grounded is nothing over and above the grounds), then grounding entails reduction — the moral is reducible to the natural. If the realist is comfortable with naturalist reductionism, this is not a problem. If the realist is a non-naturalist, the possibility that grounding entails reduction is a cost.

### Expressivist Response Evaluation

**The grounding response and expressivism:** The grounding response does not directly engage with the expressivist's explanatory claim. The expressivist claims that supervenience is better explained by the nature of moral attitudes than by any metaphysical relation. The grounding response provides an alternative model — supervenience is explained by metaphysical grounding — but does not show that this model is better than the expressivist model. The comparative question remains: is supervenience better explained by grounding or by the consistency constraints on moral attitudes?

**The expressivist's counter-response:** The expressivist can argue that the grounding response merely relocates the problem: why are moral properties grounded in natural properties? If the answer is "because of the essence of moral properties," the expressivist can ask why moral properties have this essence. If the answer is "it is a brute fact about moral properties," the realist is back to accepting brute necessities, which was the original charge.

### Overall Dialectical Status

**Rating: MODESTLY FAVORS REALISM (compared to no response), but DIALECTICALLY NEUTRAL overall.**

The grounding response shows that the realist can provide a non-trivial explanation of supervenience — it is not a "brute, unexplained necessity" as Blackburn claimed, but a consequence of the grounding of moral properties in natural properties. This significantly reduces the force of the supervenience challenge. However, the grounding response introduces new questions (why is the grounding relation necessary? Does grounding compromise non-naturalism?) that prevent it from being a decisive realist victory. The supervenience dialectic is advanced but not resolved: the debate shifts from "can realism explain supervenience?" to "can the grounding-based explanation be developed without collapsing realism into reductionism?"
```

## Example 4: Expressivist Response — Supervenience and the Logic of Attitudes

### Input

> Why does supervenience hold? Because moral judgments are not beliefs about a distinct realm of moral facts, but expressions of attitudes. When I judge that an action is wrong, I am not describing a property of the action — I am expressing a con-attitude toward actions of that kind, an attitude that guides my choices and coordinates with the attitudes of others. This function of moral judgment — to guide action and coordinate practical responses — imposes a consistency requirement. I cannot coherently express a con-attitude toward one action of a given natural type and fail to express that attitude toward another action of the same natural type. To do so would be to undermine the practical point of moral judgment: the point is to have a stable, action-guiding policy toward actions of that type. Supervenience is thus not a mysterious metaphysical fact about moral properties — it is a constraint that follows from the very nature of moral attitudinizing. Expressivism explains supervenience not as a relation between properties but as a condition on the coherence of practical attitudes. This explanatory advantage is a mark in favor of expressivism.

This is the expressivist explanation of supervenience. The analysis assesses the explanatory power of this account and its vulnerability to objections.

### Output

```
## Moral Supervenience Analysis Report

### Basic Information
- **Analysis ID**: MSEX004
- **Supervenience Relation**: Reinterpreted as a constraint on attitudes, not a relation between properties
- **Argument Type**: Expressivist defense — positive explanation of supervenience
- **Source**: Composite expressivist argument (Blackburn / Gibbard synthesis)

### Supervenience Relation Specification

**Reinterpretation under expressivism:** The expressivist reformulates the supervenience claim. Instead of "moral properties supervene on natural properties" (which quantifies over moral properties and assumes their existence), the expressivist says: "it is a constraint on the coherence of moral attitudes that one cannot have different moral attitudes toward natural duplicates."

- **Strength: Strong** — the constraint applies across all possible judgments, not merely within a world. Any coherent set of moral attitudes must treat natural duplicates alike.
- **Scope: Global** — the constraint applies to the entire set of moral attitudes. An attitude profile that assigns different moral responses to natural duplicates is incoherent.
- **Base properties: Natural properties (reconceived)** — the base is the class of natural features of the objects of moral evaluation. The expressivist does not need to specify a privileged subclass of natural properties as the supervenience base — the constraint is that attitudes must be consistent with respect to *all* natural features.
- **Modal force: Conceptual necessity** — the constraint follows from the nature of moral judgment. Anyone who understands what it is to make a moral judgment is thereby committed to consistency across natural duplicates.

### Blackburn's Challenge Assessment

**The expressivist explanation as a response to the challenge:** The expressivist does not merely note that realism cannot explain supervenience — she offers a positive explanation of why supervenience holds. The explanation proceeds in two steps:
1. Moral judgments express attitudes with a practical function (guiding action, coordinating responses).
2. Practical attitudes with this function must be consistent across natural duplicates — inconsistency would undermine the practical point of having the attitudes.

If successful, this provides an explanation that realism (allegedly) cannot match. The realist must explain a metaphysical correlation between distinct realms; the expressivist explains a conceptual constraint on attitudinizing.

**How good is the explanation?**

*Step 1 (moral judgments express attitudes):* This is the core commitment of expressivism. If expressivism is independently plausible, step 1 is plausible. If expressivism faces serious objections independent of supervenience (e.g., the Frege-Geach problem), the supervenience explanation inherits those problems.

*Step 2 (attitudes must be consistent across natural duplicates):* This is the key explanatory step. The expressivist claims that practical attitudes must be consistent across natural duplicates because the point of moral judgment is to have stable, action-guiding policies. But the realist can also claim that moral judgments must be consistent across natural duplicates — this is the principle of universalizability, which is compatible with realism. The expressivist must show that her explanation is not merely compatible with the supervenience datum but actually explains it better than the realist's alternative (e.g., the principle of universalizability plus the claim that moral properties are grounded in natural properties).

**Critical question:** Does the expressivist explanation explain the *necessity* of supervenience, or merely the *acceptance* of supervenience? If the expressivist only explains why we think supervenience holds (because our moral attitudes impose consistency requirements), but not why supervenience actually holds (as a feature of the world), the explanation is incomplete. The expressivist's quasi-realist ambitions may address this: the expressivist can "earn the right" to speak of moral properties and their supervenience, but whether quasi-realism succeeds is the broader question.

### Realist Response Evaluation

**Realist challenge to the expressivist explanation:** The realist can argue that the expressivist explanation is no better — and may be worse — than the realist explanation:

- *Parity:* Both realists and expressivists can note that moral judgment involves a consistency commitment. This is not a distinctive advantage for expressivism.
- *The supervenience of attitudes:* The expressivist must explain not only why moral attitudes supervene on natural properties, but why attitudes in general supervene on natural properties (or why moral attitudes in particular exhibit this pattern). This is a second explanatory burden that the expressivist may not have noticed, and it is not obviously easier to discharge than the realist's burden.
- *No property, no problem?:* The expressivist denies (or quasi-realistically reconstructs) the existence of moral properties. But the supervenience datum is naturally expressed as a claim about properties. If the expressivist cannot capture the full content of the supervenience intuition without invoking properties, her explanation is incomplete.

### Expressivist Response Evaluation

**Dreier's triviality challenge applied:** The expressivist explanation may be trivial. The claim that "we cannot have different moral attitudes toward natural duplicates" is a specific instance of the general principle that we cannot have different attitudes of any kind toward exact duplicates. This general principle is explained by the nature of attitudes (or the logic of predicates), not by anything specific to morality. So the expressivist does not explain anything distinctively moral — she explains something true of all attitudes. The expressivist can respond that moral attitudes have a distinctive practical function that generates a *substantive* consistency requirement, not merely a formal one — but spelling this out is challenging.

**The Frege-Geach shadow:** The expressivist explanation of supervenience must be compatible with the expressivist's handling of the Frege-Geach problem. If expressivism struggles to account for the logical behavior of moral terms in embedded contexts, it may also struggle to account for the modal behavior expressed by supervenience claims.

### Overall Dialectical Status

**Rating: DIALECTICALLY NEUTRAL — the expressivist explanation has intuitive appeal but faces unresolved challenges.**

The expressivist explanation captures something genuine about moral judgment: there is a practical inconsistency in treating natural duplicates differently from a moral point of view, and this inconsistency is best understood in terms of the action-guiding function of moral judgment. However, the explanation faces three significant challenges:
1. **Triviality:** It may explain only what is true of all attitudes, not what is distinctively moral.
2. **Necessity:** It may explain why we accept supervenience, not why supervenience holds as a modal fact.
3. **The supervenience of attitudes:** The expressivist inherits a parallel explanatory burden about attitudes.

The expressivist explanation is a genuine contribution to the debate, but it does not provide a decisive reason to prefer expressivism over realism on the question of supervenience.
```

## Example 5: Cross-Domain Supervenience Comparison (Aesthetic, Mental)

### Input

> The supervenience of the moral on the natural is not unique. Aesthetic properties also supervene on non-aesthetic properties — a painting cannot be beautiful without differing from an ugly painting in its non-aesthetic features (color distribution, composition, brushwork). Mental properties supervene on physical properties — two physically identical brains cannot differ in their mental states. If the moral case is structurally similar to these other cases, then whatever explains supervenience in one domain may explain it in the others. If the mental supervenience is accepted as a brute fact (or is explained by grounding, identity, or supervenience without explanatory burden), the moral case may be treated similarly. Conversely, if the moral supervenience is considered deeply mysterious but the mental supervenience is not, this asymmetry needs explanation. The supervenience challenge to moral realism may thus be weakened by the observation that supervenience is a pervasive phenomenon across many domains, and singling out the moral case as uniquely problematic may be arbitrary.

This is a cross-domain comparison argument. The analysis assesses whether the supervenience of the moral on the natural is relevantly analogous to supervenience in other domains.

## Moral Supervenience Analysis Report

### Basic Information
- **Analysis ID**: MSEX005
- **Supervenience Relation**: Cross-domain comparison — moral supervenience compared with aesthetic and mental supervenience
- **Argument Type**: Analogical argument — parity-based defense of realism
- **Source**: Composite cross-domain comparison argument

### Supervenience Relation Specification

**Moral supervenience (target):** Strong global supervenience of moral properties on natural properties.

**Aesthetic supervenience (comparison domain 1):** Strong global supervenience of aesthetic properties (beauty, ugliness, elegance, garishness) on non-aesthetic properties (color, shape, composition, texture). Two paintings identical in all non-aesthetic respects cannot differ in their aesthetic properties.

**Mental supervenience (comparison domain 2):** Strong global supervenience of mental properties (beliefs, desires, sensations) on physical properties (brain states, neural configurations). Two organisms identical in all physical respects cannot differ in their mental states.

**Structural comparison:** All three domains exhibit a covariance relation between a "higher-level" domain and a "lower-level" base. In each case, the covariance is modal (it holds across possible worlds) and the base is restricted (non-aesthetic, physical).

### Blackburn's Challenge Assessment

**Parity argument against Blackburn:** The cross-domain comparison challenges the claim that moral supervenience is *distinctively* problematic for realism. The structure of the argument:

1. Mental properties supervene on physical properties. This supervenience is (typically) not considered a decisive objection to realism about mental properties.
2. Aesthetic properties supervene on non-aesthetic properties. This supervenience is (typically) not considered a decisive objection to realism about aesthetic properties.
3. Moral supervenience is structurally analogous to mental and aesthetic supervenience.
4. Therefore, moral supervenience should not be considered a decisive objection to moral realism — or, if it is, parity demands that mental and aesthetic realism be similarly challenged.

**Assessment of the parity argument:** The force of the argument depends on whether the moral case is genuinely analogous to the comparison cases. Potential disanalogies:

- *Mental-physical supervenience:* The mental supervenience is typically explained by functionalism, identity theory, or grounding. The realist about mental states has a well-developed metaphysical story — the mental is realized by or identical to the physical. The moral realist (especially the non-naturalist) does not have a comparably well-developed story about the relation between moral and natural properties.
- *Aesthetic supervenience:* Aesthetic realism is itself controversial. Many philosophers treat aesthetic properties as response-dependent — beauty is constituted by the response of ideal observers under ideal conditions. If this is correct, aesthetic "supervenience" is explained by the response-dependence, which is analogous to the expressivist explanation of moral supervenience, not the realist one.
- *Normative vs. descriptive:* Moral properties are normative — they provide reasons for action. Mental and aesthetic properties may not be normative in the same way (though some aesthetic properties may be). The normative character of moral properties may make their supervenience on non-normative properties distinctively puzzling.

### Realist Response Evaluation

**The cross-domain comparison AS a realist response:** The comparison serves as a defensive realist move — it blocks the claim that moral supervenience is uniquely problematic by pointing out that structurally similar supervenience relations are accepted without explanatory anxiety in other domains. If the anti-realist wants to press the supervenience challenge against moral realism, she must either:
- Show that the moral case is disanalogous to the mental and aesthetic cases, or
- Accept that the mental and aesthetic cases are also problematic (which would be a radical extension of the challenge).

**Limitations of this response:** The parity argument does not *explain* moral supervenience — it merely argues that the failure to explain it is not dialectically decisive. This is a defensive move, not a constructive one. A full realist response would need to go beyond parity and provide a positive explanation of moral supervenience (e.g., grounding, conceptual truth, reduction).

### Expressivist Response Evaluation

**Expressivism and cross-domain comparison:** The expressivist can respond to the parity argument by noting that expressivism provides a unified explanation of supervenience across normative domains. Moral, aesthetic, and perhaps other normative judgments (prudential, epistemic) all express attitudes with practical functions, and the supervenience constraint applies to all of them. This would actually give expressivism an advantage: it explains not only moral supervenience but a broader pattern of normative supervenience in a unified way.

**The "earn the right" move:** The expressivist (especially the quasi-realist) can also "earn the right" to talk about mental and aesthetic supervenience in expressivist terms, extending the expressivist program to these domains. Whether this is plausible depends on whether expressivism is plausible for these other domains — and many who accept expressivism about morality reject it for mentality.

### Overall Dialectical Status

**Rating: MODESTLY FAVORS REALISM — the cross-domain comparison weakens the claim that moral supervenience is uniquely problematic.**

The cross-domain comparison is an effective defensive move for the realist. It shows that the mere existence of a strong supervenience relation is not dialectically decisive — such relations are pervasive and broadly accepted in other domains. The anti-realist who wants to press the supervenience challenge must show that the moral case is relevantly different from the mental and aesthetic cases. This can be done (the normative character of moral properties, the lack of a developed realist metaphysical story), but it requires argument. The parity argument raises the bar for the anti-realist without providing a positive explanation of moral supervenience on behalf of the realist.

**Strongest element:** The mental supervenience analogy — mental-physical supervenience is widely accepted without being considered a decisive objection to realism about mental states, and moral-natural supervenience may be structurally similar.
**Weakest element:** The disanalogy between mental-physical supervenience (for which robust explanatory models exist) and moral-natural supervenience (for which they do not, at least for non-naturalist realism). Parity may fail because the explanatory gaps are of different magnitudes.
