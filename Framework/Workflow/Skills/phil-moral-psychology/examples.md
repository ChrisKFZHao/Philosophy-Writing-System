# Moral Psychology Analysis Examples

> This document demonstrates the practical application of moral psychology analysis across different argument types. Each example shows the complete input-to-output process.

---

## Example 1: Strong Motivational Internalism (Hypothetical)

### Input

> To sincerely judge that an action is morally wrong is to disapprove of it. When I judge that lying is wrong, I am not merely reporting a fact about lying — I am expressing my opposition to it, my commitment to avoiding it, my disposition to censure those who lie. This is what distinguishes moral judgment from mere factual belief. A person who says "Lying is wrong" but feels no inclination whatsoever to avoid lying, who sees nothing objectionable about lying, who would happily lie whenever convenient — such a person does not genuinely judge that lying is wrong. They are merely mouthing the words, using moral language in what Hare called an "inverted commas" sense. Moral judgment is constitutively motivational: to judge that φ is wrong just is, in part, to be motivated to avoid φ-ing.

### Output

```
## Moral Psychology Analysis Report

### Executive Summary

This argument defends strong motivational internalism with an expressivist underpinning. Moral judgment is constitutively motivational — it is part of the very concept of moral judgment that it involves motivation. The argument treats the amoralist as conceptually impossible (a person who utters moral words without motivation is not genuinely making a moral judgment) and implicitly relies on a Humean framework in which attitudes (not beliefs) motivate. The combination of strong internalism and Humeanism entails non-cognitivism: if moral judgments necessarily motivate, and beliefs alone cannot motivate, then moral judgments cannot be beliefs. The argument accepts this entailment and explicitly defends an expressivist semantics.

---

### 1. Internalism/Externalism Classification

**Classification: Strong Motivational Internalism**

*Evidence:*
- "To sincerely judge that an action is morally wrong is to disapprove of it" — judgment is identified with a motivational attitude (disapproval).
- "Moral judgment is constitutively motivational: to judge that φ is wrong just is, in part, to be motivated to avoid φ-ing" — the necessity is conceptual, not merely causal or normative.
- "A person who says 'Lying is wrong' but feels no inclination whatsoever to avoid lying... does not genuinely judge that lying is wrong" — the amoralist is ruled out as conceptually impossible; such a person is not making a genuine moral judgment.

*Confidence:* High. The argument explicitly asserts strong internalism and deploys it as the central premise.

*Amoralist status under this view:* Conceptually impossible. Apparent amoralists are either insincere (merely mouthing words) or using moral language in an inverted-commas sense without genuine moral understanding.

*Akrasia status under this view:* Problematic. If moral judgment constitutively involves motivation, and motivation is sufficient for action (a natural reading of expressivism), then genuinely judging that φ is wrong would preclude intentionally φ-ing. The argument does not address this. If motivation is merely contributory (can be overridden), the view can accommodate akrasia but weakens the constitutive claim.

---

### 2. Humean/Anti-Humean Diagnosis

**Classification: Humean (Implicit)**

*Evidence:*
- The argument distinguishes moral judgment from "mere factual belief" and associates moral judgment with attitudes (disapproval, opposition, commitment) rather than beliefs. This presupposes that beliefs are motivationally inert — the core Humean thesis.
- The argument's expressivism — moral judgments are expressions of attitude, not reports of fact — is standardly motivated by the Humean view that beliefs alone cannot motivate.

*Framework status:* Implicit Humean. The argument does not explicitly argue for the Humean theory but relies on it throughout. The inference from "moral judgments motivate" to "moral judgments are not beliefs" requires the Humean premise that beliefs alone do not motivate.

---

### 3. Amoralist Handling Assessment

**Handling: Amoralist denied as conceptually impossible**

*Argument's strategy:* The amoralist is dismissed as not genuinely making a moral judgment. They are "merely mouthing the words" — they lack the requisite motivational attitude and therefore do not count as sincerely judging.

*Plausibility assessment:* This response faces the burden of explaining why the amoralist appears to understand moral concepts perfectly well. Consider a sociopath who says, "I know that torturing for fun is wrong — I just don't care." The sociopath can deploy moral concepts in inference, can identify wrong-making features, and can predict others' moral reactions. If this is not "genuine moral judgment," the internalist owes an account of what is missing — and it cannot simply be "motivation," on pain of circularity. The argument gestures at this with the "inverted commas" strategy (Hare), but does not develop it.

*Dialectical burden:* The argument must explain how an agent can be fully competent with moral concepts yet fail to make genuine moral judgments when using them. If moral concepts are essentially tied to motivation, then conceptual competence should bring motivation with it — but this seems empirically false.

---

### 4. Akrasia Analysis

**Handling: Not directly addressed — potential inconsistency**

*Problem:* If moral judgment is constitutively motivational (strong internalism), and the motivation is sufficient for action, then akrasia is impossible. But akrasia — knowing the better but doing the worse — is a pervasive phenomenon. The agent who judges that they ought to tell the truth but lies anyway for personal gain is akratic; on the argument's view, their judgment may not be "genuine" if it fails to motivate.

*Possible responses:*
- **Contributory motivation:** The motivation entailed by moral judgment is one force among many; stronger competing motivations can override it. This preserves akrasia but weakens internalism — if the motivation can always be overridden, what does "constitutively motivational" amount to?
- **Inverted-commas strategy:** The akratic agent is not genuinely judging; they are in some conflicted or self-deceived state. But this makes akrasia proof against empirical falsification — any apparent case is re-described as non-genuine judgment.

*Empirical adequacy:* Weak. The view cannot easily accommodate the ordinary experience of moral conflict and weakness of will.

---

### 5. Motivational Architecture Map

```
Moral Judgment (disapproval/commitment attitude)
    │  [direction of fit: world-to-mind]
    │  [constitutively motivational]
    ├── Motivation to φ (avoid/perform)
    │       │
    │       └── Intention to φ
    │               │
    │               └── Action (unless overridden)
    │
    └── (No judgment without motivation path)
```

*Notes:* The argument collapses the distinction between judging and being motivated — they are not two separate states but aspects of a single attitudinal state. There is no separate "belief that φ is wrong" that then (contingently or necessarily) produces motivation; the judgment is itself a motivational state.

---

### 6. Metaethical Implications

*Applying the "Moral Problem" trilemma:*
- The argument accepts strong internalism (B): "Moral judgment is constitutively motivational."
- The argument implicitly accepts Humeanism (C): Beliefs alone cannot motivate.
- Therefore, the argument must reject cognitivism (A): Moral judgments are not beliefs.

*Compatible positions:* Expressivism, prescriptivism, emotivism — all forms of non-cognitivism.

*Incompatible positions:* Cognitivist realism, error theory (which holds that moral judgments are beliefs, albeit false ones), constructivism that treats moral judgments as beliefs about constructed facts.

*Dialectical consistency:* The argument is internally consistent — it explicitly embraces the non-cognitivism that its moral psychology entails. No tension between psychology and metaethics.
```

## Example 2: The Amoralist Challenge (Hypothetical)

### Input

> Consider Charles: a highly intelligent, conceptually sophisticated individual who has been raised in a moral community and fully understands moral concepts. Charles can identify wrong-making features of actions, can engage in moral reasoning, and can predict others' moral reactions with accuracy. Yet Charles feels no motivation whatsoever to act morally. He says, sincerely and reflectively, "I know that donating to famine relief is morally required. I just don't care. I have absolutely no inclination to do it." Charles is not depressed, not exhausted, not conceptually confused. He simply lacks the motivational connection that internalists claim is conceptually guaranteed. If Charles is possible — and he certainly seems possible — then strong motivational internalism is false. Moral judgment does not conceptually entail motivation. The connection between judgment and motivation is contingent, mediated by desires, character, and concern — factors that can be absent without any conceptual incoherence.

### Output

```
## Moral Psychology Analysis Report

### Executive Summary

This argument deploys the amoralist — a conceptually competent, sincere moral judge who lacks all motivation — as a counterexample to strong and weak motivational internalism. The argument defends motivational externalism: moral judgment does not necessarily involve motivation; motivation requires additional factors (desire, character, concern) that are contingently present or absent. The argument presupposes a Humean framework (beliefs alone do not motivate) and treats the amoralist as fully possible. The externalist position is compatible with cognitivist realism, error theory, and constructivism but must respond to Smith's fetishism charge: if motivation is external to moral judgment, why is being motivated by one's moral judgments anything more than a fetish?

---

### 1. Internalism/Externalism Classification

**Classification: Motivational Externalism**

*Evidence:*
- "Moral judgment does not conceptually entail motivation" — explicit denial of strong internalism.
- "The connection between judgment and motivation is contingent, mediated by desires, character, and concern" — motivation is external to judgment and depends on additional factors.
- "Charles is not depressed, not exhausted, not conceptually confused" — Charles is not a defective case of a normally internalist pattern; he is a fully competent moral judge who simply lacks the contingent motivational factors.

*Confidence:* High. The argument explicitly asserts externalism and provides a counterexample to internalism.

*Amoralist status:* Fully possible and unproblematic. Charles is conceptually coherent and empirically plausible.

*Akrasia status:* Fully accommodated. The akratic agent judges that φ is required but lacks the contingent motivational factors to φ.

---

### 2. Humean/Anti-Humean Diagnosis

**Classification: Humean (Implicit)**

*Evidence:*
- The argument states that motivation is "mediated by desires, character, and concern" — desires are treated as the primary motivational engines, consistent with the Humean picture.
- The amoralist is described as lacking the right desires — "I just don't care" — and this absence explains the absence of motivation. This is precisely the Humean claim: without desire, judgment (belief) is motivationally inert.

*Framework status:* Implicit Humean. The argument assumes the Humean theory in its diagnosis of why Charles lacks motivation: he has the belief (the moral judgment) but lacks the relevant desire. The Humean framework makes the amoralist straightforward to describe — the belief-desire pair that would produce motivation is missing the desire component.

---

### 3. Amoralist Handling Assessment

**Handling: Amoralist fully accommodated — used as evidence for externalism**

*Argument's strategy:* Charles is offered as a direct counterexample to internalism. The argument characterizes him in detail to preempt internalist responses: he is not insincere (he "reflectively" judges), not conceptually confused (he "fully understands moral concepts"), not defeated (not depressed, not exhausted). The possibility of Charles is treated as intuitively compelling.

*Plausibility assessment:* The argument is dialectically effective — it forces the internalist to either deny Charles's possibility (explaining why the appearance of possibility is misleading) or weaken internalism to accommodate him (e.g., rational internalism: Charles is irrational). The description of Charles is designed to block standard internalist responses: he is not merely mouthing words (he is sincere and reflective), not conceptually deficient (he understands moral concepts fully), and not in a defeated state (no depression or exhaustion).

*Dialectical burden shifted to internalist:* The internalist must now explain why Charles is impossible, despite his apparent conceptual coherence. The burden is significant.

*Externalist's own burden:* The externalist must address Smith's fetishism charge. If Charles is a fully competent moral judge who lacks all moral motivation, what would it mean for someone else to be motivated by their moral judgments? Would that motivation be a fetish — a concern for rightness as such rather than for the right-making features?

---

### 4. Akrasia Analysis

**Handling: Fully accommodated**

*Externalist account of akrasia:* The akratic agent judges that φ is required but lacks the desire, character, or concern to be motivated to φ. There is no puzzle: judgment and motivation are distinct existences, and they can come apart. Akrasia is simply a case where the contingent motivational factors do not align with the moral judgment.

*Empirical adequacy:* High. Akrasia is a pervasive phenomenon that externalism accommodates naturally. No special explanation is needed.

*Potential weakness:* If akrasia is so easy, the externalist may have trouble explaining why moral judgment ever motivates. If motivation is entirely contingent, why do most people feel at least some motivation when they make moral judgments? The externalist must provide an account of the typical motivational connection without making it necessary — perhaps through moral education, empathy, or the internalization of norms.

---

### 5. Motivational Architecture Map

```
Moral Judgment (belief that φ is required/wrong)
    │  [direction of fit: mind-to-world]
    │  [cognitively represents moral facts]
    │  [motivationally inert by itself]
    │
    ├── + Desire to be moral ──────┐
    ├── + Empathic concern ────────┤
    ├── + Character trait ─────────┤
    ├── + Internalized norm ───────┤
    │                              ▼
    │                        Motivation to φ
    │                              │
    │                              ▼
    │                        Intention to φ
    │                              │
    │                              ▼
    │                        Action
    │
    └── Without additional factors
         │
         └── No motivation (Charles case)
```

*Notes:* Moral judgment is a belief; beliefs alone are motivationally inert (Humeanism). Motivation requires a belief-desire pair. The moral belief must combine with a desire (to be moral, to help others, etc.) to produce motivation. Without the desire, the belief produces no motivation — this is the amoralist.

---

### 6. Metaethical Implications

*Applying the "Moral Problem" trilemma:*
- The argument rejects internalism (B): Moral judgment does not necessarily motivate.
- The argument implicitly accepts cognitivism (A): Moral judgments are beliefs (they are truth-apt, they represent facts).
- The argument implicitly accepts Humeanism (C): Beliefs alone cannot motivate.

*Compatible positions:* Cognitivist realism, error theory, constructivism — any view that treats moral judgments as beliefs.

*Incompatible positions:* Strong non-cognitivism (expressivism, emotivism) — these views are primarily motivated by internalism, and lose their central motivation if externalism is true.

*Fetishism challenge:* Smith's charge that externalism makes moral motivation a fetish must be addressed. The argument does not explicitly address this. If the argument is paired with a virtue-ethical or sentimentalist account of moral motivation (moral motivation arises from properly cultivated character or empathic concern, not from a de dicto concern for rightness), the fetishism charge may be answerable. Without such an account, the charge stands.
```

## Example 3: Humean Theory of Motivation (Hypothetical)

### Input

> Consider the structure of intentional action. When I raise my hand, what explains this action? It is because I desire to signal my agreement, and I believe that raising my hand will signal my agreement. Remove the desire, and I do not raise my hand — no matter how confident I am that hand-raising signals agreement. Remove the belief, and I do not raise my hand either — no matter how strongly I desire to signal agreement. Both elements are necessary; neither is sufficient alone. This is the Humean theory of motivation in its most general form: reasons explain actions, but motivating reasons always involve both a belief (representing how the world is) and a desire (representing how the agent wants the world to be). Beliefs have a mind-to-world direction of fit — they aim to represent the world accurately. Desires have a world-to-mind direction of fit — they aim to make the world match their content. No amount of purely cognitive activity — no chain of reasoning, no accumulation of beliefs — can produce a motivation to act without a desire somewhere in the chain. Reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions.

### Output

```
## Moral Psychology Analysis Report

### Executive Summary

This argument defends the Humean theory of motivation using the direction-of-fit analysis and the belief-desire pair model of action explanation. It does not take a position on motivational internalism — the Humean thesis is neutral between internalism and externalism, as it concerns the general structure of motivation, not specifically moral motivation. The argument's significance for metaethics lies in the constraint it imposes: if the Humean theory is true, then any view on which moral judgments necessarily motivate must either treat moral judgments as desires (non-cognitivism) or reject the Humean theory (anti-Humeanism). The argument is silent on internalism, the amoralist, and akrasia per se.

---

### 1. Internalism/Externalism Classification

**Classification: Not Specified**

*Rationale:* The argument defends the general Humean theory of motivation and does not address the specific relation between moral judgment and motivation. The Humean thesis is compatible with both internalism (if moral judgments are desires or desire-involving states) and externalism (if moral judgments are beliefs that require additional desires to motivate).

*Implicit implications:* The Humean theory constrains the space of internalist views. If Humeanism is true and a view holds that moral judgments necessarily motivate (internalism), then moral judgments cannot be purely cognitive beliefs — they must be desires or desire-involving states. The argument does not draw this conclusion, but it is a natural extension.

---

### 2. Humean/Anti-Humean Diagnosis

**Classification: Humean (Explicit)**

*Evidence:*
- "Both elements are necessary; neither is sufficient alone" — the belief-desire pair is the minimal sufficient condition for motivation.
- "Beliefs have a mind-to-world direction of fit... Desires have a world-to-mind direction of fit" — explicit commitment to the direction-of-fit analysis (Anscombe, Smith).
- "No amount of purely cognitive activity... can produce a motivation to act without a desire somewhere in the chain" — explicit anti-Humean denial.
- "Reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions" — direct quotation of the Humean slogan.

*Confidence:* High. The argument explicitly and systematically defends the Humean theory.

*Framework precision:* The argument distinguishes motivating reasons (psychological states that explain action, which always involve desires) from what might be termed normative reasons. This distinction is characteristic of sophisticated Humean views.

---

### 3. Amoralist Handling Assessment

**Handling: Not directly addressed**

*Implications for the amoralist:* If the Humean theory is true, the amoralist is straightforward to describe: the amoralist makes a moral judgment (which, if it is a belief, is motivationally inert by itself) and lacks the relevant desire (to be moral, to avoid harm, etc.), so no motivation results. But this analysis depends on the independent premise that moral judgments are beliefs (cognitivism), which the Humean argument does not assert.

*Compatibility with internalism:* If combined with strong internalism, the Humean theory forces the conclusion that moral judgments are not beliefs (non-cognitivism), which in turn makes the amoralist conceptually impossible. The argument does not address this interaction.

---

### 4. Akrasia Analysis

**Handling: Implicitly accommodated**

*Humean account of akrasia:* The akratic agent has a belief about what is best and a standing desire to do what is best, but also a stronger competing desire (for pleasure, for ease, etc.). The all-things-considered judgment is a belief; it requires a desire to produce motivation. If the competing desire is stronger, action follows the stronger desire despite the all-things-considered belief. This is a natural Humean account of akrasia.

*Empirical adequacy:* The Humean theory accommodates akrasia well — it explains why we sometimes act against our better judgment as a matter of competing desires.

---

### 5. Motivational Architecture Map

```
Belief (mind-to-world fit)                Desire (world-to-mind fit)
    │                                          │
    │  "Raising my hand will                   │  "I want to signal agreement"
    │   signal agreement"                      │
    │                                          │
    └────────────┬─────────────────────────────┘
                 │
                 ▼
         Intention / Motivating Reason
                 │
                 ▼
              Action
```

*Notes:* Both belief and desire are necessary; neither is sufficient alone. The belief identifies a means; the desire sets an end. Without the desire, the belief is motivationally inert. Without the belief, the desire cannot guide action toward satisfaction. The direction-of-fit distinction is fundamental: beliefs aim to fit the world (they are correct when true), desires aim to make the world fit them (they are satisfied when realized).

---

### 6. Metaethical Implications

*The Humean theory as a constraint:* The Humean theory is a premise in the "Moral Problem" trilemma. If Humeanism is true:
- Strong internalism → Non-cognitivism (moral judgments are desires or desire-like states).
- Cognitivism + Externalism → Viable (moral judgments are beliefs that contingently motivate).
- Cognitivism + Internalism → Requires anti-Humeanism.

*The argument's position:* The argument defends Humeanism but does not take a position on internalism or cognitivism. It leaves open all three solutions to the trilemma: reject cognitivism, reject internalism, or reject Humeanism.

*Dialectical contribution:* The argument establishes one vertex of the trilemma. Its force in metaethics is conditional: if you accept Humeanism, you are constrained in ways the trilemma makes explicit. But the argument does not force a particular metaethical position.
```

## Example 4: Akrasia Analysis (Hypothetical)

### Input

> Weakness of will is a genuine and pervasive phenomenon. Consider: I judge, all things considered, that I ought to finish this paper tonight. I know the deadline is tomorrow; I know the consequences of missing it. I sincerely believe that finishing the paper is what I have most reason to do. Yet I find myself watching videos online, knowing full well that I am acting against my better judgment. This is not a case where I have changed my mind — I still judge that I ought to be writing. This is not a case where I am compelled — I could stop watching videos at any moment. This is akrasia: free, intentional action against one's own all-things-considered better judgment. Any adequate moral psychology must account for akrasia. Views that make it impossible — that claim moral judgment guarantees corresponding action — are empirically inadequate. But views that make it too easy — that sever the connection between judgment and motivation entirely — fail to capture why akrasia feels like a failure, why we experience it as akrasia rather than as a simple change of preference.

### Output

```
## Moral Psychology Analysis Report

### Executive Summary

This argument uses the phenomenon of akrasia as a constraint on moral psychology. It argues against strong internalism (which makes akrasia impossible) and against reductive externalism (which makes akrasia indistinguishable from a mere change of preference). The argument's implicit position is that an adequate moral psychology must make akrasia possible but non-trivial — a middle position that neither guarantees action nor severs the judgment-motivation link entirely. The argument aligns most naturally with rational internalism: moral judgment provides reasons that would motivate a rational agent, and akrasia is a failure of rationality — possible, common, but genuinely a failure rather than a neutral psychological configuration.

---

### 1. Internalism/Externalism Classification

**Classification: Implicit Rational Internalism**

*Evidence:*
- The argument rejects strong internalism indirectly: "Views that make [akrasia] impossible — that claim moral judgment guarantees corresponding action — are empirically inadequate."
- The argument rejects reductive externalism indirectly: views that "sever the connection between judgment and motivation entirely... fail to capture why akrasia feels like a failure."
- The positive position is implied by the conjunction of these rejections: there must be a normative connection between judgment and motivation (so that akrasia counts as a failure), but the connection cannot be sufficiency (so that akrasia is possible). Rational internalism satisfies both: moral judgment provides reasons that a rational agent would act on; the akratic agent fails to respond to those reasons and is thereby irrational.

*Confidence:* Medium. The argument's positive position is implicit in its critique of extremes. It does not explicitly endorse rational internalism.

*Amoralist status (implied):* The amoralist is possible but irrational — someone who has moral reasons but systematically fails to be motivated by them due to a rationality deficit. The amoralist is "failing" in a way that mere preference difference does not capture.

---

### 2. Humean/Anti-Humean Diagnosis

**Classification: Not Specified (Neutral between options)**

*Rationale:* The argument does not address the belief-desire structure of motivation. Its claim about akrasia is compatible with both Humean and anti-Humean frameworks:
- *Humean:* Akrasia occurs when a competing desire overrides the desire that pairs with the all-things-considered belief.
- *Anti-Humean:* Akrasia occurs when a competing desire overrides the motivation that the all-things-considered belief directly produces.

The argument's internalist leanings (rational internalism) are compatible with either framework, though the combination with Humeanism yields the Smith-style "Moral Problem" that requires careful navigation.

---

### 3. Amoralist Handling Assessment

**Handling: Not directly addressed — but rational internalist implications**

*Implied handling:* If the argument's positive position is rational internalism, the amoralist is possible but irrational. The amoralist — someone who makes moral judgments without motivation — is like the akratic agent: they fail to be motivated by reasons they possess. The difference is that the amoralist's failure is systematic rather than episodic, suggesting a deeper rationality deficit.

*Assessment:* This is a plausible middle ground. It avoids the strong internalist's burden of denying the amoralist's conceptual possibility, while avoiding the externalist's problem of making the amoralist too unremarkable. The challenge is to specify what rationality failure consists in without making the view trivially unfalsifiable.

---

### 4. Akrasia Analysis

**Handling: Central — akrasia is the primary constraint on moral psychology**

*Argument's account of akrasia:*
- Akrasia is genuine: it is "free, intentional action against one's own all-things-considered better judgment."
- Akrasia is distinguishable from a change of mind: the akratic agent "still judges that [they] ought to be writing."
- Akrasia feels like a failure: the agent experiences it as acting against their better judgment, not as a neutral preference shift.

*Constraint 1 (anti-strong-internalism):* "Views that make [akrasia] impossible... are empirically inadequate." If moral judgment guarantees corresponding action, akrasia never occurs. Since akrasia does occur, strong internalism (with sufficiency) is false.

*Constraint 2 (anti-reductive-externalism):* Views that "sever the connection between judgment and motivation entirely" cannot explain the phenomenology of akrasia — why it feels like a failure. If judgment and motivation are fully independent, acting against one's judgment is no different from preferring chocolate but eating vanilla.

*Positive proposal (rational internalism):* Akrasia is a failure of practical rationality — failing to be motivated by one's own normative judgments. This makes akrasia possible (irrationality is common) while preserving its normative significance (irrationality is a genuine failure).

*Empirical adequacy:* High. The rational internalist account makes akrasia both possible and normatively significant. The challenge is to specify the rationality standard in a non-circular way.

---

### 5. Motivational Architecture Map

```
All-Things-Considered Judgment (φ is what I ought to do)
    │
    │  [Rational Internalism:] Provides reason to φ
    │  [Rationally requires motivation to φ]
    │
    ├── Rational agent ──► Motivation to φ ──► Intention ──► φ
    │
    └── Irrational agent (akratic)
         │
         ├── Motivation to φ (may be present but weak)
         └── Stronger competing motivation to ψ
              │
              └── Intention to ψ ──► ψ (akratic action)
```

*Notes:* The connection between judgment and motivation is rational, not conceptual or psychological. The akratic agent's judgment provides a reason to φ; a rational agent would be motivated by that reason. The akratic agent's failure to be (sufficiently) motivated is a rational failing. This structure makes akrasia both possible (irrationality occurs) and normatively significant (it is a genuine failing).

---

### 6. Metaethical Implications

*Position in the moral problem trilemma:*
- The argument leans toward rational internalism (weakened form of B).
- It is neutral on cognitivism (A) and Humeanism (C).
- If cognitivism + rational internalism + Humeanism: Smith-style solution — moral judgments are beliefs (cognitivism), they rationally require motivation (rational internalism), but beliefs alone do not motivate (Humeanism). Motivation comes from the rational agent's desires, which converge on what the moral beliefs prescribe.

*Compatible positions:* Smith-style cognitivist realism, Korsgaard-style constitutivism, any view that grounds motivation in rationality rather than in judgment directly.

*Incompatible positions:* Strong non-cognitivism (needs strong internalism), strong cognitivist realism with externalism (makes akrasia unremarkable).

*Key tension:* Rational internalism's account of akrasia must avoid making the view unfalsifiable. If every failure of motivation can be attributed to irrationality, the claim that "rational agents would be motivated" is empirically empty. The argument does not address this methodological concern.
```

## Example 5: Moral Judgment as Emotion — Prinz/Haidt Style (Hypothetical)

### Input

> Moral judgments are not the output of rational deliberation. They are emotional responses — gut-level reactions of approval and disapproval. Jonathan Haidt's social intuitionist model shows that moral judgment is typically the product of quick, automatic emotional intuitions, with reasoned justification arriving post hoc as confabulation. Jesse Prinz's "constructive sentimentalism" goes further: moral judgments are constituted by emotional responses — to judge that something is wrong just is to feel a sentiment of disapprobation toward it. This has significant metaethical implications. If moral judgments are emotions, then moral judgments are not beliefs — they do not represent moral facts, they express emotional responses. And if moral judgments are emotions, then they are inherently motivational: emotions are felt action-tendencies. Fear motivates flight; anger motivates aggression; moral disapprobation motivates avoidance and censure. The practicality of moral judgment — its action-guiding character — is explained by its emotional nature.

### Output

```
## Moral Psychology Analysis Report

### Executive Summary

This argument defends a sentimentalist moral psychology: moral judgments are constituted by emotional responses rather than cognitive beliefs. The view entails strong motivational internalism — emotions are inherently motivational, so moral judgments necessarily motivate. Combined with the claim that moral judgments are not beliefs (because emotions are not beliefs), this yields non-cognitivism: moral judgments are expressive rather than descriptive. The view handles the amoralist by denying conceptual possibility (someone who lacks the emotion lacks the judgment) and handles akrasia through emotional conflict (a weaker moral emotion is overridden by a stronger competing emotion). The key tension is whether emotions can have the cognitive complexity required for full-fledged moral judgment — can an emotion represent the kind of rich normative content that moral judgments seem to carry?

---

### 1. Internalism/Externalism Classification

**Classification: Strong Motivational Internalism (Emotion-Based)**

*Evidence:*
- "If moral judgments are emotions, then they are inherently motivational — emotions are felt action-tendencies."
- "Moral disapprobation motivates avoidance and censure" — the motivational force is built into the emotion that constitutes the judgment.
- The argument appeals to the "practicality of moral judgment" — its action-guiding character — as the phenomenon that sentimentalism explains.

*Confidence:* High. The argument explicitly asserts that moral judgments are emotions and that emotions are inherently motivational.

*Distinctive feature:* Unlike standard strong internalism (which may treat motivation as conceptually tied to judgment in the abstract), this version grounds the motivational force in the specific nature of emotions as action-tendencies. This provides a psychological mechanism for the internalist connection rather than treating it as a brute conceptual truth.

*Amoralist status:* Conceptually impossible — someone who lacks the emotion lacks the judgment. "To judge that something is wrong just is to feel a sentiment of disapprobation toward it." No emotion, no judgment.

*Akrasia status:* Accommodated through emotional conflict — the moral emotion (disapprobation) may be weaker than a competing emotion (desire, fear), resulting in action against the moral judgment. The judgment still involves motivation, but the motivation is not sufficient.

---

### 2. Humean/Anti-Humean Diagnosis

**Classification: Humean (Reinterpreted)**

*Evidence:*
- The argument explicitly denies that moral judgments are beliefs: "If moral judgments are emotions, then moral judgments are not beliefs — they do not represent moral facts, they express emotional responses."
- This is consistent with the Humean picture in which beliefs (representations) are motivationally inert and motivation comes from non-cognitive states (desires, emotions).
- However, the argument's motivational engine is emotion rather than desire. In standard Humeanism, desires are the motivational states; emotions are more complex (they involve both cognitive appraisals and felt action-tendencies). The sentimentalist reinterprets the Humean framework by making emotion, rather than desire, the fundamental motivational state.

*Framework status:* Humean in spirit (motivation comes from non-cognitive states), but the specific mechanism (emotion vs. desire) departs from classical Humeanism. Would classify as `humean` with the note that the motivational state is emotion rather than desire.

---

### 3. Amoralist Handling Assessment

**Handling: Amoralist denied as conceptually impossible — emotion-based version**

*Argument's strategy:* If to judge that φ is wrong just is to feel disapprobation toward φ, then someone who feels no disapprobation toward φ does not judge φ to be wrong. The amoralist is conceptually impossible because the judgment is constituted by the emotion, and lacking the emotion entails lacking the judgment.

*Plausibility assessment:* The sentimentalist version of this response has some advantages over the purely conceptual version. The conceptual internalist says "moral judgment conceptually entails motivation" without specifying the mechanism; the sentimentalist specifies the mechanism (emotion) and derives the internalist claim from the nature of emotion. This makes the response more substantive — it is an empirical-psychological claim about what moral judgment is, rather than a conceptual stipulation.

*Remaining burden:* The sentimentalist must explain apparent cases of unemotional moral judgment (e.g., the person who coolly judges that a policy is unjust without feeling anger or outrage). The Haidt-style response is that the emotion is present but subtle (a faint flicker rather than a full-blown passion) and that even "cool" judgments involve emotional processing below conscious awareness. This is an empirical claim that needs empirical support.

---

### 4. Akrasia Analysis

**Handling: Accommodated through emotional conflict**

*Sentimentalist account:* The moral emotion (disapprobation) motivates against φ-ing, but a competing emotion (desire for pleasure, fear of social cost) may motivate more strongly toward φ-ing. The akratic agent feels the pull of the moral emotion but is overpowered by the competing emotion. This makes akrasia possible (motivation is contributory, not sufficient) while preserving the internalist claim (the moral judgment inherently involves motivation).

*Empirical adequacy:* Plausible. The phenomenon of emotional conflict — being torn between the pull of conscience and the pull of desire — is a familiar feature of moral experience. The sentimentalist provides a natural psychological mechanism for akrasia: conflicting emotions with different motivational strengths.

*Potential weakness:* The sufficiency question returns. If the moral emotion can always be overpowered, does it provide a sufficiently robust connection between judgment and motivation? The internalist might respond that this is exactly the right result — moral motivation is real but not omnipotent, which matches our experience.

### 5. Motivational Architecture Map

```
Moral Situation (e.g., witnessing an act of cruelty)
    │
    │  [automatic, intuitive emotional processing]
    │
    ▼
Moral Emotion (disapprobation, outrage, guilt)
    │
    │  [felt action-tendency; world-to-mind direction of fit]
    │  [constitutes the moral judgment "that is wrong"]
    │
    ├── Motivation to avoid/censure
    │       │
    │       ├── (If unopposed) ──► Intention ──► Action
    │       │
    │       └── (If opposed by stronger emotion) ──► Overridden (akrasia)
    │
    └── Post-hoc rationalization (Haidt)
         │
         └── "Reasoned" justification (confabulation)
```

*Notes:* The judgment is the emotion; the emotion is the judgment. There is no prior cognitive evaluation that then produces an emotional response — the emotional response is the evaluation. The motivational architecture is anti-rationalist: reason plays a post-hoc justificatory role rather than a generative one.

### 6. Metaethical Implications

*Applying the "Moral Problem" trilemma:*
- The argument accepts strong internalism (B): Moral judgments necessarily motivate (because they are emotions).
- The argument implicitly accepts Humeanism (C): Non-cognitive states (emotions) motivate; beliefs do not by themselves.
- The argument rejects cognitivism (A): Moral judgments are not beliefs.

*Compatible positions:* Expressivism, emotivism, prescriptivism, and other non-cognitivist views. Sentimentalism provides a psychological foundation for expressivism — the psychological story explains why moral language is expressive rather than descriptive.

*Incompatible positions:* Cognitivist realism, error theory, constructivism — any view that treats moral judgments as truth-apt beliefs.

*Key tension:* The argument claims that moral judgments "do not represent moral facts." But emotions typically have intentional objects — fear is fear of something; anger is anger at something. If moral emotions have intentional objects with moral content (e.g., disapprobation of cruelty), do they not represent moral properties? The Prinz-style response is that the emotion does not represent a moral property; rather, the emotion is triggered by non-moral properties, and the moral judgment is the emotional response, not a representation of a moral fact. This is a subtle semantic claim that needs development.

*Dialectical consistency:* The argument is internally consistent — its moral psychology (sentimentalism) and its metaethics (non-cognitivism) align. The main vulnerability is the claim that emotions are not beliefs — this depends on a sharp belief/emotion distinction that some philosophers (Nussbaum, Solomon) reject.
```
