# Inductive Generalization

## Form

### Enumerative Induction

```
[1] O₁ possesses property P
[2] O₂ possesses property P
[3] O₃ possesses property P
…
[N] Oₙ possesses property P
(O₁, O₂, …, Oₙ are a representative sample of kind K)
∴ [N+1] All objects of kind K possess property P
```

### Statistical Induction

```
[1] In sample S, X% possess property P
[2] S is a representative sample of population T
∴ [3] In population T, approximately X% possess property P
```

## Evaluation Criteria

The strength (as opposed to validity) of an inductive argument depends on:

| Criterion | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Sample size** | The larger the sample, the stronger the induction |
| **Representativeness** | Does the sample cover key subclasses; is there sampling bias? |
| **Variability** | The lower the variability within the population, the stronger the induction |
| **Counterexamples** | Are there known counterexamples? |

## Special Concerns in Philosophical Analysis

Inductive arguments in philosophical texts are often not strict statistical inductions, but are based on "paradigm cases":

```
[1] Case A exhibits property P
[2] Case A is representative of this kind of case
∴ [3] Cases of this kind generally possess P
```

The criterion of "representativeness" here is a crucial implicit premise that often needs to be made explicit.

## Reconstruction Notes

- Mark the size of the leap from sample to population, and whether this leap is defended in the text.
- Pay attention to the distinction between "example" and "evidence" -- philosophical disputes often involve only the former.
- In reconstructing inductive generalizations, the most critical element is the **representativeness assumption**, typically handled as an implicit premise.
