# Moral Supervenience Argument (Blackburn)

## Form

### Core Structure — Blackburn's Supervenience Challenge

```
[1] Moral properties supervene on natural properties: necessarily, if X and Y share
    all natural (descriptive) properties, then X and Y share all moral properties
    (no moral difference without a natural difference)
[2] This supervenience relation itself requires explanation — it is not a brute
    modal coincidence that moral properties covary with natural properties in this
    way
[3] For the realist (especially the non-naturalist): why does this necessary
    covariance hold? If moral properties are independent, non-natural entities
    distinct from natural properties, what metaphysically forces them to track
    natural properties across all possible worlds? This seems mysterious
[4] For the expressivist / projectivist: supervenience is explained by the function
    of moral language — moral predicates exist precisely to generalize over natural
    patterns for coordinating attitudes and practical purposes. The supervenience
    constraint is built into the semantics of moral discourse, not an external
    metaphysical fact that needs explaining
∴ [5] Expressivism / projectivism provides a better explanation of moral
    supervenience than realism does — realism incurs an explanatory debt it
    cannot discharge
```

The argument is an **inference to the best explanation** (IBE): both realism and expressivism can *accommodate* the fact of moral supervenience, but only expressivism can *explain* it without invoking mysterious necessary connections between distinct ontological realms. The anti-realist advantage is explanatory, not deductive.

### Refined Structure (McPherson's Challenge)

```
[1] Moral properties robustly supervene on natural properties:
    □(∀x)(∀y)((x and y are natural duplicates) → (x and y are moral duplicates))
[2] For the non-naturalist realist: moral properties are sui generis and distinct
    from natural properties
[3] If moral properties are distinct from natural properties, the necessary
    covariance expressed by [1] stands in need of explanation — why can't a
    natural duplicate of an action that is wrong be permissible?
[4] Realists lack a satisfactory explanation of this necessary covariance
    (grounding accounts either collapse into naturalism or make the necessity brute)
∴ [5] Non-naturalist realism is in an inferior explanatory position regarding
    moral supervenience
```

## Distinctive Features

1. **Explanatory, not deductive**: The argument does not purport to refute realism. It claims that realism leaves supervenience as a brute fact, while expressivism explains it. The realist can accept the explanatory gap while denying that it is a fatal defect.

2. **Supervenience as common ground**: Both realists and anti-realists accept that moral properties supervene on natural properties. The argument exploits this shared premise — the question is which metaethical view better accounts for it.

3. **Anti-Humean necessary connections**: The core challenge is that non-naturalist realism seems to require necessary connections between distinct existences (moral and natural properties), which violates a broadly Humean prohibition. Realists must either reject Hume's dictum or show how the connection is not objectionable.

4. **The semantic vs. metaphysical reading of supervenience**: Blackburn reads supervenience as a conceptual or semantic constraint on moral discourse; realists read it as a metaphysical relation of dependence or grounding. The argument's force depends on which reading is correct.

## Evaluation Criteria / Objections

| Objection | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Supervenience as a conceptual truth (Jackson, Smith)** | Supervenience is an analytic or conceptual truth — it is part of the concept of a moral property that it supervenes on natural properties. If so, supervenience needs no further metaphysical explanation; it is a truth about our concepts, not about the world. The realist can accept this as a conceptualist answer without becoming an expressivist. |
| **The grounding approach (Fine, Rosen)** | Realists can deploy the metaphysics of grounding: moral properties are *grounded in* natural properties without being *identical to* them. Grounding is a non-causal, metaphysical determination relation that provides the needed necessary connection without violating Hume's dictum. Supervenience is the modal shadow of grounding. |
| **The explanatory demand may be illegitimate** | Why must supervenience be *explained* rather than accepted as a primitive modal fact? Realists can argue that every theory has primitives, and that supervenience is no more mysterious than any other necessary truth (e.g., logical truths). The explanatory demand may be question-begging if it assumes that only expressivism can answer it. |
| **Expressivism has its own explanatory burdens** | Expressivism must explain why moral supervenience feels like a necessary constraint rather than a contingent feature of our practices. If supervenience is merely a constraint on coherent moralizing, why does it present itself as a metaphysical necessity? Realism accounts for the phenomenology better. |
| **The triviality objection (Dreier)** | Dreier argues that supervenience might be a trivial truth — of course if two things share all properties, they share moral properties. The real challenge is to specify *which* subset of natural properties grounds moral properties, not to explain supervenience tout court. |

## Famous Cases

| Proponent | Framework | Key Move |
|-----------|-----------|----------|
| Blackburn (1971, 1984, 1993) | Quasi-realism / projectivism | Supervenience as a constraint on moralizing; explains it without metaphysical mystery |
| Dreier (1992) | Metaethics | The supervenience argument against realism; also the triviality challenge |
| McPherson (2012) | Metaethics | The "robust supervenience" challenge aimed specifically at non-naturalist realism |
| Jackson (1998) | Analytical functionalism | Supervenience as a conceptual truth — the realist's analytic answer |
| Fine (2001) / Rosen (2010) | Metaphysics of grounding | Grounding explains supervenience; modal covariance is the shadow of non-modal dependence |

## Reconstruction Notes

- Supervenience is a modal thesis — it is about covariance across possible worlds. In reconstruction, distinguish **weak supervenience** (within a world) from **strong supervenience** (across all possible worlds). Blackburn's argument requires at least strong supervenience.
- The argument's logical form is abduction, not deduction (see `inference-to-best-explanation.md`). The conclusion is that expressivism explains supervenience *better*, not that realism is inconsistent with supervenience.
- The **implicit premise** is: `[IP] Necessary covariance between distinct realms of properties requires explanation — it cannot be accepted as brute.`
- When reconstructing, note that Blackburn's full project (quasi-realism) attempts to *earn back* the realist-seeming features of moral discourse (truth-aptness, objectivity) from an expressivist starting point. The supervenience argument is one step in this larger project, not a standalone refutation of realism.
- The argument is closely related to the **argument from queerness** (see `queerness-argument.md`): the necessary connection between natural and moral properties is one species of metaphysical queerness.
