# Practical Reasoning

## Form

### Basic Structure (Aristotelian Practical Syllogism)

```
[1] I aim to achieve goal G (desire G)
[2] Action M is the (best) means to achieve G
∴ [3] I ought to do M
```

### Humean Internalist Form

```
[1] I have a desire / preference for X
[2] Y is a means of satisfying that desire / preference
∴ [3] I have reason to do Y
```

### Universalized Form (Kantian)

```
[1] For any rational agent R: if R has goal G, then R ought to take the necessary means M to achieve G
[2] I am a rational agent, and I have goal G
[3] M is a necessary means to achieve G
∴ [4] I ought to do M
```

## Core Philosophical Disputes

### The Means-End Relation

The most central premise of practical reasoning is the **means-end relation**. This relation is philosophically contested:

| Position | Representative | Principle |
|----------|---------------|-----------|
| Instrumental Rationalism | Hume | Reason only transmits desire; it does not generate new reasons for action |
| Kantian Rationalism | Kant | Reason can generate normative reasons independently of desire |
| Expressivism | Blackburn, Gibbard | "Ought to do M" expresses an attitude toward taking an action, not a description of fact |
| Aristotelian Virtue Ethics | Anscombe, Foot | Practical rationality cannot be captured by a purely inferential framework; requires the context of character |

### Practical Reasoning and Moral Reasons

The key role of practical reasoning in ethics and the philosophy of action:
- **Prudential reasoning**: Why does "I desire G" entail "I ought to do M"? (The "is/ought" gap)
- **Determinism**: Practical reasoning presupposes free will (see `phil-identity-persistence`)
- **Rational choice**: Practical reasoning vs. utility maximization
- **The motive / reason distinction**: Does practical reasoning supply reasons or describe motives?

## Evaluation Criteria

| Dimension | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Means-sufficiency** | Can M genuinely achieve G? (Causal / conceptual / logical sufficiency) |
| **Means-uniqueness** | Are there better alternative means M'? (If a superior means exists, the practical inference does not hold) |
| **Desirability of the goal** | Is goal G itself reasonable? (Higher-order goal legitimacy) |
| **Acceptability of counterpart premises** | Especially in obligatory actions: is the goal shared by all? |

## Reconstruction Notes

- **Most critically, mark where the "means-end" inferential leap occurs in the argument**. Not every action-to-goal inference is practical reasoning.
- Distinguish **descriptive premises** ("M is a means to achieve G") from **normative premises** ("I ought to achieve G") -- this is the core leap from fact to value.
- The implicit premise is typically: **there is no better alternative means M'** (see `phil-premise-detect`).
- Kantian practical reasoning is entirely different: it proceeds from the universal conditions of rationality and does not depend on particular goals.
