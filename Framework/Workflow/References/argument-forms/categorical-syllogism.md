# Categorical Syllogism

## Form

### Basic Structure

```
[1] All M are P    (Major premise)
[2] All S are M    (Minor premise)
∴ [3] All S are P  (Conclusion)
```

(Barbara, AAA-1, valid)

### Three Proposition Types

| Type | Label | Form | Meaning |
|------|-------|------|---------|
| Universal Affirmative | A | All S are P | ∀x(Sx → Px) |
| Universal Negative | E | No S are P | ∀x(Sx → ¬Px) |
| Particular Affirmative | I | Some S are P | ∃x(Sx ∧ Px) |
| Particular Negative | O | Some S are not P | ∃x(Sx ∧ ¬Px) |

### Common Valid Moods

| Mood | Major Premise | Minor Premise | Conclusion |
|------|---------------|---------------|------------|
| Barbara (AAA-1) | All M are P | All S are M | All S are P |
| Celarent (EAE-1) | No M are P | All S are M | No S are P |
| Darii (AII-1) | All M are P | Some S are M | Some S are P |
| Ferio (EIO-1) | No M are P | Some S are M | Some S are not P |

## Application in Philosophical Analysis

Categorical syllogisms rarely appear as strict formal structures in contemporary analytic philosophy, but:
- **Everyday arguments** still make heavy use of this structure, especially "All X are Y" forms.
- **Definitional arguments** often use this structure: All X by definition contain Y → a particular X is Y.
- **Classification arguments** (e.g., natural kind arguments) are built on this foundation.

## Reconstruction Notes

- Categorical claims in ordinary language often lack explicit quantifiers: "X is Y" → is this universal or particular? This is a crucial implicit premise.
- One must check whether the middle term (M) is distributed -- syllogistic fallacies typically arise from an undistributed middle.
- A common missing-premise pattern: the original text gives only "Some S are M" and "No M are P" and then infers "No S are P" (invalid: the middle term M is not distributed in the major premise).
