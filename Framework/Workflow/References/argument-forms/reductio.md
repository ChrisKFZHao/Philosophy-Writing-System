# Reductio ad Absurdum (Reductio)

## Form

```
[1] P            (Assumed premise -- the proposition to be refuted)
[2] …inferential steps…
[3] Q ∧ ¬Q       (A contradiction is derived)
∴ [4] ¬P         (Therefore, P is false)
```

Alternatively:

```
[1] P            (Assumption)
[2] …inference…
[3] Q            (Q is derived)
[4] ¬Q           (¬Q is also derived)
∴ [5] ¬P         (Therefore, P is false)
```

## Description

Reductio ad absurdum is a critically important argument form widely used in analytic philosophy. Its core strategy: assume that the proposition P to be refuted is true; through valid inferential steps, derive a contradiction (or a manifestly absurd conclusion) from it; thereby demonstrate that P cannot hold.

Strictly speaking, reductio is a specialized form of modus tollens (see modus-tollens.md), but its centrality in philosophy and its distinctive structure warrant independent treatment.

## Variants in Philosophical Analysis

### Reductio ad Ridiculum (Reduction to Absurdity)

```
[1] P
[2] …inference…
[3] R          (R is a manifestly absurd, unacceptable conclusion)
∴ [4] ¬P
```

Difference from logical contradiction: what is derived here is not `Q ∧ ¬Q`, but a philosophically unacceptable conclusion. The strength depends on the degree of absurdity of R.

### Inconsistency Argument (Reductio to Inconsistency)

Argues that a proposition P is inconsistent with other beliefs T that the author herself holds.

```
[1] P
[2] …inference…
[3] ¬T          (Not-T is derived)
[4] T           But T is explicitly affirmed by the author
∴ [5] ¬P
```

### Slippery Slope Reductio

```
[1] P (a concept with a vague boundary)
[2] If P holds for borderline case C₁, then there is no non-arbitrary way to deny P for C₂
[3] …and so on until Cₙ…
[4] Cₙ is a manifestly absurd case
∴ [5] Therefore, either reject P or provide a non-arbitrary boundary
```

## Reconstruction Notes

- The most critical element in reconstructing a reductio is **marking the position of the assumed premise** -- i.e., the proposition that is assumed and subsequently shown to entail a contradiction.
- One must explicitly label **the type of contradiction** (logical contradiction / absurd conclusion / conflict with known truths).
- The validity of a reductio depends on the necessity of each inferential step -- if even one step is not necessary, the reductio is weakened.
- Distinguish **reductio arguments** from **simple refutations**: the former shows that the assumption entails a contradiction, while the latter merely points out that the conclusion does not hold.

## Famous Cases in Analytic Philosophy

- **Plato**, *Euthyphro*: Is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because it is loved by the gods? → Derives a dilemma
- **Descartes**, Skeptical Doubt: The senses may deceive me → Derives unknowability
- **Putnam**, Brain-in-a-Vat Argument: Assume I am a brain in a vat → Derives a semantic contradiction
- **Williams**, Internal Reasons: Assume all reasons are internal → Conflicts with facts about moral motivation
- **Parfit**, Reductio of Personal Identity: Fission cases → Derives a contradiction between "not identical" and "not distinct"
