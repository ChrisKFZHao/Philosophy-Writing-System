# Argument from Moral Disagreement

## Form

### Core Structure — Mackie's Argument from Relativity

```
[1] There is widespread, persistent moral disagreement across cultures, eras, and
    individuals — on slavery, gender roles, distributive justice, the permissibility
    of abortion, the moral status of animals, etc.
[2] Such moral disagreement differs in kind from scientific disagreement:
    it persists even among knowledgeable, impartial, and rational reasoners with
    full non-moral information (peer disagreement among idealized observers)
[3] The best explanation of this persistent, intractable disagreement is that moral
    beliefs reflect culturally variable ways of life, emotional responses, and
    socialization — not objective moral facts that would be expected to produce
    convergence
∴ [4] Moral realism is false — there are no objective moral facts
    (or, more modestly: moral realism is unjustified, since the evidence of
    disagreement undercuts our entitlement to moral beliefs)
```

The argument has two main versions: **metaphysical** (concluding that moral facts do not exist) and **epistemological** (concluding that we are not justified in our moral beliefs). The latter, more modest version is often called the **conciliationist challenge**: when we encounter peers who disagree with us on moral matters after full reflection, we should reduce our confidence — and if this generalizes across all moral beliefs, moral knowledge is unattainable.

### Conciliationist Structure

```
[1] You and your epistemic peer disagree about moral proposition P after both have
    considered all available evidence and reasoning
[2] In cases of peer disagreement, you should suspend judgment or significantly
    reduce confidence about P (conciliationism)
[3] For virtually any substantive moral proposition P, there exists or could exist
    an epistemic peer who disagrees
∴ [4] You should suspend judgment about (almost) all substantive moral propositions
    — moral knowledge is impossible
```

## Distinctive Features

1. **Inference to the best explanation**: The argument is fundamentally abductive — it does not derive a contradiction from moral realism but claims that realism is *a worse explanation* of the observed disagreement than anti-realism. This makes it vulnerable to competing explanations.

2. **Two distinct targets**: The metaphysical version targets the truth of moral realism; the epistemological version targets the justification of moral beliefs. A realist can concede the epistemological point (our beliefs are less secure than we thought) without conceding the metaphysical one (moral facts still exist).

3. **The idealization problem**: The argument requires that disagreement persists under *idealized* conditions (full information, full rationality). But we have limited access to ideal disagreement — actual disagreement may reflect cognitive biases, limited information, or partiality. The argument's strength depends on how much idealization is legitimate.

4. **Anthropological and philosophical dimensions**: The empirical premise draws on cultural anthropology (the diversity of moral codes); the philosophical premise draws on the persistence of disagreement among professional ethicists. Both are contestable.

## Evaluation Criteria / Objections

| Objection | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Convergence arguments** | Moral disagreement is less deep than it appears. Across cultures, there is convergence on core values (harm, fairness, loyalty, authority, purity — Moral Foundations Theory) and disagreement is often about *application* to specific cases, not about fundamental principles. Real convergence suggests a common moral reality. |
| **Disagreement in non-moral domains** | Persistent disagreement exists in philosophy, metaphysics, and even interpretive areas of science. If disagreement does not force nihilism about these domains, why should it force nihilism about morality? (This is itself a companions-in-guilt move.) |
| **Moral progress** | The fact of moral progress (the abolition of slavery, the expansion of rights) is better explained by realism — we are *discovering* moral truths, not merely changing our attitudes. Widespread disagreement at some time does not entail that no fact of the matter exists. |
| **The idealization objection** | Actual moral disagreement is rarely among fully informed, fully rational peers. When disagreement disappears upon reflection, it is not evidence against realism. The anti-realist must show that *idealized* disagreement persists — and this is hard to establish. |
| **The self-defeat worry** | If peer disagreement requires suspension of judgment, and if metaethicists disagree about the force of the disagreement argument itself, then the argument's proponents must suspend judgment about their own conclusion. |
| **Local vs. global skepticism** | Even if disagreement forces skepticism about *some* moral beliefs (contentious cases), it may not force skepticism about *all* moral beliefs (paradigmatic cases of harm). A local retreat is compatible with realism. |

## Famous Cases

| Proponent | Version | Key Claim |
|-----------|---------|-----------|
| Mackie (1977) | Metaphysical | Relativity best explained by anti-realism |
| Doris & Stich (2020) | Empirical | Cognitive science supports persistent disagreement |
| Tersman (2006) | Epistemological | Moral disagreement generates skeptical pressure |
| Enoch (2009) | Response | Disagreement is overrated; ideal convergence is possible |
| Shafer-Landau (2003) | Response | Convergence on core principles; disagreement is about application |

## Reconstruction Notes

- The argument's strength depends on whether the **idealization condition** can be satisfied without begging the question. If "full information" includes the moral facts themselves, the condition is trivial; if it excludes them, the anti-realist needs an independent account of what full non-moral information includes.
- Distinguish **first-order moral disagreement** (disagreement about specific moral claims) from **metaethical disagreement** (disagreement about the nature of morality). The argument typically targets the first, but may be extended to the second — with self-defeat worries.
- The implicit premise is often: `[IP] If there were objective moral facts, we would expect convergence among rational inquirers (as in science).`
- This is structurally close to an **inference to the best explanation** (see `inference-to-best-explanation.md`). In reconstruction, explicitly note when the author shifts between the metaphysical and epistemological readings.
