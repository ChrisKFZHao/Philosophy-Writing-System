# Fallacy & Logical Jump Catalog

> This file catalogs common implicit logical jumps and inferential fallacies, used for type identification and reference during implicit premise detection.

---

## I. Logical Jumps (for detecting implicit premises)

### J-1: Is-Ought Gap
- **Form**: From "X operates in manner Y" to "we ought to support X / act according to Y"
- **Detection Trigger**: Premises use descriptive language (is, has, does); conclusion contains normative vocabulary (should, ought, must)
- **Implicit Premise**: A normative principle bridging description and prescription
- **Source**: Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature*, III.i.1

### J-2: Possible-Actual Gap
- **Form**: From "X could occur" to "X will occur / has occurred"
- **Detection Trigger**: Premises use "possibly," "could," "might"; conclusion lacks such qualification
- **Implicit Premise**: The possibility has been factually realized

### J-3: Part-Whole Jump (Fallacy of Composition)
- **Form**: From a property of parts to a property of the whole
- **Detection Trigger**: Premise predicates attach to parts; conclusion predicates attach to the entire entity
- **Implicit Premise**: Properties of the whole are determined by properties of parts (no emergence assumed)
- **Note**: This jump may be valid for certain entities (where composition is additive), but must be made explicit

### J-4: Local-Global Jump
- **Form**: From a limited set of instances or cases to a principle applying universally
- **Detection Trigger**: Premises based on limited case set; conclusion claims universal validity
- **Implicit Premise**: The limited cases are representative; no exceptions will defeat the generalization

### J-5: Theoretical-Normative Jump
- **Form**: From a theoretical proof that X is true/false to "we should/should not act in a certain way"
- **Detection Trigger**: Abrupt transition from purely theoretical assertions to action recommendations
- **Implicit Premise**: A transition principle whereby theoretical analysis entails normative practice

## II. Fallacies (for naming known erroneous reasoning patterns)

### F-1: Begging the Question (*Petitio Principii*)
- **Definition**: The conclusion's content appears directly or indirectly in the premises
- **Form**: Premises require accepting the conclusion to be persuasive
- **Detection Trigger**: Challenging a premise requires challenging the conclusion itself

### F-2: False Dilemma (Bifurcation)
- **Definition**: Reducing a situation to (A ∨ B) when three, four, or more possibilities actually exist
- **Form**: Claiming "either P or Q, no other options," but hidden options R, S, ... exist
- **Detection Trigger**: "It must be that..." / "Either... or..." / "Otherwise it's..." where available options outnumber those presented

### F-3: Straw Person (*Straw Man*)
- **Definition**: Constructing a weakened or distorted version of an opponent's position, refuting that version, then claiming to have refuted the original position
- **Form**: Attacking A′ (≠ A), claiming to have refuted A
- **Detection Trigger**: The version of the argument under attack differs significantly from the author's actual argument

### F-4: Slippery Slope
- **Definition**: From accepting principle P, inferring inevitable bad outcome R, without demonstrating necessity
- **Form**: P → Q → R → ... → Z (bad), ∴ ¬P (but each intermediate step may be merely possible rather than necessary)
- **Detection Trigger**: Each arrow assumes necessity without argument

### F-5: Equivocation
- **Definition**: The same term carries different meanings at different stages of the argument, but the reasoning treats it as having a single meaning
- **Form**: Meaning of "X" in premises ≠ meaning of "X" in conclusion
- **Detection Trigger**: Polysemous terms used across different inferential steps

### F-6: *Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc*
- **Definition**: Because X occurred after Y, X is the cause of Y
- **Form**: X occurred after Y → X caused Y
- **Detection Trigger**: Temporal sequence treated as causal relation

### F-7: Appeal to Authority (*Argumentum ad Verecundiam*)
- **Definition**: Treating an authority figure's assertion as fact without evaluating the argument
- **Form**: Expert E says X → X is true
- **Detection Trigger**: Premise is "Authority X / celebrity / ancient philosopher says..." with no further argument

### F-8: Appeal to Nature
- **Definition**: From "this is natural / accords with nature" to "this is good / correct"
- **Form**: X is natural → X is good (special case of Is-Ought)
- **Detection Trigger**: Premises mention natural/biological/evolutionary states; conclusion contains normative content

### F-9: Fallacy of Composition / Division
- **Definition**: From properties of parts to properties of the whole (composition), or vice versa (division)
- **Form**: Parts have F → whole has F (composition); whole has F → parts have F (division)
- **Detection Trigger**: Predicates conflated across levels of aggregation (element-level vs. set-level)

### F-10: Appeal to Ignorance (*Argumentum ad Ignorantiam*)
- **Definition**: Because X has not been proven false, X is true (or vice versa)
- **Form**: ¬∃evidence(¬X) → X
- **Detection Trigger**: The argument's sole "evidence" is the absence of counterevidence

## III. Presupposition Types (for detecting existential and framework presuppositions)

### P-1: Entity Presupposition
- The argument presupposes the existence of a certain class of entities (physical objects, abstract objects, moral facts, etc.)
- If controversial in the argumentative context, this presupposition is an implicit premise

### P-2: Property/Universal Presupposition
- The argument presupposes the existence of a certain class of properties or universals ("courage," "temperature," etc.)
- Especially for controversial properties (e.g., "race," "gender," "essence"), this presupposition requires scrutiny

### P-3: Causal Presupposition
- The argument presupposes the direction, strength, or independence of causal relations
- Under Humean analysis, causation is not treated as an independent fundamental; it requires argumentative support

### P-4: Value Presupposition
- The argument presupposes certain value judgments ("happiness is good," "freedom is good")
- Presuppositions uncontroversial within a given value system may be accepted, but should be flagged

### P-5: Epistemic Presupposition
- The argument presupposes a certain cognitive capacity or knowledge condition ("we have perceptual access to the world," "we can obtain knowledge through reason," etc.)
- Skeptical challenges directly target these presuppositions

### P-6: Normative Presupposition
- The argument presupposes certain normative principles ("the right action is the one that maximizes duty-fulfillment," etc.)
- Most common in ethical debates

---

*This catalog does not exhaust all fallacy types, but covers the most common implicit jumps in philosophical argumentation. New fallacies will be added periodically.*
