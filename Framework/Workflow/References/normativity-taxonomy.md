# TAXONOMY OF NORMATIVITY THEORIES

## INTRODUCTION

Normativity — the "ought"-ness, reason-giving force, or bindingness that
characterizes moral, prudential, epistemic, and instrumental domains — is a
central puzzle in metaethics: what makes normative claims true or binding, and
where does this force come from?

This taxonomy covers eleven families of views, each entry providing the core
thesis, key proponents, signature arguments, and central objections. A
structural comparison table maps theories across six diagnostic dimensions.
Designed for the `phil-normativity` and `phil-moral-psychology` domains.

## 1. REASONS FUNDAMENTALISM

### Core Thesis

Normative *reasons* are the fundamental, unanalyzable normative unit. Concepts
such as "ought," "good," and "value" are explained in terms of reasons, not the
other way around. "Being a reason for" is the primitive normative relation.

### Key Proponents

- **Derek Parfit** (*On What Matters*, 2011, 2017): Reasons are primitive,
  irreducible, non-ontological normative truths. Normative truths do not "exist"
  in the way rocks or electrons exist — they are non-spatiotemporal, non-causal
  truths. Reductive accounts fail; the concept is too basic for non-circular
  analysis.

- **T. M. Scanlon** (*What We Owe to Each Other*, 1998; *Being Realistic About
  Reasons*, 2014): "Being a reason for" is the basic normative relation. A
  reason is a consideration that *counts in favor*. Scanlon defends "quietist
  realism" — normative truths are irreducibly normative but do not require
  ontological commitment to non-natural facts.

### Signature Moves

- **The Buck-Passing Account of Value** (Scanlon): "X is good" = "X has
  properties that provide reasons to respond favorably to X." Goodness does not
  itself provide independent normative weight — reasons carry the load.

- **Parfit's Triviality Argument**: Any attempt to define "normative reason"
  in non-normative terms either changes the subject or is circular, analogous
  to the concept of *truth* in some semantic theories.

### Objections

- **Circularity worry**: Reasons fundamentalism may presuppose rather than
  explain normativity. "You ought to φ because you have decisive reason to φ"
  restates rather than solves the problem.
- **Explanatory gap**: If reasons are primitive, the view offers no explanation
  of why certain facts are reasons rather than others.
- **Epistemological access**: If normative truths are non-causal and
  non-ontological, how do we know them? Parfit appeals to rational intuition,
  which faces its own challenges.
- **Counterexamples to buck-passing**: Some argue goodness does independent
  normative work — the goodness of pleasure seems to *ground* reasons, not
  merely to consist in them (Crisp, Lemos).

## 2. CONSTITUTIVISM

### Core Thesis

Normative standards arise from *constitutive features* of agency, practical
identity, or the nature of action. To act at all is to be committed to norms
that make action possible — they are constitutive of action, not externally
imposed.

### Key Proponents

- **Christine Korsgaard** (*The Sources of Normativity*, 1996; *Self-Constitution*,
  2009): The source of normativity is our *practical identity* as reflective
  agents. Acting requires unifying ourselves under a practical identity;
  therefore we are committed to the categorical imperative as constitutive of
  action.

- **David Velleman** (*The Possibility of Practical Reason*, 2000; *How We Get
  Along*, 2009): The constitutive aim of action is *self-understanding* — an
  agent acts in order to know what she is doing.

- **Michael Smith** ("A Constitutivist Theory of Reasons," 2013): The
  constitutive aim of belief is truth; the constitutive aim of desire is
  coherence with one's idealized rational self. These constitutive aims
  generate normative standards without external metaphysics.

### Signature Moves

- **Korsgaard's Regress Argument**: Any normative principle can be challenged
  with "Why should I follow it?" The regress must terminate in a principle
  *constitutive* of the activity of asking for reasons — inescapable because
  rejecting it is not an alternative but a failure to engage in practical reasoning.
- **Self-Constitution Model**: Action requires the agent to constitute herself
  as a unified agent across time, imposing formal constraints (universalizability,
  consistency) that yield substantive normative conclusions.
- **Velleman's Reflective Knowledge**: The constitutive aim of action is to know
  what one is doing — this explains why deliberation terminates in action.

### Objections

- **Enoch's "Shmagency" Objection** (2006): Why care about being an agent at
  all? If I can coherently want not to be an agent (preferring "shmagency"),
  constitutivism cannot show agency's norms bind me. The norms are escapable.
- **Wrong results**: Formal constraints of agency (consistency, universalizability)
  seem too thin to yield substantive moral principles — they risk being empty
  or overreaching.
- **Conditional fallacy**: "If you are engaged in action, you are committed to
  norm N" leaves open whether one has reason to engage in action at all.
- **Empirical underdetermination**: Different constitutivists identify different
  constitutive aims (self-understanding, self-constitution, autonomy), and it is
  unclear how to adjudicate without independent normative standards.


## 3. HUMEAN INSTRUMENTALISM

### Core Thesis

All normativity is *hypothetical*. "You ought to φ" is elliptical for "You
ought to φ *if* you desire some end that φ-ing serves." Normative reasons are
grounded in an agent's contingent desires, goals, or motivational set. There
are no categorical reasons.

### Key Proponents

- **David Hume** (*Treatise*, 1739–40): Reason is the slave of the passions.
  "'Tis not contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the whole world to
  the scratching of my finger."

- **Bernard Williams** ("Internal and External Reasons," 1981): *Internal
  reasons* — "A has reason to φ" only if φ-ing can be reached by a *sound
  deliberative route* from A's subjective motivational set S. All genuine
  reasons are internal; "external reasons" are a myth.

- **Mark Schroeder** (*Slaves of the Passions*, 2007): *Hypotheticalism* —
  "R is a reason for A to φ" = "R explains why φ-ing promotes at least one
  of A's desires." Weight of reasons cashed out naturalistically via desire
  strength, degree of promotion, and number of desires promoted.

### Signature Moves

- **Williams's Internal Reasons Argument**: If a reason is genuinely normative
  for A, A must be capable of being motivated by it. Sound deliberation can only
  work on materials already in A's motivational set. Therefore, all reasons are
  internal.
- **Hume's Fork**: Practical reasoning is either instrumental (means-to-end) or
  not reasoning at all. No "pure practical reason" generates ends from reason
  alone.
- **Schroeder's Weighing Mechanism**: Reason weight is a function of desire
  strength, promotion degree, and desire count — all naturalistic.

### Objections

- **Categorical reasons**: Moral reasons (not to torture), prudential reasons
  (caring about one's future self despite indifference), and epistemic reasons
  (believing on the evidence) seem irreducibly categorical.
- **Williams's "desire" too thin**: If S includes projects, commitments, and
  dispositions, internalism risks vacuity — any reason connects to *some*
  element of S with enough ingenuity.
- **Conditional fallacy** (Shope, Johnson): If "A has reason to φ" is
  conditional on desire D, discovering that reason cannot itself be a reason to
  acquire D. But intuitively it can.
- **"Too few reasons"** (for Williams): Agents with impoverished motivational
  sets (indifference to future self) seem to have reasons they lack on
  Williams's account.
- **"Too many reasons"** (for Schroeder): Bad desires (sadistic, self-destructive)
  generate reasons to fulfill them. Schroeder accepts this but says they are
  outweighed — a controversial bullet to bite.


## 4. KANTIAN RATIONALISM

### Core Thesis

Normativity arises from the *structure of practical reason itself* — not from
external facts, and not from contingent desires. Reason can generate ends and
normative commitments independently of desire. The authority of norms comes
from their being principles that pure practical reason gives to itself.

### Key Proponents

- **Immanuel Kant** (*Groundwork*, 1785; *Critique of Practical Reason*, 1788):
  The categorical imperative is a *synthetic a priori* principle of pure
  practical reason. The moral law is the form rational willing takes when it
  governs itself independently of inclination.

- **Christine Korsgaard** (Kant-interpretation emphasis): The will's
  self-legislation is the source of normativity; autonomy is the property of
  the will by which it is a law to itself.

- **R. Jay Wallace** (*Normativity and the Will*, 2006; *The Moral Nexus*,
  2019): Develops a Kantian account of responsibility and the relational
  character of moral obligation.

### Signature Moves

- **The Fact of Reason** (*Faktum der Vernunft*): The moral law is a datum of
  rational consciousness — not inferred but the ground of moral reasoning.
- **The Autonomy Thesis**: The will is not merely subject to norms but their
  *author* — distinguishing Kant from Platonism (norms discovered) and
  Humeanism (norms slave to desire).
- **The Formula of Humanity**: Rational nature as end-in-itself with
  unconditional dignity — a synthetic a priori normative truth.

### Objections

- **Overdetermination**: If reason necessarily legislates one moral law,
  practical pluralism (competing principles, incommensurable values, tragic
  choices) is hard to accommodate.
- **Pluralism about practical reason**: Instrumental, prudential, moral, and
  aesthetic deliberation may not reduce to a single set of principles from a
  unified faculty of "practical reason."
- **Content problem**: The maxim test yields false negatives (too permissive) or
  false positives (too restrictive). If derivation fails, normative content is
  in doubt.
- **Transcendental idealism**: Kant's metaethics is embedded in transcendental
  idealism. Whether normative conclusions survive rejecting that framework is
  contested.


## 5. SMITHIAN RATIONALISM (THE NORMATIVE REASONS APPROACH)

### Core Thesis

What an agent has normative reason to do is what the agent's *fully rational
counterpart* would desire or advise the actual agent to do. Normativity is
analyzed via the responses of an idealized version of the agent.

### Key Proponent

- **Michael Smith** (*The Moral Problem*, 1994): "A has normative reason to φ
  in C" = "A's fully rational counterpart would desire that A φ-s in C." The
  fully rational counterpart is A after full information, maximal coherence,
  and correct deliberation — "the systematic justification of desires."

### Signature Moves

- **The Moral Problem**: Reconcile (1) moral judgments express beliefs
  (cognitivism), (2) moral judgments are intrinsically motivating (internalism),
  (3) beliefs alone cannot motivate (Humean psychology). Solution: moral
  judgments are beliefs about what our fully rational selves would desire.
- **Advice model vs. example model**: The advice model says "A has reason to φ
  because A's ideal self *advises* A to φ" (the ideal self may diverge from
  what it does). The example model says "A has reason to φ because φ-ing would
  make A more like A's ideal self." Smith defends the advice model.
- **Coherence theory of justification**: Desires are justified as a coherent
  set, corrected for full information — a coherence theory of normative
  justification.

### Objections

- **Thinness objection**: Is "fully rational" (informed + coherent) robust
  enough to exclude immoral desires that are coherent under full information?
  What if the rational psychopath desires harm?
- **Internalism undermined**: If the ideal self wants the actual self to φ,
  this is the desire of *another* — it does not guarantee motivational uptake
  in the actual self.
- **Normative indeterminacy**: If "fully rational" is purely procedural,
  multiple incompatible coherent desire sets may exist, yielding conflicting
  advice. Smith needs a uniqueness assumption (the "convergence thesis") that
  may not hold.
- **Parfit's objection**: The ideal-advisor account is "sophisticated
  subjectivism" — normativity still depends on contingent starting desires.
  This misrepresents the objectivity of at least some normative truths.


## 6. DARWALLIAN SECOND-PERSON STANDPUNKT

### Core Thesis

Moral normativity — specifically the normativity of *obligation* — derives from
*second-personal address*. When one person makes a claim on another (a demand,
request, accusation), they invoke a distinctively second-personal framework:
they presuppose authority to hold the other accountable and address the other
as someone who can recognize and respond to reasons. This standpoint is
irreducible to first-personal (deliberative) or third-personal (observer) sources.

### Key Proponent

- **Stephen Darwall** (*The Second-Person Standpoint*, 2006; *Morality,
  Authority, and Law*, 2013): Moral obligation is tied to *accountability* —
  what we owe to each other as equal members of the moral community.
  Second-personal reasons are reasons whose validity depends on presupposed
  authority relations between persons.

### Signature Moves

- **Irreducibility thesis**: Second-personal reasons cannot be reduced to
  first-personal (deliberative) or third-personal (impartial value) reasons.
  The reason to stop stepping on my foot when I *demand* it differs in kind
  from the reason deriving from the badness of pain — it is relational and
  authority-mediated.
- **Pufendorf-Fichte-Strawson lineage**: Pufendorf (obligation presupposes
  divine authority to command), Fichte (summons/*Aufforderung* as foundation
  of right), Strawson (*Freedom and Resentment*: reactive attitudes are
  second-personal responses to violations of interpersonal demands).
- **The circle of moral concepts**: Obligation, accountability, authority,
  and the reactive attitudes (resentment, guilt, indignation) form an
  interdefinable circle — no member is more fundamental. Moral obligation
  just *is* that which it makes sense to hold people accountable for.

### Objections

- **Can second-personal reasons be fundamental?**: Presupposed authority needs
  normative grounding — "Why does your demand give me a reason?" cannot be
  answered by "Because I have the authority to demand it."
- **Scope limitation**: Best suited to deontological obligations; less natural
  for duties to self, non-persons, impersonal values, and supererogation.
- **Authority circle objection**: If second-personal concepts form a tight
  circle, the view illuminates connections but fails to provide *grounding* —
  it tells us how concepts relate, not why they have normative force.


## 7. SCANLONIAN CONTRACTUALISM AS METAETHICS

### Core Thesis

The nature of moral *wrongness* is explained by contractualist structure: an
act is wrong if it would be disallowed by any set of principles that no one
could *reasonably reject* as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement.
This is not merely a criterion for wrongness (normative contractualism) but an
account of the nature of moral wrongness and moral motivation (metaethical
contractualism).

### Key Proponent

- **T. M. Scanlon** (*What We Owe to Each Other*, 1998): Contractualism
  characterizes "what we owe to each other" — the distinctively moral domain.
  Moral motivation is the motivation of being able to justify one's conduct to
  others on grounds they could not reasonably reject.

### Signature Moves

- **Reasonable vs. rational rejection**: Reasonable rejection is determined by
  generic reasons anyone in one's position would have, not by what serves one's
  interests. This distinguishes Scanlon from Hobbesian contractarianism (mutual
  advantage) and from utilitarian aggregation (summing individual complaints).
- **Motivation of justifiability**: The desire to justify one's actions to others
  is a direct normative concern partly constitutive of the interpersonal
  relationship morality governs — not an external sanction.
- **Delimiting "the moral"**: Contractualism is restricted to "what we owe to
  each other" — narrower than the good or the valuable — avoiding the objection
  that it must account for all normativity.

### Objections

- **Does the structure need grounding?**: Why do facts about reasonable rejection
  *generate* moral obligations? The test presupposes persons have standing to
  make claims on each other — a normative fact the structure does not explain.
- **Indeterminacy**: For contested moral questions, reasonable people disagree
  about which principles could be reasonably rejected — limiting the theory's value.
- **"Wrong kind of reasons"**: Some rejection-grounding considerations (trivial
  burdens) seem of the wrong *kind* — filtering them may require independent
  normative machinery.
- **Demandingness**: The low threshold for reasonable rejection when serious harm
  is at stake can generate very demanding obligations.


## 8. REALIST THEORIES (NON-NATURALIST)

### Core Thesis

Normative facts exist *independently* of any agent's attitudes, desires, or
practical commitments. They are part of the fundamental fabric of reality
(robust realism) or are irreducible, non-causal truths (non-naturalist realism).
Normativity is *discovered*, not created. Normative properties are *sui generis*
— not identical to, reducible to, or supervenient upon natural properties in a
metaphysically explanatory way.

### Key Proponents

- **David Enoch** (*Taking Morality Seriously*, 2011): "Robust realism" —
  objective, non-natural normative truths are part of reality in a full-blooded
  sense. Known through rational intuition refined by reflective equilibrium.

- **Derek Parfit** (*On What Matters*, vol. 3, 2017): *Non-ontological*
  normative realism: "There are normative truths, but they don't exist in the
  way rocks exist." They lack spatiotemporal location and causal powers, yet
  are objectively true.

- **Russ Shafer-Landau** (*Moral Realism: A Defence*, 2003): Non-naturalist
  moral realism with comprehensive response to standard objections. Moral facts
  are constituted by but not reducible to natural facts.

### Signature Moves

- **Objectivity argument** (Parfit, Enoch): Normative truths have the same
  objectivity as mathematical truths — "Agony is bad" is no more
  attitude-contingent than "2 + 2 = 4." Burden of proof is on the skeptic.
- **Enoch's indispensability argument**: Practical deliberation commits us to
  normative truths in parallel to how science commits us to mathematics (Quine-Putnam).
- **Companions in guilt**: Normativity is no more "queer" than modality,
  mathematics, or meaning — all face parallel challenges.
- **Non-ontological maneuver** (Parfit): Denying normative truths are "entities"
  sidesteps metaphysical objections while retaining normative objectivity.

### Objections

- **Queerness** (Mackie, *Ethics*, 1977): Non-natural facts would be "of a
  very strange sort" — metaphysically queer (non-causal) and epistemologically
  queer (requiring "special faculty" of intuition). Best explanation: no such
  facts exist; our "knowledge" is projection.
- **Epistemological challenge**: If normative facts are non-causal, how do we
  know them? Rational intuition is mysterious; causal epistemology rules out
  non-causal access.
- **Supervenience without explanation**: If sui generis normative properties
  supervene on natural ones, this brute necessary connection between distinct
  kinds is an unexplained metaphysical posit.
- **Darwinian challenge** (Street, 2006; see Section 10): Evolution-shaped
  judgments tracking fitness, not truth, create an implausible coincidence.
- **Disagreement**: If fundamental normative truths are self-evident to
  intuition, pervasive disagreement is hard to explain without attributing
  irrationality to dissenters.


## 9. EXPRESSIVIST THEORIES OF NORMATIVITY

### Core Thesis

Normative language and thought do not primarily *describe* normative facts.
They *express* non-cognitive attitudes (desires, plans, acceptance of norms)
and prescribe action. Expressivism is non-cognitivism about normative judgment,
combined with a sophisticated account of how normative language exhibits
truth-aptness, logical embedding, and inference.

### Key Proponents

- **Allan Gibbard** (*Wise Choices, Apt Feelings*, 1990; *Thinking How to
  Live*, 2003): Normative statements express *plans* or *acceptance of norms*.
  "X is wrong" expresses the speaker's plan to avoid X and blame those who do
  not. Later work develops formal semantics using fact-plan worlds.

- **Simon Blackburn** (*Essays in Quasi-Realism*, 1993; *Ruling Passions*,
  1998): *Quasi-realism* — the project of showing expressivists can "earn the
  right" to talk as if there are normative facts, truths, and knowledge, while
  maintaining that the underlying function of normative discourse is expressive.
  Normativity is "projected" onto the world, but the projection is legitimate
  and indispensable.

### Signature Moves

- **Expressivist analysis of judgment**: To think φ is wrong is to be in a
  conative state — *world-guiding* rather than *world-guided* — explaining the
  judgment-motivation connection without positing special motivational facts.
- **Quasi-realism** (Blackburn): Expressivists earn realist talk one move at a
  time — "normative truth" as the proper object of attitudes under ideal
  conditions, "normative knowledge" as the state achieved through proper
  deliberation, "correctness" in a deflationary sense.
- **Frege-Geach response**: Gibbard's "fact-plan worlds" model treats normative
  content as a function from factual possibilities to plan-states, enabling
  embedding semantics.

### Objections

- **Frege-Geach problem**: Normative sentences embed in truth-functional contexts
  — negation ("It is *not* wrong"), conditionals ("*If* lying is wrong, getting
  your brother to lie is wrong"). If unembedded "lying is wrong" expresses an
  attitude, what is expressed when embedded? Expressivists need compositional
  semantics without truth-conditional content.
- **Creeping realism**: If quasi-realism earns all realist moves (truth,
  objectivity, independence), what distinguishes it from realism? Either it
  collapses into realism or fails to earn some crucial commitment.
- **Negative existentials**: "Nothing is wrong" and "There are no moral facts"
  (coherent, endorsed by error theorists) are hard to express — they require
  denying the attitudes constituting normative thought.
- **Disagreement**: If normative statements express varying attitudes, explaining
  genuine *disagreement* where one party is *wrong* is hard — the view risks
  sliding into "you have your attitudes, I have mine."


## 10. NORMATIVITY AND EVOLUTION

### Core Thesis

Understanding the *genealogy* of normative thought — how creatures like us came
to have normative concepts and judgments — is essential to evaluating the
metaphysical and epistemological status of normativity. Evolutionary explanations
offer either a *debunking* argument (normative judgments track fitness, not truth)
or open space for a *vindicating* argument (truth-tracking and fitness can
coincide).

### Key Proponents

- **Sharon Street** ("A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value,"
  2006): Either (a) evolutionary forces shaped our normative judgments (best
  explanation doesn't involve their truth — debunking), or (b) they didn't
  (massive coincidence that judgments track mind-independent truths). Either
  way, realism is in trouble.

- **Richard Joyce** (*The Evolution of Morality*, 2006): Evolutionary genealogy
  debunks moral judgments — the best explanation of why we hold them doesn't
  require their truth. Joyce concludes moral error theory combined with
  fictionalism (retain moral discourse as a useful social fiction).

### Signature Moves

- **Street's Darwinian dilemma**: Either evolution shaped our judgments (best
  explanation doesn't involve their truth — debunking) or it didn't (massive
  coincidence that judgments track mind-independent truths). Realists must find
  a way between the horns.
- **Tracking vs. adaptive-link**: The tracking account says judgments are
  truth-tracking; the adaptive-link account says they are fitness-tracking
  (ancestral judgments promoting reproductive success were reinforced). Street
  argues the adaptive-link account is far more plausible.
- **Joyce's fictionalism**: Even if moral judgments are systematically false,
  moral discourse is worth retaining as a useful social fiction.

### Objections

- **Third-factor responses** (Enoch, Shafer-Landau, Copp): Survival/reproduction
  *are genuinely good* — a third factor explains both why evolution favored
  pursuing them and why we judge them good, avoiding both horns.
- **Capacity vs. content**: Evolution may explain our *capacity* for normative
  judgment but not the *content* of specific judgments (from cultural evolution,
  reflection, or direct access) — none trigger debunking.
- **Self-defeat worry**: If debunking applies to *all* normative judgments, it
  applies to the argument's own premises — self-undermining.
- **Anti-realism avoids the dilemma**: Constitutivists, expressivists, and
  Humeans can accept the adaptive-link account without debunking — their views
  don't require tracking independent facts. The challenge targets realism
  specifically, not normativity as such.

### Table Notes

- **Internalist/Externalist** tracks *reasons internalism* (whether reasons depend
  on the agent's motivational makeup), not *judgment internalism*. Expressivists
  are judgment internalists; their reasons-internalism commitments depend on how
  they reconstruct "reasons"-talk.
- **Categorical/Hypothetical**: Categorical normativity applies regardless of
  contingent desires; hypothetical is conditional on them. Smithian rationalism
  blurs the line.
- **Quasi-realist expressivism** may differ from "pure" expressivism; the table
  reflects *fundamental* commitments.
- **Evolutionary approaches** (Section 10) resist classification on most
  dimensions — included for completeness.
