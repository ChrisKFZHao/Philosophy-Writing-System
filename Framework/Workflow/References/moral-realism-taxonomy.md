# Moral Realism: A Taxonomy of Positions

## Introduction: The Spectrum, Not the Binary

The question "Are there objective moral facts?" is often posed as though the landscape divides
neatly into two camps: moral realists and moral anti-realists. This binary framing is a
pedagogical convenience at best. The actual logical space of metaethical positions forms a
**spectrum** -- a graded continuum from the most metaphysically robust realism, through
naturalizing, quietist, and constructivist intermediate positions, to fictionalism and
relativism at the far anti-realist edge.

The spectrum is organized around a master question: **How much metaphysical and epistemological
work must we do to earn the right to speak of moral truth?** Robust realists answer: a great
deal, but it can be done. Fictionalists answer: no work can earn that right, so we should
maintain moral discourse as a useful pretense. In between lie positions that attempt to secure
moral truth while paying progressively lower metaphysical costs.

Understanding this spectrum matters for philosophical practice: arguments that succeed against
one position often leave neighboring positions untouched. The argument from queerness is serious
for robust non-naturalism but has far less traction against quietist realism. The Frege-Geach
problem devastates simple emotivism but loses force against sophisticated quasi-realism.
Knowing *where* a position sits is essential to knowing *which* objections apply.

## 1. Robust Non-Naturalist Realism

**Core claims:** Moral properties exist as irreducible, non-natural, stance-independent
properties. Moral judgments express beliefs that are straightforwardly truth-apt. Moral truths
hold independently of any actual or idealized human attitudes, desires, or conventions.

**Variants:**
- **Moore (Principia Ethica, 1903):** Goodness is a simple, unanalyzable, non-natural property,
  grasped by a sui generis faculty of intuition. Any naturalistic definition commits the
  "naturalistic fallacy."
- **Shafer-Landau (Moral Realism: A Defence, 2003):** Moral facts exist as non-natural but
  non-mysterious constituents of reality. We access them through reflective equilibrium, not
  special intuition. Moral properties are constituted by but not reducible to natural properties
  (parallel to non-reductive physicalism).
- **Parfit (On What Matters, 2011/2017):** Normative truths are non-ontological and sui generis.
  They are not entities at all; they are irreducibly normative truths about reasons that do not
  require a special ontology. Parfit's "non-realist cognitivism" denies that normative truths
  need truth-makers.
- **Enoch (Taking Morality Seriously, 2011):** Moral truths are robustly objective --
  non-natural, irreducible, stance-independent. Defended via the deliberative indispensability
  argument.

**Strongest argument -- Deliberative indispensability (Enoch, 2011):**
```
P1: In deliberating, we are unavoidably committed to the existence of normative
    reasons not determined by our desires or attitudes.
P2: Unavoidable practical commitments provide epistemic justification.
C:  Therefore, we are justified in believing irreducibly normative reasons exist.
```
The argument is transcendental: it asks what must be the case for deliberation to be
intelligible. The answer is that there must be normative truths not of our own making.

**Central vulnerability -- The queerness argument (Mackie, 1977):**
- **Metaphysical queerness:** Objective values would have "to-be-doneness" built in --
  intrinsic prescriptivity found nowhere else in our ontology.
- **Epistemological queerness:** Non-natural, causally inert properties cannot be accessed
  through ordinary causal epistemology; a mysterious intuition-faculty would be required.
  Parfit's response (denying ontological weight) defuses one prong but leaves the
  epistemological prong intact: even if they are not entities, how do we *know* them?

## 2. Constitutivist Realism

**Core claims:** Moral truths are real but grounded in constitutive features of agency or
practical reason, not in a special ontology. The demand for metaphysical grounding of moral
truth is a mistake. Moral norms are constitutive of the very activity the skeptic engages in
when raising the skeptical challenge.

**Key distinction from robust realism:** Unlike robust realists, constitutivists deny that
moral truths require an independent metaphysical grounding story. The truth of moral claims is
*constituted by*, not merely tracked by, the structure of agency.

**Variants:**
- **Korsgaard (The Sources of Normativity, 1996; Self-Constitution, 2009):** Moral obligations
  are constitutive standards of agency. You cannot violate moral norms without undermining
  your own agency. The categorical imperative is the constitutive principle of action.
- **Velleman (The Possibility of Practical Reason, 2000):** The aim of action is
  intelligibility; the standards of intelligibility constitute practical reason.
- **Scanlon (Being Realistic about Reasons, 2014):** Irreducibly normative truths about reasons
  are fundamental and not "ontologically weighty." Scanlon's "reasons fundamentalism."
- **Dworkin (Justice for Hedgehogs, 2011):** Moral truths are interpretive truths, true in
  virtue of being the best interpretation of our moral concepts and practices. The
  "independence of value" thesis.

**Strongest argument -- The constitutivist strategy:**
You cannot coherently ask "Why should I be rational?" because the asking presupposes the norms
of practical reason. If moral norms are constitutive of agency, then the skeptical regress stops
-- not by positing a metaphysical fact, but by revealing that the skeptic's own activity
commits her to the norms she pretends to question.

**Central vulnerability -- Does this give us enough?**
The constitutivist can derive the *form* of moral law (universalizability) but struggles for
determinate *content*. The gap between thin constitutive norms (coherence, means-end
rationality) and thick morality (prohibitions on cruelty, requirements of beneficence) remains
wide. Enoch's (2006) dilemma: either the constitutive standards are truly inescapable (in which
case immorality is impossible -- absurd), or they can be violated (in which case they are not
truly constitutive, and skepticism re-emerges).

## 3. Cornell Realism (Synthetic Naturalist Realism)

**Core claims:** Moral properties are natural properties, but their moral nature is discovered
a posteriori through scientific methodology. Moral epistemology is continuous with scientific
epistemology. Moral properties play a genuine causal-explanatory role.

**Key philosophers:** Richard Boyd ("How to Be a Moral Realist," 1988), Nicholas Sturgeon
("Moral Explanations," 1985), David Brink (Moral Realism and the Foundations of Ethics, 1989).

**Key mechanism -- Homeostatic property cluster theory:** Moral properties are natural clusters
of properties that tend to co-occur in a homeostatic relationship. "Goodness" is not a single
natural property but a cluster (promoting welfare, satisfying desires, being approved by
impartial observers) whose members mutually support and stabilize one another.

**Strongest argument -- The causal regress argument:**
```
P1: Our moral beliefs are caused by moral facts.
P2: If moral beliefs are caused by moral facts, moral facts are causally
    efficacious natural properties.
C:  Therefore, moral realism (in a naturalistic form) is true.
```
Sturgeon: "Hitler was morally depraved" explains his actions in a way purely non-moral
descriptions cannot. Moral explanations are indispensable and genuine.

**Central vulnerability -- Causal efficacy of moral properties:**
The natural properties doing the causal work (causing suffering) are fully captured by
non-moral descriptions. The "moral overlay" does no additional explanatory work. If moral
properties are constituted by natural property-clusters, their causal power is the power of
their natural realizers -- but then why do we need moral vocabulary at all?

## 4. Analytic Functionalist Realism (Canberra Plan)

**Core claims:** Moral properties are defined by their functional role in folk moral theory,
then we scientifically identify what plays that role.

**Key philosophers:** Frank Jackson (From Metaphysics to Ethics, 1998), Michael Smith
(The Moral Problem, 1994).

**Methodology (three steps):**
1. **Assemble platitudes** from folk moral theory (morality is action-guiding, objective,
   supervenes on natural properties, etc.).
2. **Ramseyfy:** Replace moral terms with variables in the conjunction of platitudes, yielding
   a functional definition without moral vocabulary.
3. **Identify realizers:** Empirically discover what properties in the world satisfy the
   Ramsey sentence. Those properties *are* the moral properties.

Smith's version: moral facts are facts about what fully rational agents would desire.

**Strongest argument -- The location problem solved:**
The Canberra Plan shows how moral properties fit into a naturalistic world picture without
non-natural mystery or revisionary elimination. Morality turns out to be part of the natural
world -- just not in a way obvious from the surface grammar of moral language.

**Central vulnerability -- The permutation problem:**
The Ramsey sentence may have no unique realizer. Folk morality is complex, historically
contingent, and potentially inconsistent. Possible outcomes: (a) no realizer -- nothing
satisfies all platitudes; (b) multiple realizers -- arbitrary choice required; (c) wildly
disjunctive realizers -- no natural kind corresponds. Smith's idealization (full rationality)
moves the view toward revisionary rather than descriptive metaethics.

## 5. Reductive Naturalist Realism

**Core claims:** Moral properties are identical to natural properties via theoretical
identification (like water = H2O, temperature = mean molecular kinetic energy). The
identification is synthetic a posteriori -- discovered empirically, not by conceptual analysis.
Moral terms rigidly designate natural properties.

**Variants:**
- **Railton ("Moral Realism," 1986):** Moral goodness is what would be approved by a fully
  informed, impartial spectator -- a naturalistic reduction cashed out counterfactually.
- **Brandt (A Theory of the Good and the Right, 1979):** "Good" is what would survive cognitive
  psychotherapy -- what we would desire after maximal exposure to facts and logic. "Reforming
  definitions" preserve moral discourse's function while eliminating unsupported presuppositions.

**Strongest argument -- The water/H2O model:**
"Water" was understood superficially (clear liquid, fills lakes) before being identified with
H2O -- a discovery, not a conceptual truth. Moral terms work the same way: "good" is understood
by its role in discourse, but empirical investigation may reveal goodness *is* a natural
property. This gives realism without non-natural metaphysics.

**Central vulnerability -- Moore's open question argument:**
For any proposed naturalistic identification (good = pleasant), it is always an "open question"
whether the natural property really is good. "I know X is pleasant, but is it good?" seems
intelligible and non-trivial. If "good" and "pleasant" were co-referential, the question should
feel closed. The reductive naturalist replies that identity claims can be informative (water/H2O
was once open). But the open question for good/pleasant persists even after all empirical facts
are in, suggesting it is conceptual rather than empirical.

## 6. Response-Dependent Realism

**Core claims:** Moral properties are dispositions to elicit certain responses (approval,
disapproval) in suitable subjects under suitable conditions. They are analogous to secondary
qualities like colors -- real but essentially involving a relation to perceiving subjects.

**Key philosophers:** John McDowell ("Values and Secondary Qualities," 1985), David Wiggins
("A Sensible Subjectivism?", 1987), Mark Johnston ("Dispositional Theories of Value," 1989).

**The secondary-quality analogy:** Colors are both objective (the world is really colored) and
subjective (color is essentially a disposition to produce visual experiences in perceivers).
A world without perceivers has no colors, but that does not make color ascriptions false or
subjective. If moral properties are analogous, we can have realism without non-natural mystery
and without the reductionist's struggle for unique natural property identification.

McDowell's key move: disentangle objectivity from the elimination of reference to subjective
responses. Moral properties are "there to be experienced" but their nature essentially
references the responses of suitably situated subjects.

**Strongest argument -- Phenomenological and metaphysical parsimony:**
The analogy gains force from phenomenology: just as color experience presents color as
"out there" (even though response-dependent), moral experience presents value as "out there"
in the action. The view accommodates both the phenomenology of objectivity and the
metaphysical parsimony of naturalism.

**Central vulnerability -- The missing explanation problem:**
"Wrong" is supposed to explain why we disapprove. But if "wrong" means "such as to elicit
disapproval in suitable subjects," then "the action is such as to elicit disapproval" is
asked to explain "we disapprove of it" -- circular. McDowell replies that the relation is
*meriting*, not causal: the value merits the response. But then: what is it for a property
to *merit* a response, if not a non-response-dependent normative fact? If it is itself
response-dependent, the circle re-emerges; if not, a non-response-dependent fact has been
smuggled in.

## 7. Quietist Realism (Minimalist / Wittgensteinian)

**Core claims:** Moral discourse is truth-apt and often true, but this does not require a heavy
metaphysical apparatus. "Realism without metaphysics." The demand that moral truths be
"grounded" in something non-moral is a philosophical mistake.

**Key philosophers:** Ronald Dworkin (Justice for Hedgehogs, 2011), Matthew Kramer
(Moral Realism as a Moral Doctrine, 1999), T.M. Scanlon (Being Realistic about Reasons, 2014).

**The key move -- Rejecting the grounding demand:**
Moral truth is deflationary or disquotational. "It is true that lying is wrong" is equivalent
to "Lying is wrong." There is no further question about what *makes* it true. The distinction
between first-order moral claims and second-order metaethical claims is illusory: metaethics is
just ethics at a higher level of abstraction (Dworkin). Moral realism is a first-order *moral*
doctrine, not a metaphysical one (Kramer).

**Strongest argument -- The deflationist parallel:**
We do not demand metaphysical grounding of logical truths (modus ponens is valid) or
mathematical truths (2+2=4). Why should morality be different? The grounding demand is not a
neutral requirement of rational inquiry; it is a demand the skeptic imposes on morality, and
the quietist declines the burden.

**Central vulnerability -- Evasion, not answer:**
The quietist's response can look like a refusal to answer the skeptical challenge. The skeptic
asks: "Why think moral claims are true?" The quietist replies by rejecting the question's
presuppositions. There is a standing concern that quietism collapses into either (a) unearned
dogmatism that simply asserts moral truths, or (b) expressivism using realist language without
realist commitments. If any domain with minimal truth-aptitude qualifies for "realism," the
quietist's realism comes too cheaply.

## 8. Constructivism (Liminal Position)

**Core claims:** Moral truths are constructed by procedures of practical reasoning, not
discovered as pre-existing facts. The procedure, not independent facts, determines moral truth.
Constructivism occupies the liminal space between realism and anti-realism.

**Variants:**
- **Kantian constructivism (Rawls, "Kantian Constructivism in Moral Theory," 1980):** Moral
  principles are the outcome of the original position, which models free and equal moral
  persons. Principles are reasonable, not "true" in a correspondence sense.
- **Kantian constructivism (Korsgaard, The Sources of Normativity, 1996):** The categorical
  imperative is a procedure for testing maxims. The procedure constitutes moral truth.
- **Humean constructivism (Street, "What Is Constructivism in Ethics and Metaethics?", 2010):**
  The constructive procedure takes contingent evaluative attitudes as input. Moral truths are
  constructed from these attitudes. Street explicitly identifies as anti-realist.

**Is constructivism realism?**
- For realism: the procedure yields objectively correct answers; conformity is not optional for
  agents; the procedure constrains thinking beyond mere attitude-expression (Korsgaard, Schafer).
- Against realism: no normative facts exist prior to or independent of the constructive
  procedure; truth is constituted, not discovered (Street, Hussain & Shah).

**Strongest argument -- Practical reason's self-sufficiency:**
Constructivism secures objectivity without metaphysical cost. The constructive procedure
provides a standard of correctness internal to practical reasoning. Because practical reasoning
is inescapable, the resulting standards are inescapable -- no special ontology needed.

**Central vulnerability -- The input problem:**
If the procedure takes contingent attitudes as input (Humean), output is only as objective as
input -- different starting attitudes yield different moral conclusions, undermining
universality. If it does not (Kantian), output is formal and empty -- the procedure requires
consistency and universality but does not determine *what* to will. Too much content smuggled
in makes the view secretly realist; too little makes it insufficiently moral.

## 9. Error Theory (Anti-Realist Cognitivism)

**Core claims:** Moral claims are truth-apt (cognitivism) but systematically false (error).
The error is ontological: moral discourse presupposes objective, categorically prescriptive
facts, and no such facts exist.

**Key philosophers:** J.L. Mackie (Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong, 1977), Richard Joyce
(The Myth of Morality, 2001; The Evolution of Morality, 2006), Jonas Olson (Moral Error
Theory: History, Critique, Defence, 2014).

**Two canonical arguments:**
1. **Argument from queerness:** Objective values would be metaphysically queer (intrinsic
   prescriptivity) and epistemologically queer (requiring a mysterious intuition-faculty).
   Parsimony: theory that denies such entities explains the same data without the queer
   ontology.
2. **Argument from relativity:** Persistent, intractable moral disagreement is best explained
   by cultural conditioning and social pressures, not by perception of objective moral facts.

**Variants:** Revisionary (maintain moral discourse with revised understanding -- Mackie, Joyce)
vs. Eliminativist/abolitionist (abandon moral discourse entirely -- Garner, Hinckfuss).

**Strongest argument -- Parsimony:**
Error theory explains all the data (our having the moral beliefs we do) via evolutionary
pressures, cultural transmission, and psychology -- without objective values. Unless the
realist offers a compelling reason to prefer the richer ontology, rational parsimony favors
error theory.

**Central vulnerability -- Companions in guilt:**
If moral facts are queer, epistemic facts are equally queer -- they share the categorical,
irreducibly normative features that allegedly make moral facts problematic. But the error
theorist's own argument presupposes epistemic facts (evidence, justification, good reasoning).
The error theorist cannot consistently maintain that queerness defeats moral facts while
relying on epistemic facts (Cuneo, Shafer-Landau). This does not show error theory is false,
but that it is self-defeating.

## 10. Non-Cognitivism / Expressivism

**Core claims:** Moral sentences express non-cognitive attitudes (emotions, desires, norm
acceptances), not beliefs. Moral language is fundamentally practical -- to influence action,
express attitudes, coordinate behavior -- not to describe reality.

**Variants:**
- **Emotivism (Ayer, Language, Truth and Logic, 1936; Stevenson, Ethics and Language, 1944):**
  Moral statements express emotions and evoke emotions in others.
- **Prescriptivism (Hare, The Language of Morals, 1952; Freedom and Reason, 1963):**
  Moral statements are universal prescriptions. Universalizability provides objectivity without
  moral facts.
- **Norm-expressivism (Gibbard, Wise Choices, Apt Feelings, 1990):** Moral statements express
  acceptance of systems of norms. More cognitively integrated than simple emotivism.
- **Quasi-realism (Blackburn, Spreading the Word, 1984; Ruling Passions, 1998):** We can
  *earn the right* to talk as if moral realism were true. Projectivist expressivism: we project
  attitudes onto the world and legitimately use realist-seeming language (truth, fact,
  knowledge) within moral discourse.
- **Hybrid expressivism (Ridge, Impassioned Belief, 2014):** Moral statements express both a
  belief and a non-cognitive attitude. The belief explains logical behavior; the attitude
  explains practicality.
- **Relational expressivism (Schroeder, Being For, 2008):** Moral statements express a relation
  of "being for" -- e.g., "Murder is wrong" = being for blaming for murder.

**Strongest argument -- The motivational argument:**
```
P1: Moral judgments are necessarily motivating (motivational internalism).
P2: Beliefs alone cannot motivate; motivation requires a non-cognitive state
    (Humean theory of motivation).
C:  Therefore, moral judgments are (at least partly) non-cognitive states.
```
Non-cognitivism explains the tight connection between moral judgment and motivation without
mysterious intrinsically motivating beliefs or queer prescriptive facts.

**Central vulnerability -- The Frege-Geach problem:**
```
P1: If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong.
P2: Lying is wrong.
C:  Therefore, getting your little brother to lie is wrong.
```
This is valid modus ponens. For validity, "lying is wrong" must mean the same in P1's
unasserted antecedent as in P2's asserted occurrence. But if it expresses a non-cognitive
attitude (disapproval) when asserted, what does it express when unasserted? The speaker of P1
is not expressing disapproval of lying. Non-cognitivists must provide compositional semantics
that explains embedding behavior without treating moral sentences as belief-expressions.
Responses (Blackburn's quasi-realism, Gibbard's hyperplan semantics, hybrid expressivism) add
significant complexity, eroding the parsimony advantage over realism.

## 11. Fictionalism

**Core claims:** Moral discourse is false but useful -- we should maintain it as a fiction.
Engaging in moral discourse involves a pretense or make-believe. The justification for moral
discourse is pragmatic, not alethic.

**Variants:**
- **Revolutionary fictionalism (Joyce, The Myth of Morality, 2001):** We should *adopt* a
  fictionalist stance. After discovering the error theory, we continue moral discourse as a
  deliberate, useful pretense while acknowledging (at the reflective level) that it is false.
- **Hermeneutic fictionalism (Kalderon, Moral Fictionalism, 2005):** We already *are*
  fictionalists. The best interpretation of actual moral discourse is that it is a fiction --
  ordinary speakers are not making truth-claims.

**Strongest argument -- Practical indispensability:**
Moral discourse coordinates behavior, resolves collective action problems, and provides
deliberative frameworks. The fact that moral claims are literally false does not require
abandoning a practically indispensable practice. Fictionalism captures metaphysical honesty
plus practical preservation.

**Central vulnerability -- Can a known fiction genuinely guide action?**
A known fiction cannot provide genuine reasons. If I know "Lying is wrong" is literally false,
why should the pretense carry motivational weight? The fictionalist substitutes causal efficacy
(habit, conditioning) for normative force -- but this substitution misplaces what we want from
morality. A further dilemma: the fictionalist's recommendation to maintain the fiction seems
itself a normative claim. If all normative claims are false, the recommendation is false and
the position is self-undermining. If only *moral* normative claims are false, why do prudential
normative claims survive the arguments that defeated moral ones?

## 12. Moral Relativism

**Core claims:** Moral truth is relative to a framework -- a culture, society, set of
standards, or individual. There is no framework-transcendent moral truth. Moral claims can be
true (relative to a framework), distinguishing relativism from non-cognitivism.

**Variants:**
- **Appraiser relativism:** Truth of "Lying is wrong" is relative to the speaker's standards.
- **Agent relativism:** Truth is relative to the evaluated agent's standards.
- **Harman ("Moral Relativism Defended," 1975):** Moral judgments are elliptical for judgments
  about reasons relative to an agent's motivational set. "You ought not to lie" means "Given
  your motivations and evidence, you have most reason not to lie."
- **Wong (Natural Moralities, 2006):** Pluralistic relativism. Universal constraints (human
  nature, cooperation requirements) limit what can count as an adequate morality, but do not
  determine a single true morality. Multiple incompatible moralities satisfy the constraints.

**Strongest argument -- The argument from disagreement:**
```
P1: There is widespread, systematic, intractable moral disagreement across cultures.
P2: The best explanation of this disagreement is that moral truths are relative to
    frameworks, not absolute.
C:  Therefore, relativism should be accepted.
```
Persistent disagreement is exactly what relativism predicts and realism struggles to explain.

**Central vulnerability -- Disagreement and normative force:**
1. **Disagreement problem:** If truths are relative to frameworks, then cross-cultural "moral
   disagreement" is not genuine disagreement -- each side makes a different claim about what is
   true-relative-to-its-own-framework. This misdescribes the phenomenology: when cultures clash
   over women's rights or honor killing, the disagreement *feels* genuine.
2. **Normative force problem:** "According to my framework, I ought to phi" is a description of
   what I accept, not a prescription. The question "Why should I do what my framework says?"
   is always open. Relativism drains moral claims of normative force by turning them into
   sociological descriptions.
Wong addresses these by building in universal constraints -- but the more universal content
is added, the closer the view comes to naturalist realism.

## Logical Relations and Dialectical Transitions

The positions form a structured logical space with systematic transition pressures:

```
Non-Naturalism --> Constitutivism:      Epistemological pressure. If non-natural
                                         knowledge is mysterious, ground norms in agency.
Constitutivism --> Cornell Realism:     Content pressure. If constitutivism yields only
                                         formal norms, seek empirical moral properties.
Cornell Realism --> Analytic Funct.:    Methodological pressure. If the causal regress is
                                         too reliant on contested empirical claims, define
                                         functionally first, identify empirically second.
Analytic Funct. --> Reductive Nat.:     Permutation pressure. If no unique realizer
                                         exists, abandon two-step for direct identification.
Reductive Nat. --> Response-Dep.:       Open question pressure. If reduction leaves out
                                         the normative "oomph," build it into the property.
Response-Dep. --> Quietism:             Missing explanation pressure. If response-dependence
                                         cannot explain normativity, deny explanation needed.
Quietism --> Constructivism:            Evasion pressure. If quietism won't say what makes
                                         moral claims true, constructivism offers procedure.
Constructivism --> Error Theory:        Input pressure. If constructivism cannot deliver
                                         determinate universal content, perhaps the whole
                                         project of securing moral truth fails.
Error Theory --> Non-Cognitivism:       If the error is too high a price, drop cognitivism
                                         instead -- moral language was never truth-business.
Non-Cognitivism --> Fictionalism:       Frege-Geach pressure. If moral language embeds like
                                         descriptive language, perhaps it is descriptive
                                         (but false). Accept the error, preserve the practice.
Fictionalism --> Relativism:            Motivational pressure. If a known fiction cannot
                                         motivate, restore truth (relative) at the cost of
                                         absoluteness.
```

## Conclusion: Why the Taxonomy Matters

The richness of positions between robust realism and fictionalism is not a sign of failure --
it reflects the genuine difficulty of reconciling competing desiderata: moral truth, moral
objectivity, practical bindingness, and a naturalistic world picture.

For philosophical practice, the taxonomy serves to:

1. **Pinpoint arguments.** Knowing where a position sits on the spectrum tells you which
   objections are dialectically relevant.
2. **Map dialectical space.** The logical pressures driving transitions between positions help
   anticipate moves in ongoing debates.
3. **Identify burden-bearing claims.** Each position has a distinctive central vulnerability;
   isolating it is key to evaluation.
4. **Recognize hybrid positions.** Philosophers like Scanlon, Dworkin, and Parfit straddle
   categories. Understanding pure types helps identify what hybridization achieves.
5. **Avoid the binary trap.** The question is not *whether* you are a realist; it is *what
   kind* of realist (or anti-realist) you are, and what commitments that classification
   requires.
