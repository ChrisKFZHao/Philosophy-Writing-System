# Thought Experiment Typology

> This document catalogues common structural patterns of thought experiments in analytic philosophy, for use in type identification and structural comparison during thought-experiment analysis.

---

## Type I: Impossibility Demo

**Structure**: Construct a scenario that satisfies all the stated conditions, yet those conditions lead to an intuitively unacceptable result → the conditions themselves are problematic.

**Classic Cases**:
- Gettier, "The Ten Coins" (1963): satisfies "justified true belief" → but is not knowledge
- Frankfurt, "Counterexamples" (1969): satisfies "could have done otherwise" → but no moral responsibility
- Thomson, "The Violinist" (1971): satisfies "right to life" conditions → but the conclusion is absurd

**Analytic Focus**: Are the conditions in the scenario exhaustive? Can the intuition be generalized?

---

## Type II: Symmetry Breaker

**Structure**: Two cases are symmetric across all relevant dimensions, yet yield different intuitive verdicts → the theory under scrutiny cannot explain this difference.

**Classic Cases**:
- Foot, "The Trolley Problem" (1967): two cases (flipping a switch vs. pushing a person) → different intuitive verdicts
- Parfit, "Fission" (1984): one person splits into two → intuitions about personal identity break down

**Analytic Focus**: Has the selection of "relevant dimensions" of symmetry been fair? Have irrelevant dimensions been smuggled in?

---

## Type III: Counterfactual Exercise

**Structure**: Reason within a counterfactual scenario → demonstrate the necessity or the limits of some concept or principle.

**Classic Cases**:
- Searle, "The Chinese Room" (1980): counterfactual scenario (a person executes rules) → demonstrates that computers do not "understand"
- Putnam, "Brains in a Vat" (1981): counterfactual scenario (envatted brains) → demonstrates semantic externalism
- Chalmers, "Zombie World" (1996): counterfactual scenario (unconscious physical duplicates) → demonstrates that consciousness is not reducible to the physical

**Analytic Focus**: Is the counterfactual scenario coherent and well-grounded? Is the inference from scenario to conclusion a valid modal-logical move?

---

## Type IV: Intuition Consistency Test

**Structure**: Compare intuitive responses to two cases; if they are inconsistent → force the critic to revise the theory or acknowledge a conflict.

**Classic Cases**:
- Nozick, "The Experience Machine" (1974): invites the reader to assess whether they would plug in → intuitive refusal reveals the value of "realness"
- Kripke, "Godel/Schmidt" (1980): two cases of descriptively matching persons → intuitive distinction reveals that the description theory fails

**Analytic Focus**: Are the intuition-sources biased by cultural or educational differences? What is the gap between "inviting the reader to decide" and "deriving a philosophical conclusion"?

---

## Type V: Degeneration / Spectrum

**Structure**: Start from a clear positive case, then proceed by minute increments to a clear negative case → force the opponent to draw the line somewhere.

**Classic Cases**:
- Sorites (the Heap): 1 grain → 2 grains → ... → boundary vanishes
- Parfit, "Personal Identity" (1984): gradual replacement of bodily cells → does identity have a sharp boundary?
- Temkin, "Inequality" (1993): a series of inequality cases in which it is hard to judge which is worse

**Analytic Focus**: Where does the "safe threshold" for small changes lie? If the opponent can specify a threshold, does the argument collapse?

---

## Type VI: Epistemic Paradox

**Structure**: Construct a scenario in which the participant has perfect information yet still cannot attain intuitive knowledge → challenge the conditions on knowledge.

**Classic Cases**:
- A variant of Nozick, "The Experience Machine" (1974)
- Williamson, "Anti-Luminosity" (2000): even with perfect empirical evidence, one may still fail to know

**Analytic Focus**: Are the epistemic conditions truly perfect in the scenario? Can the intuition of "still not knowing" be attributed to some other factor?

---

## Use in Thought-Experiment Analysis

When analyzing a thought experiment:
1. First, identify which type it belongs to (it may be a hybrid)
2. Do the typical vulnerabilities of that type manifest in this thought experiment?
3. Are there type-specific "resistances" that need to be tested?
