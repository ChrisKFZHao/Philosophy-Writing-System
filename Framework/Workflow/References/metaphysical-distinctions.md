# Catalog of Core Metaphysical Distinctions

> This catalog organizes the fundamental conceptual distinctions that structure metaphysical inquiry. Each entry defines the two (or more) sides of a distinction, identifies key philosophers associated with each position, presents canonical arguments, and maps the distinction to the sub-domain of metaphysics it organizes. This catalog is used by `phil-distinction-track`, `phil-conceptual-analysis`, and all Phase 4 metaphysics skills for type identification, position classification, and dialectical mapping.

## 1. Endurance vs Perdurance (Identity through Time)

**Alternative formulations**: Three-Dimensionalism vs Four-Dimensionalism; Endurantism vs Perdurantism

**Sub-domain**: Persistence and identity through time

### Endurance (Three-Dimensionalism)

An object is wholly present at each moment it exists. It does not have temporal parts; rather, the very same object exists at different times, bearing its properties (perhaps time-indexed) simpliciter. Persistence is a matter of strict numerical identity across times.

- **Key philosophers**: Peter van Inwagen, Judith Jarvis Thomson, E.J. Lowe, Sally Haslanger
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Common Sense* — We ordinarily say "I am the same person I was yesterday," not "I have a temporal part that was located yesterday." Three-dimensionalism preserves the intuitive notion that objects endure as complete entities through time without being composed of temporal slices.
- **Canonical argument**: *The Problem of Temporary Intrinsics* (Thomson) — If an object has incompatible intrinsic properties at different times (e.g., being bent at t1 and straight at t2), and if properties are relations to times, then endurance can accommodate temporary intrinsics without contradiction by indexing property possession to times.

### Perdurance (Four-Dimensionalism)

An object is a spacetime worm composed of temporal parts. It is not wholly present at any single moment; rather, it has distinct temporal parts (slices, stages) located at each moment, and the whole object is the mereological sum of these parts.

- **Key philosophers**: David Lewis, Theodore Sider, Mark Heller, Katherine Hawley
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Temporary Intrinsics* (Lewis) — Endurance cannot handle temporary intrinsics without contradiction. If an object O is bent at t1 and straight at t2, and O is wholly present at both times, then O is both bent and straight simpliciter — a contradiction. Perdurance resolves this: the t1-temporal-part is bent, the t2-temporal-part is straight; no single entity bears incompatible properties.
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Special Relativity* — Relativity theory suggests there is no privileged foliation of spacetime into simultaneity slices. Four-dimensionalism aligns naturally with relativistic spacetime by treating objects as extended in time just as they are extended in space.

### Exdurantism (Stage Theory)

A third position: objects are instantaneous stages, and what we call "persistence through time" is a matter of distinct stages standing in the appropriate counterpart relation (temporal counterpart theory).

- **Key philosophers**: Theodore Sider (later work), Katherine Hawley

### Comparison Table

| Dimension | Endurance | Perdurance | Exdurantism |
|-----------|-----------|------------|-------------|
| Ontology of object | Wholly present at each time | Spacetime worm of temporal parts | Instantaneous stages |
| Identity across time | Strict numerical identity | Mereological sum relation | Counterpart relation |
| Temporary intrinsics | Time-indexed property possession | Different parts bear properties | Different stages bear properties |
| Alignment with physics | Tension with relativity | Natural fit with relativity | Natural fit with relativity |

## 2. Presentism vs Eternalism (Philosophy of Time)

**Alternative formulations**: A-Theory vs B-Theory (closely related); Tensed vs Tenseless time

**Sub-domain**: Philosophy of time, ontology of temporal reality

### Presentism

Only present objects and events exist. The past no longer exists; the future does not yet exist. Only the present moment is ontologically real. Truths about the past and future must be grounded in presently existing entities (e.g., present records, traces, or tensed facts).

- **Key philosophers**: Arthur Prior, Dean Zimmerman, John Bigelow, Ned Markosian
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Experience* — Our experience is irreducibly tensed. We experience the present as uniquely real and the passage of time as events receding into the past. Presentism takes this phenomenological datum at face value.
- **Canonical argument**: *The Grounding Problem* — Presentism can ground truths about the past in presently existing truthmakers (records, memories, causal traces), avoiding commitment to non-present entities.

### Eternalism

Past, present, and future objects and events all exist equally. Temporal reality is a four-dimensional block in which all times are ontologically on a par. "Now" is indexical — it picks out the time of utterance, not a metaphysically privileged moment.

- **Key philosophers**: J.J.C. Smart, D.H. Mellor, Adolf Grunbaum, Ted Sider
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Special Relativity* — Relativity theory denies a privileged global "now." Simultaneity is frame-relative. Eternalism coheres with the relativistic picture of spacetime as a four-dimensional manifold with no objective present.
- **Canonical argument**: *The Problem of Cross-Time Relations* — Presentism struggles with relations between present and non-present entities (e.g., "Lincoln is admired by historians"). Eternalism treats these relations as unproblematic: both relata exist tenselessly in the block universe.

### Growing Block

A middle position: the past and present exist, but the future does not. The block of reality grows as new times come into being.

- **Key philosophers**: C.D. Broad, Michael Tooley

### Comparison Table

| Dimension | Presentism | Growing Block | Eternalism |
|-----------|------------|---------------|------------|
| Past | Does not exist | Exists | Exists |
| Present | Exists (only) | Exists | Exists |
| Future | Does not exist | Does not exist | Exists |
| Truthmakers for past | Present entities | Past entities | Past entities |
| Privileged now | Metaphysically real | Metaphysically real | Indexical only |

## 3. Concrete vs Abstract Entities

**Alternative formulations**: Physical vs Platonic; Spatiotemporal vs Non-spatiotemporal

**Sub-domain**: Ontology, metaphysics of abstracta

### Abstract Entities

Entities that lack spatiotemporal location and lack causal powers. They do not enter into causal relations; they cannot be perceived; they are not located in space or time.

- **Examples**: Numbers, sets, properties (on some views), propositions, possible worlds (on some views)
- **Key criterion (Negative/Causal)**: An entity is abstract if it lacks causal efficacy — it neither affects nor is affected by concrete particulars.
- **Key criterion (Spatiotemporal)**: An entity is abstract if it is not located in space or time.
- **Key criterion (by Way of Negation)**: An entity is abstract if it is non-mental and non-physical.
- **Key philosophers**: Plato (Forms), Gottlob Frege (numbers as abstract objects), Kurt Godel (mathematical Platonism)
- **Canonical argument**: *The Indispensability Argument* (Quine-Putnam) — Mathematical entities are indispensable to our best scientific theories. By Quine's criterion of ontological commitment, we are committed to the existence of these abstracta.

### Concrete Entities

Entities that exist in space and time and enter into causal relations. They are the inhabitants of the physical world — objects, events, persons, fields, particles.

- **Key criterion**: An entity is concrete if it has spatiotemporal location and/or causal powers.

### The Boundary Problem

There is substantial dispute about whether the abstract/concrete distinction is exhaustive and exclusive. Problematic cases include:
- **Immanent universals** (Aristotelian forms): located in their instances, yet multiply located and not straightforwardly causal.
- **Fictional objects**: causally inert but arguably created by human activity and located in cultural space.
- **Sets of concrete objects**: the set {Socrates} is abstract by the causal criterion, but its existence depends on a concrete particular.

### Comparison Table

| Criterion | Abstract | Concrete | Problematic |
|-----------|----------|----------|-------------|
| Spatiotemporal location | No | Yes | Immanent universals |
| Causal powers | No | Yes | Abstract artifacts (e.g., nations) |
| Mental dependence | No (Platonism) | No | Psychologistic abstracta |
| Grounding in physical | No | Yes | Sets of concreta |

## 4. Realism vs Anti-Realism (Domain-Specific)

**Alternative formulations**: Objectivism vs Subjectivism; Mind-Independence vs Mind-Dependence

**Sub-domain**: Cross-cutting meta-ontological distinction; organizes modality, mathematics, science, morality

This distinction is not a single metaphysical position but a template instantiated across domains. Within each domain, realism asserts that the entities or facts in question exist mind-independently; anti-realism denies this.

### Scientific Realism vs Anti-Realism

**Realism**: The entities posited by our best scientific theories (electrons, genes, black holes) exist mind-independently, and scientific theories aim at true description of a theory-independent world.
- **Key philosophers**: Hilary Putnam (early), Richard Boyd, Stathis Psillos
- **Canonical argument**: *The No Miracles Argument* — The empirical success of science would be a miracle if scientific theories were not at least approximately true. Realism best explains predictive success.
- **Anti-Realism (Constructive Empiricism)**: Science aims at empirical adequacy, not truth. Observable phenomena are all that matter; commitment to unobservable entities is not warranted.
- **Key philosophers**: Bas van Fraassen, Larry Laudan
- **Canonical argument**: *The Pessimistic Meta-Induction* — The history of science is a graveyard of empirically successful but false theories. Present theories are likely false as well.

### Modal Realism vs Anti-Realism

**Realism (Concrete)**: Possible worlds exist as concrete, spatiotemporally isolated universes just as actual as our own. "Actual" is indexical.
- **Key philosophers**: David Lewis
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Theoretical Utility* — Possible worlds provide extraordinary explanatory power for modality, counterfactuals, content, properties, and causation, with no primitive modal notions required.
- **Anti-Realism (Ersatzism)**: Possible worlds exist as abstract representations — maximal consistent sets of propositions, states of affairs, or linguistic constructions.
- **Key philosophers**: Alvin Plantinga, Robert Stalnaker, Robert Adams
- **Anti-Realism (Fictionalism)**: Possible-worlds discourse is a useful fiction. There are no possible worlds, but talking as if there were yields theoretical benefits.
- **Key philosophers**: Gideon Rosen

### Mathematical Realism vs Anti-Realism

**Realism (Platonism)**: Mathematical objects (numbers, sets, functions) exist abstractly and mind-independently.
- **Key philosophers**: Gottlob Frege, Kurt Godel, Penelope Maddy (early)
- **Canonical argument**: *Frege's Argument from Singular Terms* — Mathematical statements contain singular terms that purport to refer; true mathematical statements must have referents for those terms.
- **Anti-Realism (Nominalism)**: There are no abstract mathematical objects. Mathematics is either false, or its truths must be reinterpreted without ontological commitment.
- **Key philosophers**: Hartry Field, Jody Azzouni
- **Anti-Realism (Intuitionism)**: Mathematical objects are mental constructions; mathematical truth is provability.
- **Key philosophers**: L.E.J. Brouwer, Michael Dummett

### Comparison Table

| Domain | Realism | Leading Anti-Realism | Key Dividing Question |
|--------|---------|---------------------|----------------------|
| Science | Theories refer to unobservables | Theories aim at empirical adequacy | Does success entail truth? |
| Modality | Concrete possible worlds | Ersatz abstract worlds | Can modality be reduced? |
| Mathematics | Abstract objects exist | Nominalism / Intuitionism | What grounds mathematical truth? |
| Morality | Mind-independent moral facts | Expressivism / Error theory | Are moral facts irreducible? |

## 5. Humean vs Anti-Humean about Laws / Causation

**Alternative formulations**: Regularity vs Necessitation; Humean Supervenience vs Governing Laws

**Sub-domain**: Metaphysics of laws, metaphysics of causation

### Humean (Regularity) View

There is no necessary connection between distinct existences. Laws of nature are merely descriptions of regularities — they summarize what happens, but they do not govern or produce what happens. The Humean mosaic of local, particular matters of fact exhausts reality; everything else supervenes on this mosaic.

- **Key philosophers**: David Lewis (Humean Supervenience), David Hume, John Earman, Barry Loewer
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Parsimony* — Humeanism requires no primitive modal notions (nomic necessity, causal oomph). It makes do with a sparse ontology of local qualities and the spatiotemporal relations among them. The best system of laws is the axiomatization of the Humean mosaic that achieves the best balance of simplicity and strength.
- **Formulation (Lewis)**: "Humean Supervenience is the doctrine that all there is to the world is a vast mosaic of local matters of particular fact, just one little thing and then another."

### Anti-Humean Views

Laws are not mere summaries; they govern, produce, or necessitate. There are real modal connections in nature that are not reducible to patterns of particular facts.

- **Key philosophers**: D.M. Armstrong (nomic necessitation), Tim Maudlin (primitivism), Nancy Cartwright, Alexander Bird

Three main Anti-Humean positions:

1. **Nomic Necessitation (Armstrong)**: Laws are relations of necessitation between universals. N(F, G) — "F-ness necessitates G-ness" — is a second-order universal relating the universals F and G. This relation is contingent; it could have been otherwise.

2. **Primitivism (Maudlin)**: Laws are fundamental, primitive entities not reducible to anything else. They produce or generate subsequent states from prior states. The notion of a law is conceptually basic.

3. **Dispositional Essentialism (Bird, Ellis)**: Laws flow from the essential natures of properties. Properties are inherently dispositional — they are powers that necessarily manifest certain responses to stimuli. Laws are not additional entities; they are consequences of what properties essentially are.

### Comparison Table

| Dimension | Humean | Armstrong (Nomic Necessitation) | Maudlin (Primitivism) | Dispositional Essentialism |
|-----------|--------|--------------------------------|----------------------|---------------------------|
| Status of laws | Descriptions of regularities | Second-order relations between universals | Primitive, fundamental | Consequences of property essences |
| Modal force | No primitive modality | Contingent necessitation | Primitive production | Metaphysical necessity |
| Ontology | Local matters of fact only | Universals + necessitation relation | Laws as additional entities | Powers as fundamental |
| Explanation of regularities | Laws summarize but do not explain | Laws explain by necessitating | Laws produce regularities | Essences necessitate regularities |

## 6. Sparse vs Abundant Properties

**Alternative formulations**: Natural vs Non-Natural properties; Elite vs Abundant properties; Joint-Carving vs Gerrymandered

**Sub-domain**: Metaphysics of properties, meta-ontology

### Sparse (Natural) Properties

Only some properties are genuine or natural. These are the properties that "carve nature at the joints" — they correspond to real similarities among objects, ground objective resemblance classes, and feature in causal laws. Most predicates do not correspond to sparse properties.

- **Key philosophers**: David Lewis, D.M. Armstrong, David Malet Armstrong
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Objective Similarity* — If all properties were equally real, any two objects would share infinitely many properties and differ in infinitely many. Objective resemblance requires a privileged set of properties that ground genuine similarity.
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Causation* — Causal laws relate sparse properties. If any gerrymandered predicate counted as a property, we could formulate infinitely many "laws" — but only some properties figure in genuine causal explanations.
- **Lewis's characterization**: Sparse properties are intrinsic, highly specific, and sufficient to characterize any possible world exhaustively. They are the minimal basis on which all else supervenes.

### Abundant (Non-Natural) Properties

Every meaningful predicate corresponds to a property. Properties are abundant, cheap, and easy to come by. Any set of (actual or possible) objects defines a property — the property of belonging to that set.

- **Key philosophers**: Jonathan Schaffer (permissivism), early analytic philosophers committed to the predicate-property correspondence
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Semantic Innocence* — We attribute properties freely in ordinary discourse: "being grue," "being such that 2+2=4," "being either a raven or a desk." Denying that these are properties requires an invidious distinction between "real" and "merely nominal" predication.
- **Schaffer's Permissivism**: We can be permissive in ontology (accept abundant properties) while being restrictive in ideology (treat only sparse properties as fundamental).

### Comparison Table

| Dimension | Sparse Properties | Abundant Properties |
|-----------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Criterion for existence | Carve nature at the joints | Any meaningful predicate |
| Ground objective similarity | Yes | No |
| Figure in causal laws | Yes | Typically not |
| Count | Relatively few | Infinitely many |
| Ontological commitment | Selective, elite | Permissive, egalitarian |

## 7. Categorical vs Dispositional Properties

**Alternative formulations**: Qualitative vs Causal; Quiddities vs Powers; Categoricalism vs Dispositional Essentialism

**Sub-domain**: Metaphysics of properties, philosophy of science

### Categorical Properties (Quidditism)

Properties have a qualitative, non-dispositional nature — a "quiddity" or "suchness" that is independent of the causal role the property plays. Two properties could swap their causal roles while remaining distinct properties; what a property is, is not exhausted by what it does.

- **Key philosophers**: David Lewis (quidditism), D.M. Armstrong (categoricalism with nomic necessitation)
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Contingency of Laws* — If properties were identical to their causal powers, the laws of nature would be metaphysically necessary. But laws seem contingent. The contingency of laws is best explained by properties having a categorical nature that is independent of the laws that relate them.
- **Key concept (Quiddity)**: The inner nature of a property, distinct from its nomological role. As Lewis puts it: a property's causal role is contingent; its quiddity is what remains constant across worlds where the property plays different roles.

### Dispositional Properties (Powers View)

Properties are essentially dispositional. A property is identical to, or at least individuated by, its causal powers — the set of stimuli to which it responds and the manifestations it produces. There is no "hidden quiddity" behind the causal profile.

- **Key philosophers**: Alexander Bird, Brian Ellis, Sydney Shoemaker, C.B. Martin, Stephen Mumford
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Epistemic Humility* — If properties have quiddities beyond their causal profiles, these quiddities are unknowable in principle (since all we can detect is causal behavior). Such unknowable entities should be rejected by Ockham's razor.
- **Canonical argument**: *Shoemaker's Argument* — It is inconceivable that properties could swap causal roles while remaining the same properties. The identity of a property is constituted by its causal potentialities.
- **Dispositional Essentialism (Bird)**: The laws of nature are metaphysically necessary because they flow from the essences of dispositional properties. There is no possible world where negative charge has different causal behavior.
- **The Ungrounded Argument (Armstrong against pure powers)**: If all properties are purely dispositional, and dispositions are defined in terms of further dispositions, the regress never bottoms out in something categorical. Pure powers are "always packing but never leaving" — they never manifest because there is nothing categorical to trigger them.

### Comparison Table

| Dimension | Categoricalism | Dispositional Essentialism | Mixed View |
|-----------|---------------|---------------------------|------------|
| Nature of properties | Qualitative quiddity | Causal powers | Both categorical and dispositional aspects |
| Laws of nature | Contingent | Metaphysically necessary | Contingent |
| Epistemic access | Partial (quiddity hidden) | Full (powers observable) | Partial |
| Regress problem | Avoided (categorical base) | Possibly vulnerable | Avoided |
| Key metaphor | Properties are like substances | Properties are like forces | Properties are like coins (two-sided) |

## 8. Grounded vs Fundamental

**Alternative formulations**: Derivative vs Primitive; Ontological Dependence vs Independence

**Sub-domain**: Metaphysics of grounding, fundamentality, metaphysical explanation

### The Grounding Framework

Grounding is a relation of non-causal metaphysical explanation. When A grounds B, B obtains in virtue of A; A is metaphysically prior to B; B is nothing "over and above" A. Grounding is typically taken to be irreflexive, asymmetric, and transitive — generating a partial order of metaphysical priority.

- **Key philosophers**: Kit Fine, Jonathan Schaffer, Gideon Rosen, Karen Bennett
- **Key concept**: "Metaphysical explanation" is not causal explanation. Grounding connects facts at the same time and world; it is synchronic, constitutive determination.

### Fundamental Entities

Something is fundamental if and only if it is not grounded in anything else. The fundamental forms the minimal, irreducible ontological base on which all else depends.

- **Schaffer's Priority Monism**: Exactly one thing is fundamental — the cosmos as a whole. All proper parts of the cosmos are grounded in it (a view that inverts classical mereological foundationalism).
- **Atomistic Foundationalism**: The fundamental entities are the smallest, simplest individuals — mereological atoms, fundamental particles, spacetime points.
- **Fine's Reality Thesis**: The fundamental facts are those that constitute reality — the facts that "carve reality at the joints."

### Grounded (Derivative) Entities

Something is grounded if and only if it obtains in virtue of something else. Most ordinary entities — tables, persons, nations, mental states — are grounded in more fundamental entities.

- **Theses about grounding**: (a) Well-foundedness: every grounded entity is ultimately grounded in the fundamental (no infinite descent). (b) Grounding necessitarianism: if A fully grounds B, then necessarily, if A obtains then B obtains. (c) Grounding contingentism: grounding does not entail necessitation.

### Canonical Arguments

- **Fine's Argument**: Metaphysical explanation cannot be reduced to modal or essentialist explanation. Consider: {Socrates} is grounded in Socrates, yet necessarily, {Socrates} exists iff Socrates exists. Modal equivalence does not suffice for metaphysical priority — grounding captures a direction of dependence that modal notions miss.
- **Schaffer's Tiling Argument (for Priority Monism)**: The cosmos must be fundamental because subcosmic objects require a background spacetime for their individuation, and that background is the cosmos itself.

### Comparison Table

| Dimension | Atomistic Foundationalism | Priority Monism (Schaffer) | Infinite Descent |
|-----------|--------------------------|---------------------------|------------------|
| Fundamental entity | Mereological atoms | The one cosmos | None (regress) |
| Direction of grounding | Parts → whole | Whole → parts | No terminal ground |
| Well-founded? | Yes | Yes | No |
| Principal advocate | Russell, Wittgenstein (early) | Schaffer | Regress-friendly metaphysicians |

## 9. Necessary vs Contingent

**Alternative formulations**: Must be vs Might not be; Truth in all possible worlds vs Truth in some but not all

**Sub-domain**: Modality; cross-cutting across all metaphysics

### Necessity

A proposition is necessary if and only if its negation is impossible. In the possible-worlds framework: a proposition is necessary iff it is true in all possible worlds.

**Varieties of necessity**:
- **Logical necessity**: Truth in virtue of logical form. True in all logically possible worlds. Example: "If P and Q, then P."
- **Metaphysical necessity**: Truth in virtue of the nature or essence of things. True in all metaphysically possible worlds. Example: "Water is H2O" (Kripke). Metaphysical necessity is broader than logical necessity but narrower than physical necessity.
- **Nomological (Physical) necessity**: Truth in virtue of the laws of nature. True in all worlds with the same laws. Example: "Nothing travels faster than light."
- **Conceptual necessity**: Truth in virtue of the meanings of concepts. Example: "All bachelors are unmarried."

**Kripke's contributions**: The necessary a posteriori ("Water is H2O") and the contingent a priori ("The standard meter is one meter long"). These disrupt the traditional alignment of the necessary/contingent distinction with the a priori/a posteriori distinction.

### Contingency

A proposition is contingent if and only if both it and its negation are possible. In the possible-worlds framework: a proposition is contingent iff it is true in some but not all possible worlds.

- **Contingency of laws**: On Humean views, the laws of nature are contingent — there are worlds with different regularities.
- **Contingency of existence**: Most concrete entities exist contingently. Whether any entity exists necessarily (e.g., God, abstract objects) is a major dispute.

### Comparison Table

| Type of Necessity | Scope of Worlds | Grounds | Example |
|-------------------|----------------|---------|---------|
| Logical | All logically possible | Logical form | P ∨ ¬P |
| Metaphysical | All metaphysically possible | Essence/nature | Water = H2O |
| Nomological | All worlds with same laws | Laws of nature | E = mc^2 |
| Conceptual | All worlds where concepts apply | Meaning | Bachelors are unmarried |

### The S5 Axiom

In S5 modal logic (the standard system for metaphysical modality): "possibly necessary P" entails "necessary P." If it is possible that P is necessary, then P is necessary. This collapses iterated modalities: every world accesses every other world, so what is possible is non-contingent.

## 10. A Priori vs A Posteriori

**Alternative formulations**: Rational vs Empirical; Independent of experience vs Dependent on experience

**Sub-domain**: Epistemology of metaphysics; cross-cutting modal epistemology

### A Priori Knowledge

Knowledge whose justification does not depend on sensory experience. A priori truths can be known by reason alone — through conceptual analysis, rational intuition, or logical deduction.

- **Key philosophers**: Immanuel Kant, Saul Kripke, Laurence BonJour, George Bealer
- **Traditional alignment**: A priori = necessary; A posteriori = contingent.
- **Canonical examples**: Mathematical truths ("2 + 2 = 4"), logical truths, conceptual truths.

### A Posteriori Knowledge

Knowledge whose justification depends essentially on sensory experience. A posteriori truths require empirical investigation.

- **Canonical examples**: "Water freezes at 0 degrees Celsius," "There are trees on Earth."

### Kripke's Disruption of the Traditional Alignment

In *Naming and Necessity* (1980), Kripke argued that the two distinctions cross-cut:

- **Necessary a posteriori**: "Water is H2O" — discovered empirically (a posteriori), but true in all possible worlds (necessary). The identity statement uses rigid designators and is metaphysically necessary.
- **Contingent a priori**: "The standard meter bar S is one meter long" — known a priori (it fixes the reference of "meter"), but contingent (the bar could have been longer or shorter due to temperature).

### Implications for Metaphysics

The necessary a posteriori opens the door to substantive metaphysical knowledge: if essentialist claims are both necessary and discoverable (in part) empirically, metaphysics is not merely conceptual analysis. The connection between conceivability and possibility — central to much modal epistemology — is complicated by the existence of necessary truths that are not a priori knowable.

### Comparison Table

| Epistemic Status | Traditional View | Kripkean View |
|-----------------|------------------|---------------|
| Necessary | A priori only | A priori or a posteriori |
| Contingent | A posteriori only | A posteriori or a priori |
| Key counterexample | — | "Water = H2O" (necessary a posteriori) |
| Key counterexample | — | "S is one meter" (contingent a priori) |

## 11. De Re vs De Dicto Modality

**Alternative formulations**: Of the thing vs Of the statement; Objectual vs Propositional modality

**Sub-domain**: Modal logic, modal metaphysics

### De Dicto Modality

Modality attributed to a proposition or dictum as a whole. The modal operator takes wide scope over the entire proposition.

- **Form**: Necessarily (∃x Fx) — "it is necessary that there exists an F."
- **Example**: "Necessarily, the president of the United States is a citizen." This is false: it could have been that a non-citizen was president. The modal claim is about the dictum — about how things must be.
- **Reading**: The modal claim is about the mode of truth of the proposition.

### De Re Modality

Modality attributed to a thing (res) itself. The modal claim is that a particular object has a property essentially or accidentally.

- **Form**: ∃x (Necessarily Fx) — "there exists something such that necessarily, it is F."
- **Example**: "The president of the United States is necessarily a citizen." This is true of the person (de re): that very individual has the property citizen-of-the-US essentially (if we assume citizenship is essential to persons).
- **Reading**: The modal claim is about the object — it attributes a modal property to the thing itself.

### The Scope Distinction

The difference is logical scope. In quantified modal logic:

```
De Dicto:  □(∃x)(Fx ∧ ∀y(Fy → y=x))
De Re:     (∃x)(Fx ∧ ∀y(Fy → y=x) ∧ □(∃x)(Fx ∧ ∀y(Fy → y=x)))
          Wait — this is not the standard formulation.
```

Standardly, the distinction is between:

```
De Dicto:  □(∃x Fx)            — the proposition "something is F" is necessary
De Re:     (∃x □Fx)            — there is a thing that necessarily is F
```

### Quine's Challenge

Quine argued that de re modality is unintelligible because it requires quantifying into opaque contexts. Whether an object necessarily has a property depends on how it is described: "9 is necessarily greater than 7" is true, but "the number of planets is necessarily greater than 7" is false — yet 9 = the number of planets (actually). Essentialist attribution requires an account of how objects have properties independently of modes of presentation.

### Essentialism

De re modality is committed to essentialism — the thesis that objects have some properties essentially and others accidentally. Kripke's defense of de re modality rests on a robust essentialism about natural kinds and individuals: origin essentialism (an individual has its actual origin essentially), kind essentialism (an individual belongs to its kind essentially).

### Comparison Table

| Dimension | De Dicto | De Re |
|-----------|----------|-------|
| Modal attribution | To whole proposition | To object itself |
| Logical form | □(P) | ∃x □(Fx) or □(Fx) of the object |
| Commitments | Possibility of propositions | Essentialism about objects |
| Quine's assessment | Respectable | "Aristotelian essentialism" (pejorative) |
| Substitutivity | Salva veritate within scope of □ | Depends on description (opaque context) |

## 12. Reductionist vs Non-Reductionist

**Alternative formulations**: Nothing-Over-And-Above vs Emergent/Irreducible; Eliminativist vs Realist (in some contexts)

**Sub-domain**: Cross-cutting meta-ontological distinction; organizes philosophy of mind, metaethics, metaphysics of science

This distinction is a template instantiated across multiple domains. In each case, the question is whether the entities, facts, or properties of domain X are reducible to those of domain Y (which is taken as more fundamental).

### Key Domains

**Philosophy of Mind**:
- **Reductionism (Type Identity Theory)**: Mental states are identical to brain states. Mental properties reduce to neurobiological properties.
  - **Key philosophers**: J.J.C. Smart, U.T. Place, David Lewis
- **Non-Reductionism**: Mental states are not reducible to physical states. Options include property dualism, functionalism with multiple realizability, and emergentism.
  - **Key philosophers**: Hilary Putnam (functionalism), David Chalmers (property dualism), Jerry Fodor (multiple realizability argument against reduction)

**Metaethics**:
- **Reductionism (Naturalism)**: Moral properties are identical to or constituted by natural properties. "Good" = "maximizes pleasure" or "what we would approve under ideal conditions."
  - **Key philosophers**: Frank Jackson (analytic naturalism), Peter Railton (synthetic naturalism)
- **Non-Reductionism (Non-Naturalism)**: Moral properties are sui generis and cannot be reduced to natural properties. Moore's Open Question Argument: for any natural property N, it is an open question whether N is good.
  - **Key philosophers**: G.E. Moore, Derek Parfit

**Special Sciences**:
- **Reductionism**: All scientific theories are reducible to fundamental physics. Chemistry reduces to physics; biology reduces to chemistry.
  - **Key philosophers**: Paul Oppenheim, Hilary Putnam (early)
- **Non-Reductionism (Autonomy of Special Sciences)**: Higher-level sciences are autonomous; their explanatory categories are irreducible. Multiple realizability of higher-level kinds undermines reduction.
  - **Key philosophers**: Jerry Fodor, Philip Kitcher

### The Formal Model (Nagel Reduction)

Ernest Nagel's model of reduction: Theory T1 reduces to theory T2 if and only if the laws of T1 are derivable from the laws of T2 plus bridge principles connecting the vocabularies of T1 and T2.

### Comparison Table

| Domain | Reductionist Position | Non-Reductionist Position | Key Argument Against Reduction |
|--------|----------------------|--------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Mind | Type identity theory | Property dualism / functionalism | Multiple realizability |
| Ethics | Ethical naturalism | Non-naturalism | Open question argument |
| Biology | Gene-centrism / physicalism | Autonomy of biology | Multiple realizability of biological kinds |
| Modality | Modal realism (reduces to worlds) | Modality as primitive | Fine: grounding not captureable modally |

## 13. Mereological Universalism vs Nihilism vs Moderate Views

**Alternative formulations**: Composition as unrestricted vs Composition as never occurring; Universal composition vs mereological nihilism

**Sub-domain**: Mereology, ontology of composite objects

### Mereological Universalism (Unrestricted Composition)

For any collection of objects, there is a mereological sum — a further object composed of exactly those objects. Composition is unrestricted; every plurality has a fusion.

- **Key philosophers**: David Lewis, Theodore Sider, Achille Varzi
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Non-Arbitrariness* — Any restriction on composition would be vague and arbitrary. Consider a series of cases from intact cat to cat-tissue-scattered-across-the-room. There is no sharp cutoff for when "the cat" ceases to exist. Since existence cannot be vague (or so the argument goes), composition must be either always unrestricted or never occurring.
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Mereological Harmony* — Unrestricted composition produces an elegant, extensional mereology in which sameness of parts entails sameness of whole. No special composition principles or vagueness-ridden boundaries are needed.
- **Consequence**: Universalism implies the existence of many strange objects: the trout-turkey (the fusion of a trout and a turkey), a fusion of the Eiffel Tower and the left half of a particular electron, etc.

### Mereological Nihilism

No composite objects exist. There are no tables, chairs, persons, or planets — only mereological simples (partless entities) arranged table-wise, chair-wise, etc. Composition never occurs.

- **Key philosophers**: Peter van Inwagen (near-nihilism: only living organisms compose), Cian Dorr, Ted Sider (entertained), Ross Cameron
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Arbitrariness* — Any restriction on composition short of nihilism requires a principled, non-vague, non-arbitrary criterion. Since no such criterion has been successfully formulated, and since universalism is ontologically profligate, nihilism is the only defensible position.
- **Canonical argument**: *Paraphrase Strategy* — Ordinary discourse about composite objects can be paraphrased into statements about simples arranged in certain patterns. "There is a table" becomes "There are simples arranged table-wise." No truth of common sense is lost.

### Moderate Views

Composition is restricted: some pluralities compose an object, others do not. The challenge is to specify the restriction non-arbitrarily.

- **Van Inwagen's Organicism**: Living organisms are the only composite objects. A plurality of simples composes a whole only when their activity constitutes a life.
- **Brutal Composition (Markosian)**: Whether composition occurs is a brute fact, irreducible to any informative criterion. There is no non-circular account of when composition happens.
- **Contact-based Views**: Objects compose a whole only when they are in contact, bonded, or functionally integrated.
- **Key philosopher**: Ned Markosian, Trenton Merricks

### Comparison Table

| Position | When Does Composition Occur? | Ordinary Objects | Theoretical Cost |
|----------|----------------------------|------------------|-----------------|
| Universalism | Always (unrestricted) | Exist, plus strange fusions | Ontological inflation |
| Nihilism | Never | Do not exist (simples only) | Radical revision of common sense |
| Organicism | Only for lives | Only organisms exist | Highly revisionary |
| Brutal Comp. | Brute fact, no criterion | Most ordinary objects | Primitive, unanalyzable facts |
| Contact-based | When parts touch or bond | Most ordinary objects | Problem of gappy objects |

## 14. Bundle Theory vs Substratum Theory of Substance

**Alternative formulations**: Bundle of properties vs Bare particular; Property-collection vs Substratum + Properties

**Sub-domain**: Metaphysics of substance, ontology of particulars, the problem of individuation

### Bundle Theory

An ordinary particular (substance, object) is nothing but a bundle or collection of properties (tropes or universals) that are compresent — co-instantiated at a single spatiotemporal location. There is no underlying substratum that "has" the properties; the object just is the properties.

- **Key philosophers**: David Hume, Bertrand Russell, Peter Simons, D.C. Williams
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Parsimony* — Bundle theory eliminates the mysterious substratum — an entity that is said to be "bare" (propertyless in itself) yet functions as the bearer of properties. The substratum is epistemically inaccessible and metaphysically suspect.
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Empirical Adequacy* — All we ever encounter are properties. We never encounter a "substance" apart from its properties. Bundle theory does justice to what is empirically given.
- **Key commitment (Trope vs Universal version)**: Bundle theory can be formulated with tropes (particularized properties, e.g., this particular whiteness) or with universals. The trope bundle version avoids the problem of bundle duplication (two distinct objects with all the same universals).

### Substratum Theory

An ordinary particular is composed of a substratum (bare particular, thin particular) plus the properties that inhere in it. The substratum is the principle of individuation: it is what makes *this* bundle of properties a distinct particular from *that* qualitatively identical bundle.

- **Key philosophers**: Aristotle (primary substance), John Locke ("substance is a something I know not what"), D.M. Armstrong, E.J. Lowe
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Individuation* — If a particular is nothing but its properties, then qualitatively identical particulars would be numerically identical — violating the Identity of Indiscernibles. Since two electrons can share all their properties yet be distinct, something other than properties must individuate them. That something is the substratum.
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Property Change* — If an object is identical to its bundle of properties, any change in properties would result in a numerically different object (since the bundle changes). But objects persist through property change. A substratum provides continuity of identity through change.

### Objections to Each Side

- **Against Bundle Theory**: The Identity of Indiscernibles problem: two qualitatively identical spheres in a symmetric universe (Black's two-sphere universe). Bundle theory seems forced to identify them.
- **Against Substratum Theory**: The "bare particular" is incoherent: it is said to have no properties of its own, yet it must be a particular, must be a substratum, must exist, etc. A truly bare particular cannot even be described without attributing properties to it.

### Comparison Table

| Dimension | Bundle Theory | Substratum Theory |
|-----------|---------------|-------------------|
| Constitution of object | Collection of properties | Substratum + properties |
| Principle of individuation | Qualitative difference of compresent properties | Primitive thisness of substratum |
| Identity through change | Loses identity with any property change | Retains identity via substratum |
| Epistemic access | All constituents observable (properties) | Substratum unobservable |
| Parsimony | Yes (no substratum) | No (postulates substratum) |
| Problem case | Black's two-sphere universe | Bare particular problem |

## 15. Monism vs Pluralism

**Alternative formulations**: One vs Many; Existence Monism vs Ontological Pluralism; Priority Monism vs Atomism

**Sub-domain**: Metaphysics of fundamentality, mereology, ontology of the whole

### Monism

Reality, fundamentally, is one. The cosmos as a whole is the single fundamental entity, and all multiplicity is derivative. There are several varieties:

**Existence Monism**: Exactly one concrete object exists — the world, the One, the All. Apparent multiplicity is illusion or appearance.
- **Key philosophers**: Parmenides, Spinoza, F.H. Bradley, perhaps Hegel
- **Canonical argument (Parmenidean)**: Being is; non-being is not. Since there is no non-being, there can be no separation, differentiation, or multiplicity. The One alone is.
- **Canonical argument (Spinoza)**: There is exactly one substance (God or Nature). All particular things are modes of that single substance. Substance monism is derived from the definition of substance as that which is in itself and conceived through itself.

**Priority Monism**: Many things exist, but exactly one thing — the cosmos — is fundamental. All proper parts of the cosmos are grounded in or ontologically dependent on the whole.
- **Key philosophers**: Jonathan Schaffer, perhaps Hegel
- **Canonical argument**: *The Tiling Argument* — Subcosmic objects cannot be fundamental because their individuation conditions require a maximal spacetime background. A particle's trajectory requires the full spacetime manifold. The whole is prior to its parts.
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Quantum Entanglement* — Quantum mechanics suggests that some wholes have properties not reducible to the intrinsic properties and relations of their parts. Entangled systems exhibit holistic features that suggest the priority of the whole.

**Mereological Nihilism as Extreme Pluralism**: Only simples exist, but there are many simples. The cosmos as a unified whole does not exist (no composite objects exist).

### Pluralism

Reality is fundamentally many. The fundamental entities are many — atoms, simples, individuals, substances. The whole is derivative, not the parts.

- **Key philosophers**: Democritus, Bertrand Russell, Wittgenstein (Tractatus)
- **Atomism**: Reality is composed of many simple, indivisible entities (atoms, simples). Complex objects are constructed from simples but are not themselves fundamental.
- **Ontological Pluralism**: Different categories of entities exist without any one category being reducible to the others. Substance, property, relation, event, state of affairs — all are fundamental categories.

### Comparison Table

| Position | What is Fundamental? | Status of the Many | Status of the One (Cosmos) |
|----------|---------------------|--------------------|---------------------------|
| Existence Monism | The One (only) | Illusory appearance | The only entity |
| Priority Monism | The Cosmos | Grounded in the Cosmos | Fundamental |
| Atomism | Mereological simples | Fundamental parts | Mere sum of parts |
| Ontological Pluralism | Many categories | Fundamental | Composite, derivative |
| Mereological Nihilism | Simples (many) | Fundamental simples | Does not exist |

## 16. Determinism vs Indeterminism

**Alternative formulations**: Causal determinism vs Causal indeterminism; Nomological determinism vs Chance

**Sub-domain**: Metaphysics of causation, philosophy of science, free will

### Determinism

Given the complete state of the universe at any time, and the laws of nature, exactly one future is possible. Every event is necessitated by prior events and the laws. The world is a clockwork: the past plus the laws fix the future uniquely.

- **Key philosophers**: Pierre-Simon Laplace (Laplace's demon), Baron d'Holbach, Ted Honderich
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Classical Physics* — Newtonian mechanics is deterministic: given the positions and momenta of all particles and the force laws, all future states are uniquely determined. If the world is fundamentally Newtonian, determinism holds.
- **Key concept (Laplace's Demon)**: An intellect that knew all forces and all positions of all particles at one moment "would embrace in the same formula the movements of the greatest bodies of the universe and those of the lightest atom; for it, nothing would be uncertain and the future, as the past, would be present to its eyes."

**Varieties of Determinism**:
- **Causal determinism**: Every event has a sufficient cause.
- **Nomological determinism**: The laws of nature plus the initial state entail all subsequent states.
- **Logical determinism (Fatalism)**: All propositions about the future are already true or false (bivalence), so the future is fixed — not necessarily by causation, but by truth-values.
- **Theological determinism**: Divine foreknowledge or predestination fixes all events.

### Indeterminism

Some events are not determined by prior events and the laws. The world has objective chance: given the complete state and the laws, multiple futures are genuinely possible. The future is "open" in an ontologically robust sense.

- **Key philosophers**: C.S. Peirce (tychism), Karl Popper, Peter van Inwagen (for the consequence argument)
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Quantum Mechanics* — Standard interpretations of quantum mechanics (collapse views, GRW) are irreducibly probabilistic. The wave function evolves deterministically, but measurement outcomes involve objective chance. The world is not Laplacean.
- **Canonical argument**: *The Argument from Free Will (Libertarian)* — If determinism is true, our actions are consequences of events before our birth and the laws of nature. We could never have done otherwise. This is incompatible with moral responsibility, which requires the ability to do otherwise. Since we have moral responsibility, determinism must be false.
- **Key concept (Objective Chance)**: In indeterministic worlds, chance is not merely epistemic (reflecting our ignorance). There are irreducibly probabilistic laws. The same initial conditions, with the same laws, can lead to different outcomes.

### Compatibilism

A third position on the relationship between determinism and free will: determinism is compatible with free will and moral responsibility. Free will does not require the ability to do otherwise in the categorical sense; it requires that one's actions flow from one's own desires, character, and reasoning — even if those are determined.

- **Key philosophers**: David Hume, Harry Frankfurt, Daniel Dennett, John Martin Fischer

### Comparison Table

| Dimension | Causal Determinism | Quantum Indeterminism | Compatibilism |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------------------|---------------|
| Future possibilities | Exactly one | Genuinely many | Determinism + free will |
| Nomological basis | Classical physics | Quantum mechanics | Either |
| Free will compatibility | Incompatible (hard determinism) | Libertarian free will possible | Compatible |
| Chance | Epistemic only | Objective | Either |
| Key metaphor | Clockwork universe | Dice-playing universe | Moral agent as author |
