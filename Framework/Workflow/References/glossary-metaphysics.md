# Metaphysics Terminology Glossary

## Introduction

This glossary serves as a centralized knowledge base for seven metaphysics analysis skills: **ontological-commitment**, **identity-persistence**, **grounding-check**, **conceivability-arg**, **causation-analyze**, **essence-detect**, and **natural-kind-analyze**. Its purpose is to provide these skills with precise, academically grounded definitions of core metaphysical terms, along with their canonical source references, key philosophical controversies, and cross-references among related concepts. Each entry is structured to support automated reasoning about metaphysical arguments by supplying the conceptual scaffolding — definition, etymology, debate context, and source — that the analysis skills require when parsing, evaluating, or generating philosophical content.

The glossary is organized into ten thematic categories, moving from the most general (ontology, properties) through intermediate topics (essence, identity, composition, causation) to specialized domains (possible worlds, time, meta-ontology). Cross-references are indicated by **bolded terms** and by explicit "See also" lines at the end of entries. Comparison tables are provided where rival positions map cleanly onto one another.

## 1. Ontology Basics

### Ontology
- **Definition**: The philosophical study of what exists — the systematic investigation of the categories of being and the most general features of reality. Ontology asks not merely *what* exists but *what it is* for something to exist, and how entities of different kinds relate to one another.
- **Etymology**: From Greek *ontos* (being, genitive of *on*) + *logos* (study, discourse). Coined in the early 17th century, though the inquiry traces to Aristotle's *Metaphysics*.
- **Key Issues**: Whether ontology is a substantive inquiry or a matter of linguistic framework choice (see **meta-ontology**); whether there is a uniquely correct ontological inventory; the relationship between ontology and natural science.
- **Source References**: Aristotle, *Metaphysics* Book IV (4th century BCE); W.V.O. Quine, "On What There Is" (1948).
- **See also**: meta-ontology, entity, existence, Quinean criterion of ontological commitment.

### Entity
- **Definition**: Anything that exists or has being — the most general ontological category, encompassing objects, properties, events, facts, numbers, persons, and any other item admitted into one's ontology. An entity is whatever can be referred to, quantified over, or treated as a subject of predication.
- **Etymology**: From Medieval Latin *entitas*, from *ens* (being), present participle of Latin *esse* (to be).
- **Key Issues**: Whether "entity" is a univocal category or merely an umbrella term for fundamentally different kinds of things; whether abstract entities (numbers, propositions) are entities in the same sense as concrete ones.
- **Source References**: Quine, *Word and Object* (1960); Peter van Inwagen, "Meta-Ontology" (1998).
- **See also**: ontology, existence, being.

### Existence
- **Definition**: The property (or pseudo-property) of being real, of being part of the world rather than a mere fiction, hallucination, or possibilium. In first-order logic, existence is expressed by the existential quantifier ∃. A central dispute is whether existence is a first-order property that some individuals have and others lack, or a second-order property of concepts (as Kant and Frege held).
- **Etymology**: From Latin *existentia*, from *existere* (to stand forth, emerge, be manifest).
- **Key Issues**: Whether existence is a predicate (Kant's objection, Frege's analysis); the distinction between *being* and *existence* in Heidegger and the existentialist tradition; whether "exists" means the same thing when applied to concrete vs. abstract objects.
- **Source References**: Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason* A598/B626 (1781/1787); Gottlob Frege, *Foundations of Arithmetic* (1884); Quine, "On What There Is" (1948).
- **See also**: being, Quinean criterion of ontological commitment, ontology.

### Being
- **Definition**: The most fundamental and general aspect of reality — that in virtue of which anything is. Distinguished from **existence** in traditions (Heidegger, Sartre) where being is the overarching horizon within which particular existents appear, or in scholastic philosophy where being (*ens*) is the transcendental that applies to everything that is in any way.
- **Etymology**: From Old English *beon* (to be, exist), from Proto-Germanic *beuną*. The philosophical weight of the term draws on Greek *to on* and Latin *ens*.
- **Key Issues**: The distinction between Being (*Sein*) and beings (*Seiendes*) in Heidegger; whether being is univocal (Duns Scotus), analogical (Thomas Aquinas), or equivocal; the question "Why is there something rather than nothing?"
- **Source References**: Martin Heidegger, *Being and Time* (*Sein und Zeit*, 1927); Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I, q.13, a.5 (1265–1274); Aristotle, *Metaphysics* Book VII.
- **See also**: existence, nothingness, ontology.

### Nothingness
- **Definition**: The absence or negation of being — what there is when there is nothing at all. In metaphysical discourse, nothingness is typically invoked in the context of the question "Why is there something rather than nothing?" and in debates about whether an empty world is a genuine metaphysical possibility.
- **Etymology**: From Old English *nathing* (no thing), but the philosophical concept traces to Greek *to me on* (non-being) in Parmenides and Plato.
- **Key Issues**: Whether nothingness is a genuine *entity* that can be referred to or merely a façon de parler; whether the existence of the contingent universe requires an explanation that precludes nothingness as a default state; whether an empty possible world counts as a world.
- **Source References**: Parmenides, *On Nature* (fragment B2, 5th century BCE); Martin Heidegger, "What Is Metaphysics?" (1929); David Lewis, *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986), section on the empty world.
- **See also**: being, existence, possible world.

### Quinean Criterion of Ontological Commitment
- **Definition**: The criterion articulated by W.V.O. Quine that "to be is to be the value of a variable." An entity is ontologically committed to by a theory just in case that entity must be counted among the values of the bound variables in the theory's canonical first-order formulation for the theory's assertions to be true. In slogan form: "to be is to be the value of a variable."
- **Etymology**: Named after Willard Van Orman Quine (1908–2000).
- **Key Issues**: Whether the criterion captures all ontological commitment or only commitment as revealed by a particular linguistic regimentation; whether second-order quantification incurs commitment to properties or classes; whether the criterion presupposes rather than resolves ontological disputes.
- **Source References**: W.V.O. Quine, "On What There Is" (1948); Quine, *Word and Object* (1960), ch. 7; Hilary Putnam, *Ethics without Ontology* (2004) for a critique.
- **See also**: ontology, existence, meta-ontology, quantifier variance.

## 2. Properties and Universals

### Universal
- **Definition**: An entity that can be wholly present in multiple distinct particulars at the same time — what all instances of a given property or kind have in common. For example, if both a fire truck and a rose are red, the universal *redness* is said to be instantiated by both. Universals are posited to explain attribute agreement, the semantics of predicates, and the objectivity of natural **kinds**.
- **Etymology**: From Latin *universalis*, from *universus* (whole, entire). The problem of universals originates in Plato's Theory of Forms and Aristotle's critique.
- **Key Issues**: The problem of universals: whether universals exist independently of particulars (Platonic **realism**), exist only immanently in particulars (Aristotelian/**Armstrong's immanent realism**), or do not exist at all (**nominalism**); whether universals are located where their instances are.
- **Source References**: Plato, *Phaedo* 100b–105c; Aristotle, *Categories* ch. 5; D.M. Armstrong, *Universals: An Opinionated Introduction* (1989).
- **See also**: particular, trope, realism about universals, nominalism, instantiation.

### Particular
- **Definition**: A non-repeatable, concrete individual entity — the kind of thing that occupies a specific spatiotemporal location and cannot be wholly present in two places at once. Ordinary objects like tables, persons, and electrons are paradigm particulars. Particulars contrast with **universals**, which are repeatable.
- **Etymology**: From Latin *particularis*, from *particula* (small part). In medieval philosophy, *particulare* designated what is individual as opposed to universal.
- **Key Issues**: Whether particulars are bare substrata that support properties (the substratum theory), bundles of properties (the bundle theory), or something else; the relationship between particulars and their **tropes**.
- **Source References**: Aristotle, *Categories* 2a11–2b7; G.F. Stout, "The Nature of Universals and Propositions" (1921); D.C. Williams, "On the Elements of Being" (1953).
- **See also**: universal, trope, individual essence, haecceity.

### Trope
- **Definition**: An abstract particular — a property instance that is unique to the particular that bears it, not shared or repeated. On a trope theory, the redness of *this* apple is a numerically distinct entity from the redness of *that* apple, even if the two tropes exactly resemble each other. Trope theory is a form of **nominalism** that rejects universals while retaining properties as real constituents of the world.
- **Etymology**: From Greek *tropos* (turn, way, manner), via Latin *tropus*. Introduced into contemporary metaphysics by D.C. Williams (1953) and developed by Keith Campbell.
- **Key Issues**: Whether resemblance among tropes can be explained without appeal to a universal of resemblance; whether tropes themselves are simple or complex; the viability of trope-bundle theories of substance.
- **Source References**: D.C. Williams, "On the Elements of Being" (1953); Keith Campbell, *Abstract Particulars* (1990).
- **See also**: universal, particular, nominalism, resemblance nominalism.

### Property
- **Definition**: A way an entity is or can be — a characteristic, feature, or attribute. Properties are what predicates ascribe: being red, being square, being 2 kg in mass. The metaphysical status of properties is disputed: they may be **universals**, **tropes**, natural classes of resembling particulars, or mere shadows of linguistic predication.
- **Etymology**: From Old French *propriete*, from Latin *proprietas* (ownership, peculiar quality), from *proprius* (one's own).
- **Key Issues**: Whether there are uninstantiated properties; whether properties are abundant (every predicate corresponds to a property) or sparse (only those that ground objective similarity and causal powers); the relation between properties and **natural kinds**.
- **Source References**: David Lewis, "New Work for a Theory of Universals" (1983); D.M. Armstrong, *A Theory of Universals* (1978).
- **See also**: universal, trope, relation, instantiation, essence.

### Relation
- **Definition**: A property that holds between two or more entities — a polyadic universal. Examples include *being taller than*, *being the father of*, *being between*, and spatial/temporal relations. Relations raise distinctive metaphysical questions about whether they require non-relational foundations and whether there can be relations without relata.
- **Etymology**: From Latin *relatio*, from *referre* (to carry back, to refer). Aristotle classified relations as one of his ten categories (*pros ti* — toward something).
- **Key Issues**: Whether all relations are internal (supervenient on the intrinsic properties of their relata) or some are external (not so supervenient); whether relations are fundamental or reducible to monadic properties of states of affairs (Bradley's regress); the ontological status of symmetric vs. non-symmetric relations.
- **Source References**: F.H. Bradley, *Appearance and Reality* (1893), ch. 3; Bertrand Russell, *The Principles of Mathematics* (1903), sections on relations; David Armstrong, *A World of States of Affairs* (1997).
- **See also**: property, instantiation, ontological dependence.

### Instantiation
- **Definition**: The relation (or non-relational tie) between a **universal** and the **particular** that has it, or between a **trope** and the object that bears it. When a particular instantiates a universal, the universal is said to be "in" or "present in" that particular. The nature of instantiation is a deep metaphysical problem: is it itself a relation (risking Bradley's regress), or is it a sui generis non-relational tie?
- **Etymology**: From Latin *instantia* (presence, urgency), from *instare* (to stand upon, be present). The technical use is modern.
- **Key Issues**: Bradley's regress: if instantiation is a relation, then a further relation seems needed to connect the universal, the particular, and the instantiation relation, ad infinitum; the distinction between instantiation and exemplification (in the formal mode, a particular instantiates; in the material mode, a kind exemplifies a property).
- **Source References**: F.H. Bradley, *Appearance and Reality* (1893); E.J. Lowe, *The Four-Category Ontology* (2006); Donald Baxter, "Instantiation as Partial Identity" (2001).
- **See also**: universal, particular, trope, property.

### Realism about Universals
- **Definition**: The metaphysical thesis that universals exist mind-independently — they are real, extra-mental entities. This contrasts with **nominalism**, which denies the existence of universals, and conceptualism, which holds that universals exist only as mental concepts. Realism admits of two main varieties: *Platonic realism* (universals exist independently of their instances, in a transcendent realm — *universalia ante rem*) and *Aristotelian/immanent realism* (universals exist only in their instances — *universalia in rebus*).
- **Source References**: Plato, *Republic* Books V–VII; Aristotle, *Metaphysics* Book VII; D.M. Armstrong, *Universals and Scientific Realism* (1978).
- **See also**: universal, Armstrong's immanent realism, nominalism, trope.

### Nominalism
- **Definition**: The rejection of universals. The nominalist holds that everything that exists is **particular**; what universal terms (predicates, general terms) denote are at most linguistic items (predicate nominalism), classes of particulars (**class nominalism**), or sets of resembling **tropes** (**resemblance nominalism**). Nominalism is motivated by ontological parsimony and by skepticism about the coherence of "repeatable" entities.
- **Etymology**: From Latin *nomen* (name). The term originates in medieval philosophy, where *nominales* held that universals are mere names (*flatus vocis*).
- **Key Issues**: Whether nominalism can account for attribute agreement without circular appeal to resemblance; whether nominalism can ground objective similarity and laws of nature; Quine's argument that nominalism is committed to classes, which are arguably abstract and universal-like.
- **Source References**: Nelson Goodman and W.V.O. Quine, "Steps Toward a Constructive Nominalism" (1947); David Armstrong, *Nominalism and Realism* (1978); Gonzalo Rodriguez-Pereyra, *Resemblance Nominalism* (2002).
- **See also**: resemblance nominalism, class nominalism, realism about universals, trope.

### Resemblance Nominalism
- **Definition**: A form of **nominalism** according to which what makes a predicate like "is red" apply to multiple particulars is not a single universal *redness* but primitive resemblance relations among the particulars themselves. Red things form a resemblance class; no shared entity is required. The challenge is to explain resemblance without presupposing shared properties.
- **Source References**: H.H. Price, *Thinking and Experience* (1953); Gonzalo Rodriguez-Pereyra, *Resemblance Nominalism* (2002).
- **See also**: nominalism, class nominalism, trope, universal.

### Class Nominalism
- **Definition**: A form of **nominalism** that identifies properties with classes (sets) of particulars. The property *being red* just is the set of all red things. Class nominalism avoids appealing to primitive resemblance but faces the challenge that co-extensional properties (all and only creatures with hearts are creatures with kidneys) are identical, and that the class of red things would have been different had different things been red, threatening the modal profile of properties.
- **Source References**: David Lewis, *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986), pp. 50–69; David Armstrong, *Universals and Scientific Realism*, vol. I (1978).
- **See also**: nominalism, resemblance nominalism, realism about universals, property.

### Armstrong's Immanent Realism
- **Definition**: The theory developed by D.M. Armstrong that universals exist, but only *in rebus* — immanently, in their instances. A universal is wholly present in each particular that instantiates it, and it has no existence apart from being instantiated. Universals are sparse, not abundant: only those properties that ground objective similarity and causal powers count as universals (roughly, those discovered by natural science). Armstrong combines this with a states-of-affairs ontology in which particulars and universals are inseparable constituents of states of affairs.
- **Source References**: D.M. Armstrong, *Universals and Scientific Realism*, 2 vols. (1978); Armstrong, *A World of States of Affairs* (1997).
- **See also**: realism about universals, universal, instantiation, natural-kind-analyze.

## 3. Categories and Fundamentality

### Category
- **Definition**: The highest and most general kinds under which entities fall — the fundamental divisions of being. Aristotle proposed ten categories (substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, state, action, affection). Contemporary category theory (e.g., E.J. Lowe's four-category ontology) distinguishes substance from property, and objects from kinds/universals.
- **Etymology**: From Greek *kategoria* (predication, accusation), from *kata* (against) + *agoreuein* (to speak in the assembly). Aristotle's term was appropriated for the highest predicates of being.
- **Key Issues**: Whether there is a uniquely correct categorial scheme; the number and nature of the fundamental categories; the relationship between linguistic categories and ontological categories.
- **Source References**: Aristotle, *Categories*; E.J. Lowe, *The Four-Category Ontology* (2006); Jonathan Lowe, "Recent Advances in Metaphysics" (2002).
- **See also**: ontology, fundamentality, grounding.

### Fundamentality
- **Definition**: The status of being metaphysically basic or ungrounded — a fundamental entity or fact is one that does not depend on, reduce to, or hold in virtue of anything else. The fundamental level is the ontological bedrock. The concept is central to the grounding literature and to the project of metaphysical explanation.
- **Key Issues**: Whether fundamentality is absolute or relative; whether there must be a fundamental level (**foundationalism**) or whether reality can be infinitely descending (**infinitism**); what criteria determine fundamentality (simplicity, naturalness, completeness).
- **Source References**: Jonathan Schaffer, "On What Grounds What" (2009); Kit Fine, "The Question of Realism" (2001); Karen Bennett, *Making Things Up* (2017).
- **See also**: grounding, ontological dependence, metaphysical foundationalism vs infinitism, grounding-check.

### Grounding
- **Definition**: A non-causal form of metaphysical determination or "in virtue of" relation. To say that A *grounds* B is to say that B holds *in virtue of* A, that A metaphysically explains B. For example, the fact that the rose is scarlet grounds the fact that the rose is red; the existence and arrangement of atoms grounds the existence of the table. Grounding is typically taken to be irreflexive, asymmetric, and transitive, and to generate a notion of relative **fundamentality**.
- **Key Issues**: Whether grounding is a single unified relation or a family of relations; whether grounding must be well-founded (**foundationalism**) or may descend infinitely (**infinitism**); the logic of ground (truth-functional or hyperintensional?); the connection between grounding and ontological commitment.
- **Source References**: Kit Fine, "The Question of Realism" (2001); Jonathan Schaffer, "On What Grounds What" (2009); Gideon Rosen, "Metaphysical Dependence: Grounding and Reduction" (2010).
- **See also**: fundamentality, ontological dependence, metaphysical foundationalism vs infinitism, grounding-check.

### Ontological Dependence
- **Definition**: A relation in which the existence or nature of one entity requires the existence or nature of another. Rigid existential dependence: A cannot exist unless B exists (e.g., a boundary depends on the object it bounds). Generic dependence: A cannot exist unless *some* entity of kind K exists (e.g., a person depends on having *some* heart). Identity dependence: A cannot be the very entity it is unless B exists. Ontological dependence need not be causal.
- **Source References**: E.J. Lowe, *The Possibility of Metaphysics* (1998), ch. 6; Kit Fine, "Ontological Dependence" (1995); Kathrin Koslicki, "Varieties of Ontological Dependence" (2013).
- **See also**: grounding, fundamentality, identity.

### Metaphysical Foundationalism
- **Definition**: The thesis that reality has a well-founded grounding structure — chains of ground must terminate in a foundational level of fundamental facts or entities. Everything non-fundamental is ultimately grounded in the fundamental. Opposed to **metaphysical infinitism**, which holds that grounding chains can descend without end.
- **Source References**: Jonathan Schaffer, "On What Grounds What" (2009); Ricki Bliss and Graham Priest (eds.), *Reality and Its Structure* (2018).
- **See also**: fundamentality, grounding, metaphysical infinitism.

### Metaphysical Infinitism
- **Definition**: The thesis that grounding chains need not terminate — there is no foundational level, and reality is structured as an infinite descent of ever more fundamental grounds, or as a non-well-founded network of dependencies. Infinitism denies that metaphysical explanation requires a bedrock.
- **Source References**: Ricki Bliss, "Viciousness and the Structure of Reality" (2013); Matteo Morganti, "Metaphysical Infinitism" (2014).
- **See also**: fundamentality, grounding, metaphysical foundationalism.

### Schaffer's Priority Monism
- **Definition**: The thesis developed by Jonathan Schaffer that the whole cosmos is the one and only fundamental concrete object, and that all other concrete objects (tables, planets, persons) are derivative, existing as dependent parts of the one fundamental whole. Monism thus reverses the priority relation assumed by atomism: the whole is prior to its parts. Schaffer's view is "priority" monism (about what is fundamental) rather than "existence" monism (about what exists).
- **Source References**: Jonathan Schaffer, "Monism: The Priority of the Whole" (2010); Schaffer, "On What Grounds What" (2009).
- **See also**: fundamentality, grounding, mereology, composition.

## 4. Essence and Modality

### Essence
- **Definition**: The set of properties that make a thing *what it is* — its nature, its real definition. Essential properties are those an entity must have to be the entity it is; they are what the entity is *in itself*, as opposed to how it merely happens to be. In post-Kripkean and Finean metaphysics, essence is not reducible to modality: not all necessary properties are essential (e.g., being such that 2+2=4 is necessary of Socrates but arguably not part of his essence).
- **Etymology**: From Latin *essentia*, from *esse* (to be). The Greek predecessor is *ousia* (substance, being).
- **Key Issues**: The relationship between essence and **de re necessity**; whether essence is definable in modal terms (the modalist view) or is primitive (the Finean view); whether essences are known a priori; essentialism about **natural kinds**.
- **Source References**: Aristotle, *Metaphysics* Book VII; Saul Kripke, *Naming and Necessity* (1980); Kit Fine, "Essence and Modality" (1994); E.J. Lowe, "Two Notions of Being: Entity and Essence" (2008).
- **See also**: accident, essential property, de re necessity, haecceity, essence-detect, natural-kind-analyze.

### Accident
- **Definition**: A property a thing has but could have lacked without ceasing to be the thing it is — a non-essential property. Socrates's snub-nosedness is an accident; being human is his essence. The essence/accident distinction goes back to Aristotle and is central to essentialist metaphysics.
- **Etymology**: From Latin *accidens* (happening, befalling), translation of Greek *symbebekos* (what happens to accompany).
- **Key Issues**: Whether the distinction between essence and accident is objective or interest-relative; whether accidents are real constituents of things or merely Cambridge properties.
- **Source References**: Aristotle, *Metaphysics* Book V, ch. 30; *Topics* Book I.
- **See also**: essence, essential property, de re necessity.

### De Re Necessity
- **Definition**: Necessity that attaches to a thing (*res*) itself, as opposed to a dictum (proposition). A statement expresses *de re* necessity when it predicates a necessary property of an object: "Socrates is necessarily human." *De re* necessity is central to essentialism and is contrasted with **de dicto necessity** ("Necessarily, all humans are mortal").
- **Etymology**: Latin, "about the thing."
- **Key Issues**: Whether *de re* necessity is coherent (Quine's skepticism); whether it reduces to *de dicto* necessity plus a rigid designator; the implications for modal epistemology.
- **Source References**: W.V.O. Quine, "Reference and Modality" (1953); Saul Kripke, *Naming and Necessity* (1980); David Lewis, *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986).
- **See also**: de dicto necessity, essence, essential property.

### De Dicto Necessity
- **Definition**: Necessity that attaches to a proposition or dictum as a whole. "Necessarily, 2+2=4" is *de dicto*: it says of the proposition *that 2+2=4* that it is necessarily true. Contrasted with **de re necessity**, which attributes necessity to a thing's possessing a property.
- **Etymology**: Latin, "about what is said."
- **Key Issues**: Whether all necessity is ultimately *de dicto* (the Quinean view) or *de re* necessity is irreducible.
- **Source References**: Alvin Plantinga, *The Nature of Necessity* (1974), ch. 2; Quine, *Word and Object* (1960).
- **See also**: de re necessity, essence, possible world.

### Essential Property
- **Definition**: A property a thing possesses in virtue of its **essence** — a property without which the thing could not be the very thing it is. Contrasted with an accidental property, which the thing has contingently. Being a person is an essential property of Socrates; being snub-nosed is not.
- **Source References**: Saul Kripke, *Naming and Necessity* (1980); Kit Fine, "Essence and Modality" (1994).
- **See also**: essence, accident, de re necessity, individual essence.

### Individual Essence
- **Definition**: The set of essential properties that are uniquely identifying — the properties that make a particular individual *that* individual and no other. An individual essence is what underwrites the identity of an object across **possible worlds**. For example, *being Socrates* or *originating from gametes G* has been proposed as constituting Socrates's individual essence.
- **Key Issues**: Whether there are individual essences that are not merely haecceitistic (**haecceity**); whether origin essentialism (Kripke) provides individual essences; whether qualitative properties alone suffice.
- **Source References**: Roderick Chisholm, *Person and Object* (1976); Graeme Forbes, *The Metaphysics of Modality* (1985).
- **See also**: essence, haecceity, transworld identity, counterpart theory.

### Haecceity
- **Definition**: The "thisness" of a particular — a primitive, non-qualitative property that is uniquely instantiated by one individual and which constitutes its individual nature. In Duns Scotus's original formulation, haecceity is that which contracts the common nature (*natura communis*) into an individual. In contemporary usage, haecceities are often invoked to explain **transworld identity** without qualitative criteria.
- **Etymology**: From Latin *haecceitas*, from *haec* (this, feminine). Coined by Duns Scotus (13th century). Sometimes called "primitive thisness."
- **Source References**: John Duns Scotus, *Ordinatio* II, d.3, q.1 (ca. 1300); Robert M. Adams, "Primitive Thisness and Primitive Identity" (1979).
- **See also**: individual essence, identity, transworld identity, essence-detect.

### Modal Realism (Lewis)
- **Definition**: The thesis, championed by David Lewis, that **possible worlds** are concrete, spatiotemporally and causally isolated universes just as real as our own — they exist in exactly the same sense. "Actual" is an indexical term: every world is actual to its own inhabitants. Modal realism offers a reductive analysis of modality: necessity is truth in all worlds, possibility is truth in some world.
- **Source References**: David Lewis, *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986); Lewis, *Counterfactuals* (1973).
- **See also**: possible world, counterpart theory, actualism, possibilism, actuality indexical.

### Actualism
- **Definition**: The thesis that everything that exists is actual — there are no merely possible entities (no possibilia). The actualist analyzes modal discourse without ontological commitment to non-actual worlds or their inhabitants. Varieties include linguistic actualism (Plantinga's modal realism with abstract worlds), and combinatorial actualism.
- **Source References**: Alvin Plantinga, *The Nature of Necessity* (1974); Robert Stalnaker, "Possible Worlds" (1976); Arthur Prior and Kit Fine, *Worlds, Times, and Selves* (1977).
- **See also**: possibilism, modal realism (Lewis), possible world, ersatzism.

### Possibilism
- **Definition**: The thesis that there are entities that do not actually exist but could have existed — non-actual possibilia. Possibilism is entailed by **modal realism (Lewis)**, but one can be a possibilist without being a full Lewisian (e.g., by accepting merely possible abstracta). Contrasted with **actualism**.
- **Source References**: David Lewis, *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986); Takashi Yagisawa, *Worlds and Individuals, Possible and Otherwise* (2010).
- **See also**: actualism, modal realism (Lewis), possible world.

## 5. Persistence and Identity

### Identity
- **Definition**: The relation that every entity bears to itself and to nothing else — the relation expressed by "=". Identity is typically characterized by Leibniz's Law (**the indiscernibility of identicals**) and by reflexivity. Numerical identity (being one and the same thing) is distinguished from qualitative identity (exact similarity). Identity is central to questions of persistence, constitution, and transworld identity.
- **Etymology**: From Latin *identitas*, from *idem* (the same).
- **Key Issues**: Whether identity is a genuine relation or a trivial logical notion; whether there are criteria of identity over time (**persistence**) and across worlds; the problem of contingent identity; relative identity (Geach).
- **Source References**: Gottlob Frege, *Foundations of Arithmetic* (1884), sections on identity; David Wiggins, *Sameness and Substance* (1980); Peter Geach, "Identity" (1967).
- **See also**: indiscernibility of identicals, identity of indiscernibles, persistence, identity-persistence.

### Indiscernibility of Identicals (Leibniz's Law)
- **Definition**: The principle that if A and B are identical, then whatever is true of A is true of B, and vice versa: ∀x ∀y (x = y → ∀F (Fx ↔ Fy)). Identity entails indiscernibility. This is a theorem of classical first-order logic with identity and is uncontroversial. The converse (**identity of indiscernibles**) is philosophically contentious.
- **Source References**: Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, *Discourse on Metaphysics* (1686), section 9; Bertrand Russell and Alfred North Whitehead, *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913).
- **See also**: identity, identity of indiscernibles, persistence.

### Identity of Indiscernibles
- **Definition**: The principle that if A and B share exactly all the same properties, then they are identical: ∀x ∀y (∀F (Fx ↔ Fy) → x = y). In a world where this principle holds, no two numerically distinct things can be perfect qualitative duplicates. The principle is controversial and appears to be false in possible worlds with perfectly symmetrical arrangements (Max Black's two-sphere universe).
- **Source References**: G.W. Leibniz, *Monadology* (1714); Max Black, "The Identity of Indiscernibles" (1952); Ian Hacking, "The Identity of Indiscernibles" (1975).
- **See also**: identity, indiscernibility of identicals, haecceity.

### Persistence
- **Definition**: The relation an object bears to its past and future selves — the "through time" analog of identity. How does an object persist from one moment to the next, surviving change? The main competing answers are **endurance** (the object is wholly present at each moment it exists) and **perdurance** (the object is a sum of temporal parts).
- **Key Issues**: Whether persistence requires temporal parts; whether an object can survive intrinsic change; the problem of temporary intrinsics (how can a single thing be both straight and bent?); the grounding of persistence conditions.
- **Source References**: David Lewis, *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986), pp. 202–204; Theodore Sider, *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001).
- **See also**: identity, endurance, perdurance, exdurantism, identity-persistence.

### Endurance (Three-Dimensionalism)
- **Definition**: The thesis that objects persist by being *wholly present* at each moment they exist. An enduring object is a three-dimensional entity that gains and loses properties over time without having distinct temporal parts. When a persisting object changes from bent to straight, it is the *very same* whole object that first has one property and then the other. Endurance is the view of common sense and of most traditional metaphysics.
- **Key Issues**: The problem of temporary intrinsics (Lewis): if endurance is true, how can one and the same object have incompatible intrinsic properties at different times? Solutions include: indexicalism (properties are time-indexed), adverbialism, and presentism.
- **Source References**: David Lewis, "Tensing the Copula" (2002); E.J. Lowe, *A Survey of Metaphysics* (2002), ch. 3; Sally Haslanger, "Endurance and Temporary Intrinsics" (1989).
- **See also**: perdurance, exdurantism, persistence, identity-persistence.

### Perdurance (Four-Dimensionalism)
- **Definition**: The thesis that objects persist by having distinct **temporal parts** at different times — they *perdure*. A persisting object is a four-dimensional spacetime "worm" whose temporal stages are just as real as its spatial parts. Change consists in different temporal parts having incompatible intrinsic properties. Perdurance is typically paired with **eternalism** about time.
- **Key Issues**: Whether perdurance is consistent with the nature of ordinary objects (is a person a sum of temporal stages?); the grounding of temporal-part talk; whether perdurance requires substantivalism about spacetime.
- **Source References**: David Lewis, *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986); Theodore Sider, *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001); Mark Heller, *The Ontology of Physical Objects* (1990).
- **See also**: endurance, exdurantism, temporal parts, persistence.

### Temporal Parts
- **Definition**: The momentary or temporally extended slices of a persisting four-dimensional object. On the **perdurance** view, a persisting object is the mereological sum (fusion) of its temporal parts, just as an extended object is the sum of its spatial parts. A temporal part of an object at time *t* is the part of that object that exists only at *t* and shares all the object's *t*-local properties.
- **Source References**: Theodore Sider, *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001); David Lewis, *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986), pp. 202–204.
- **See also**: perdurance, exdurantism, endurance, mereology.

### Exdurantism (Stage Theory)
- **Definition**: The thesis that ordinary objects (persons, tables) are not four-dimensional worms but are instead momentary stages — instantaneous temporal parts that are the primary subjects of predication. What we call a persisting object is really a series of numerically distinct stages related by a temporal counterpart relation. When we say "Socrates was young," we are referring to an earlier temporal counterpart of the present Socrates-stage. Exdurantism is a radical alternative to both **endurance** and **perdurance**.
- **Source References**: Theodore Sider, *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001); Katherine Hawley, *How Things Persist* (2001); J.J. Thomson, "Parthood and Identity Across Time" (1983).
- **See also**: perdurance, endurance, temporal parts, counterpart theory, identity-persistence.

| View               | What persisting object *is*               | Relation to time-slices          | Main challenge                               |
|--------------------|-------------------------------------------|----------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| Endurance          | A 3D object wholly present at each moment | Has no temporal parts            | Problem of temporary intrinsics              |
| Perdurance (worm)  | A 4D spacetime worm                       | Is the mereological sum of parts | Counterintuitive for ordinary objects        |
| Exdurantism (stage)| A momentary stage (with counterparts)     | *Is* the stage; others are counterparts | Explaining "same object" talk across time    |

## 6. Composition and Parts

### Mereology
- **Definition**: The formal theory of parts and wholes. Classical mereology (as axiomatized by Lesniewski and Goodman/Leonard) is governed by three axioms: (1) **parthood** is transitive, (2) parthood is antisymmetric, and (3) for any non-empty collection of objects, their **fusion** exists (unrestricted composition). Mereology is applied to questions of material constitution, persistence, and ontological structure.
- **Etymology**: From Greek *meros* (part) + *logos* (study).
- **Source References**: Stanislaw Lesniewski, *Foundations of a General Theory of Sets* (1916, in Polish); Nelson Goodman and Henry Leonard, "The Calculus of Individuals and Its Uses" (1940); Peter Simons, *Parts: A Study in Ontology* (1987).
- **See also**: parthood, composition, fusion, special composition question.

### Parthood
- **Definition**: The relation that holds between a part and the whole of which it is a part. In classical mereology, parthood is a partial ordering: reflexive (everything is part of itself), transitive (a part of a part is a part of the whole), and antisymmetric (if A is part of B and B is part of A, then A = B). **Proper parthood** adds irreflexivity.
- **Source References**: Peter Simons, *Parts: A Study in Ontology* (1987); Achille Varzi, "Mereology" (2019, SEP entry).
- **See also**: mereology, proper part, composition.

### Proper Part
- **Definition**: A part of an object that is not identical to that object — a "genuine" part, as opposed to the improper part (the whole itself). If the wheel is a proper part of the car, then the wheel is part of the car and the wheel is not identical to the car.
- **See also**: parthood, composition, mereology.

### Composition
- **Definition**: The relation between a whole and the plurality of its parts, or the metaphysical "putting together" of parts into a whole. The central question is the **special composition question** (van Inwagen): under what conditions do some objects compose something? Answers range from **mereological nihilism** (never) to **mereological universalism** (always) to various intermediate restrictions.
- **Source References**: Peter van Inwagen, *Material Beings* (1990); David Lewis, *Parts of Classes* (1991).
- **See also**: fusion, special composition question, mereological nihilism, mereological universalism.

### Fusion
- **Definition**: The mereological sum — for any objects X, the fusion of X is the unique object that overlaps all and only those things that overlap at least one of the Xs. A fusion has its parts essentially (in classical mereology). The existence of arbitrary fusions is the controversial axiom of **mereological universalism**.
- **Source References**: David Lewis, *Parts of Classes* (1991); Peter van Inwagen, *Material Beings* (1990).
- **See also**: composition, mereological universalism, special composition question.

### Mereological Nihilism
- **Definition**: The thesis that composition never occurs — there are no composite objects, only mereologically **simples** (partless entities) arranged in various patterns. According to nihilism, there are no tables, chairs, or persons; there are only simples arranged table-wise, chair-wise, and person-wise. Nihilism is the most radical answer to the **special composition question**.
- **Source References**: Peter van Inwagen, *Material Beings* (1990), for a restricted version; Ted Sider, "Against Parthood" (2013); Cian Dorr, "The Simplicity of Everything" (2002).
- **See also**: mereological universalism, special composition question, simples, gunk.

### Mereological Universalism
- **Definition**: The thesis that for any objects whatsoever, there is a fusion — composition is unrestricted. Any collection of objects, however gerrymandered or scattered, composes something. Universalism is defended on grounds of simplicity and avoidance of vague existence, but it generates an ontology of bizarre objects (trout-turkeys, the mereological sum of a person's nose and the Eiffel Tower).
- **Source References**: David Lewis, *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986), pp. 211–213; Theodore Sider, *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001).
- **See also**: mereological nihilism, composition, fusion, special composition question.

### Special Composition Question (van Inwagen)
- **Definition**: Formulated by Peter van Inwagen: "Under what conditions do some objects compose something?" The question seeks the necessary and sufficient conditions for composition to occur. Van Inwagen's own answer (in *Material Beings*, 1990) is that composition occurs exactly when the activity of the parts constitutes a life — a form of restricted composition (organicism). The question frames the entire contemporary debate about material constitution.
- **Source References**: Peter van Inwagen, *Material Beings* (1990); Ned Markosian, "Brutal Composition" (1998).
- **See also**: composition, mereological nihilism, mereological universalism.

### Simples
- **Definition**: Entities that have no **proper parts** — mereologically atomic entities. Simples are the ultimate constituents of reality on any view that accepts a fundamental level of decomposition. Whether there are simples, and whether they are point-sized or extended, is a central question in fundamental metaphysics and philosophy of physics.
- **Source References**: Kris McDaniel, "Extended Simples" (2007); Ned Markosian, "Simples" (1998).
- **See also**: gunk, parthood, mereological nihilism, grounding-check.

### Gunk
- **Definition**: Matter (or objects) such that every part of it has further proper parts — gunk is infinitely divisible, with no mereological **simples** at the bottom. The world is "gunky" if there is no fundamental level of partless entities; everything has parts all the way down. Gunk is compatible with **metaphysical infinitism** about grounding.
- **Etymology**: Coined by David Lewis, *Parts of Classes* (1991), p. 20: "whatever is gunky consists entirely of gunk."
- **Source References**: David Lewis, *Parts of Classes* (1991); Theodore Sider, "Van Inwagen and the Possibility of Gunk" (1993); Arntzenius, "Gunk, Topology, and Measure" (2008).
- **See also**: simples, mereological nihilism, metaphysical infinitism, composition.

## 7. Causation

### Causation
- **Definition**: The relation between cause and effect — the "cement of the universe" (Mackie/Hume). Causation is typically understood as a relation of production, dependence, or counterfactual covariation between distinct events, facts, or states. Theories of causation aim to answer: what is it for C to cause E, and what distinguishes causal from merely accidental sequences?
- **Etymology**: From Latin *causatio*, from *causa* (cause). The philosophical analysis originates with Aristotle's four causes and receives its modern empiricist treatment in Hume.
- **Key Issues**: Whether causation is a fundamental relation or reducible to non-causal patterns (regularity, counterfactuals, interventions); the ontology of causal relata (events, facts, properties, objects); the problems of overdetermination and preemption.
- **Source References**: David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739), Book I, Part III; David Lewis, "Causation" (1973); Judea Pearl, *Causality* (2000).
- **See also**: causal relata, Humean supervenience, regularity theory, counterfactual theory, interventionism, causation-analyze.

### Causal Relata
- **Definition**: The items that stand in the causal relation — what sorts of entities are causes and effects. The main candidates are: (i) **events** (Davidson, Kim, Lewis) — particular dated occurrences; (ii) **facts** (Mellor, Bennett) — true propositions that constitute states of the world; (iii) **properties** (Shoemaker) — the instantiation of a causal power; and (iv) **objects** (Aristotle) — substances acting on substances. The choice of relata shapes the logic of causation.
- **Source References**: Donald Davidson, "Causal Relations" (1967); Jaegwon Kim, "Events as Property Exemplifications" (1976); D.H. Mellor, *The Facts of Causation* (1995).
- **See also**: causation, event, fact.

### Humean Supervenience
- **Definition**: The thesis, named and championed by David Lewis, that all facts about the world supervene on the spatiotemporal distribution of perfectly natural, intrinsic, local properties — the "Humean mosaic." There are no necessary connections between distinct existences. Causation, laws of nature, and chance are not fundamental but are patterns in the mosaic. Named after Hume's dictum that there are no necessary connections between distinct existences.
- **Source References**: David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748), Section VII; David Lewis, *Philosophical Papers*, vol. II (1986), Introduction; Barry Loewer, "Humean Supervenience" (1996).
- **See also**: causation, regularity theory, counterfactual theory.

### Regularity Theory of Causation
- **Definition**: The Humean theory that causation is nothing more than constant conjunction: C causes E if events of type C are regularly followed by events of type E, and there is no independent necessary connection between them. The theory faces the problem of distinguishing causal from accidental regularities, and the problem of singular causation (a cause can be a cause of its effect without being an instance of a regularity).
- **Source References**: David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739), Book I, Part III; J.L. Mackie, *The Cement of the Universe* (1974); John Stuart Mill, *A System of Logic* (1843).
- **See also**: causation, Humean supervenience, counterfactual theory.

### Counterfactual Theory of Causation (Lewis)
- **Definition**: The analysis of causation in terms of counterfactual conditionals: C causes E if and only if (i) C and E both occur, and (ii) if C had not occurred, E would not have occurred. Lewis (1973) refines this with a semantics for counterfactuals based on similarity of **possible worlds**. The theory handles many cases but struggles with **preemption** and **overdetermination**.
- **Source References**: David Lewis, "Causation" (1973); Lewis, "Counterfactual Dependence and Time's Arrow" (1979); Lewis, *Counterfactuals* (1973).
- **See also**: causation, possible world, overdetermination, preemption, causation-analyze.

### Interventionism (Woodward)
- **Definition**: The theory of causation and explanation developed by James Woodward according to which X causes Y if and only if there is a possible intervention on X (an idealized experimental manipulation that "breaks" X's other causal inputs) such that changing X changes Y (or Y's probability distribution). Interventionism is non-reductive (it uses causal notions to define causation) but is designed to capture the epistemology and practice of causal inference in science.
- **Source References**: James Woodward, *Making Things Happen: A Theory of Causal Explanation* (2003); Woodward, "Causation in Science" (2014).
- **See also**: causation, counterfactual theory, causation-analyze.

### Causal Exclusion Argument (Kim)
- **Definition**: An argument by Jaegwon Kim against non-reductive physicalism about the mental. If every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause (the causal closure of the physical), and mental-to-physical causation would overdetermine physical effects, then (by an exclusion principle) mental causation is excluded — mental events are epiphenomenal. The argument generalizes to any higher-level property purportedly causing effects already caused by their physical realizers.
- **Source References**: Jaegwon Kim, "Mechanism, Purpose, and Explanatory Exclusion" (1989); Kim, *Mind in a Physical World* (1998); Kim, *Physicalism, or Something Near Enough* (2005).
- **See also**: causation, overdetermination, grounding-check.

### Overdetermination
- **Definition**: A situation in which an effect has multiple causes, each of which would have been sufficient on its own to bring about the effect. For example, two rocks simultaneously strike a window, each sufficient to shatter it. Overdetermination is pervasive in symmetric cases but, some argue (Kim), objectionable when it involves distinct levels of reality in a systematic way.
- **Source References**: Jaegwon Kim, *Mind in a Physical World* (1998); Jonathan Schaffer, "Overdetermining Causes" (2003); David Lewis, "Causation" (1973).
- **See also**: causation, causal exclusion argument, preemption.

### Preemption
- **Definition**: A causal scenario in which one cause acts "first" (or a backup is preempted), preventing an alternative cause from bringing about the effect. In *early preemption*, C1 causes E and thereby cuts off C2's causal pathway. In *late preemption*, C2's process is cut off after C1 has already brought about E. Preemption cases challenge simple counterfactual theories because the preempted cause still has a would-be counterfactual dependence on the effect.
- **Source References**: David Lewis, "Causation" (1973) and "Postscripts to 'Causation'" (1986); Jonathan Schaffer, "Trumping Preemption" (2000); Ned Hall, "Two Concepts of Causation" (2004).
- **See also**: causation, counterfactual theory, overdetermination.

## 8. Possible Worlds

### Possible World
- **Definition**: A complete way things could have been — a maximal possible state of affairs, or a maximal consistent set of propositions. The notion is used to analyze modality (possibility is truth in some possible world; necessity is truth in all possible worlds), counterfactuals, and content (propositions as sets of worlds). The ontological status of possible worlds is hotly contested: are they concrete (Lewis), abstract (Plantinga, Stalnaker), or merely linguistic fictions (fictionalism)?
- **Source References**: Saul Kripke, "Semantical Considerations on Modal Logic" (1963); David Lewis, *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986); Alvin Plantinga, *The Nature of Necessity* (1974).
- **See also**: modal realism (Lewis), actualism, ersatzism, accessibility relation, conceivability-arg.

### Accessibility Relation
- **Definition**: In possible worlds semantics for modal logic, an accessibility relation R between worlds specifies which worlds are "possible relative to" a given world. A proposition is *necessarily* true at world w iff it is true at every world w' such that wRw'. Different modal logics correspond to different formal properties of R (reflexivity yields T; reflexivity + transitivity + symmetry yields S5). Philosophically, the accessibility relation captures the idea that what is necessary relative to one set of assumptions may not be necessary relative to another.
- **Source References**: Saul Kripke, "Semantical Analysis of Modal Logic I" (1963); G.E. Hughes and M.J. Cresswell, *A New Introduction to Modal Logic* (1996).
- **See also**: possible world, conceivability-arg.

### Counterpart Theory (Lewis)
- **Definition**: David Lewis's account of *de re* modality according to which an object has a property essentially just in case all of its **counterparts** (in other possible worlds) have that property. Crucially, individuals are world-bound — no object exists in more than one world. Instead of **transworld identity**, we have a counterpart relation based on similarity. Counterpart theory avoids the metaphysical puzzles of transworld identity but faces the "Humphrey objection" (Kripke): Humphrey cares about *himself* winning, not about what his counterparts do.
- **Source References**: David Lewis, "Counterpart Theory and Quantified Modal Logic" (1968); Lewis, *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986).
- **See also**: possible world, modal realism (Lewis), transworld identity, identity-persistence.

### Transworld Identity
- **Definition**: The thesis (or the problem of explaining the thesis) that one and the same individual can exist in multiple possible worlds. When we say "Socrates could have been a carpenter," we seem to be speaking of the very same Socrates in a non-actual situation. The problem is: what makes a Socrates* in another world identical with our Socrates? Proposed solutions invoke **individual essences**, **haecceities**, or primitive transworld identity. Rejected by **counterpart theory**.
- **Source References**: David Kaplan, "Transworld Heir Lines" (1979); Alvin Plantinga, *The Nature of Necessity* (1974); Saul Kripke, *Naming and Necessity* (1980).
- **See also**: counterpart theory, individual essence, haecceity, possible world.

### Actuality Indexical
- **Definition**: David Lewis's thesis that "actual" is an indexical term, like "here" or "now." Just as "here" picks out the location of the speaker, "actual" picks out the world of the speaker. Every world is actual to its own inhabitants. This allows Lewis to maintain that all possible worlds are equally real while explaining why we privilege "this" world: it is ours.
- **Source References**: David Lewis, "Anselm and Actuality" (1970); Lewis, *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986), pp. 92–96.
- **See also**: modal realism (Lewis), possible world.

### Ersatzism
- **Definition**: A family of actualist views about possible worlds that accept the utility of possible worlds discourse but deny Lewis's concrete realism. Ersatz worlds are abstract representations — maximal consistent sets of propositions (linguistic ersatzism), maximal states of affairs (Plantinga), or maximal structural universals (Forrest) — that exist within the actual world. Ersatzism claims the benefits of worlds semantics without the ontological cost.
- **Etymology**: From German *Ersatz* (replacement, substitute).
- **Source References**: Alvin Plantinga, *The Nature of Necessity* (1974); Robert Stalnaker, *Inquiry* (1984); Peter Forrest, "Ways Worlds Could Be" (1986).
- **See also**: actualism, possible world, modal realism (Lewis), combinatorialism.

### Combinatorialism
- **Definition**: A theory of modality according to which possible worlds are *recombinations* of the basic elements of the actual world — all possible worlds are simply rearrangements of actual simples and actual properties. The view is attractive to actualists because it avoids positing alien entities (entities not instantiated in the actual world) and explains the range of possibilities in terms of a combinatorial principle.
- **Source References**: D.M. Armstrong, *A Combinatorial Theory of Possibility* (1989); Brian Skyrms, "Tractarian Nominalism" (1981).
- **See also**: actualism, ersatzism, possible world, conceivability-arg.

## 9. Time and Space

### A-Theory of Time
- **Definition**: The family of views according to which temporal passage — the movement of the present moment from past to future — is an objective feature of reality. The A-theory holds that there is a genuine ontological distinction between past, present, and future, and that which time is present is not merely a matter of perspective. Key variants include **presentism**, the **growing block** theory, and the **moving spotlight** theory.
- **Etymology**: Named after J.M.E. McTaggart's "A-series" (1908): the series of positions running from the far past through the near past to the present, and from the present to the near future and far future.
- **Source References**: J.M.E. McTaggart, "The Unreality of Time" (1908); A.N. Prior, "The Notion of the Present" (1970); Dean Zimmerman, "The A-Theory of Time, the B-Theory of Time, and 'Taking Tense Seriously'" (2005).
- **See also**: B-theory, presentism, eternalism, growing block, moving spotlight.

### B-Theory of Time
- **Definition**: The view that time is fundamentally a static dimension of reality — temporal passage is not an objective feature of the world but merely a subjective or perspectival phenomenon. The B-theory (named after McTaggart's B-series, the series of positions running from earlier to later) holds that past, present, and future are equally real. The B-theory is often paired with **eternalism** and the tenseless theory of time.
- **Source References**: J.M.E. McTaggart, "The Unreality of Time" (1908); J.J.C. Smart, "The River of Time" (1949); D.H. Mellor, *Real Time* (1981); Huw Mellor, *Real Time II* (1998).
- **See also**: A-theory, eternalism, temporal ontology.

### Presentism
- **Definition**: The thesis, within **A-theory**, that only present entities and events exist. The past no longer exists; the future does not yet exist. Presentism captures the intuitive idea that the present is uniquely real but faces the problem of grounding truths about the past (what makes "Caesar crossed the Rubicon" true if there is no past event?) and cross-temporal relations.
- **Source References**: A.N. Prior, *Papers on Time and Tense* (1968); John Bigelow, "Presentism and Properties" (1996); Craig Bourne, *A Future for Presentism* (2006).
- **See also**: A-theory, eternalism, growing block.

### Eternalism
- **Definition**: The thesis, typically paired with the **B-theory**, that all times — past, present, and future — are equally real. The universe is a four-dimensional block in which temporal location is analogous to spatial location: just as distant places are no less real than here, distant times are no less real than now. Eternalism is the natural temporal ontology of **perdurance** and special relativity.
- **Source References**: J.J.C. Smart, *Philosophy and Scientific Realism* (1963); Theodore Sider, *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001); Huw Mellor, *Real Time II* (1998).
- **See also**: B-theory, presentism, perdurance, growing block.

### Growing Block
- **Definition**: A hybrid A-theory according to which the past and present are real, but the future is not — reality is an expanding block. As time passes, new slices of reality come into being at the leading edge (the present). Past events persist in the block. The view avoids the truthmaking problems of presentism but faces the "epistemic problem": how do we know we are at the edge of the block?
- **Source References**: C.D. Broad, *Scientific Thought* (1923); Michael Tooley, *Time, Tense, and Causation* (1997); Peter Forrest, "The Real but Dead Past: A Reply to Braddon-Mitchell" (2004).
- **See also**: A-theory, presentism, eternalism.

### Moving Spotlight
- **Definition**: A variant of the A-theory according to which all times (past, present, and future) exist (as per eternalism), but there is additionally an objective, moving "spotlight" of presentness that illuminates successive times. The present is ontologically privileged not by being the only thing that exists, but by being the bearer of a special monadic property of *presentness*.
- **Source References**: C.D. Broad, *Scientific Thought* (1923); Quentin Smith, *Language and Time* (1993); Ross Cameron, *The Moving Spotlight* (2015).
- **See also**: A-theory, eternalism, growing block.

### Temporal Ontology
- **Definition**: The subfield of metaphysics concerned with the existence and nature of time and temporal entities. Central questions include: Do past and future entities exist? Is time real or ideal? Does time "flow"? Is time continuous or discrete? Temporal ontology encompasses debates between **presentism**, **eternalism**, **growing block**, and **moving spotlight** theories.
- **Source References**: Theodore Sider, *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001); Robin Le Poidevin, *The Images of Time* (2007).
- **See also**: A-theory, B-theory, presentism, eternalism.

### Substantivalism about Spacetime
- **Definition**: The thesis (defended by Newton and Clarke against Leibniz) that spacetime is a substance in its own right — an entity that exists independently of the material objects and events located within it. Spacetime points or regions are real, concrete entities. By contrast, **relationalism** holds that spacetime is merely a system of spatiotemporal relations among material bodies, with no independent existence.
- **Source References**: Isaac Newton, *Principia Mathematica* (1687), Scholium on Space and Time; G.W. Leibniz, correspondence with Samuel Clarke (1715–1716); Lawrence Sklar, *Space, Time, and Spacetime* (1974).
- **See also**: relationalism about spacetime, temporal ontology.

### Relationalism about Spacetime
- **Definition**: The thesis (defended by Leibniz, Mach, and some interpretations of general relativity) that spacetime is not a substance but merely the network of spatiotemporal relations among material things. Space is nothing over and above the spatial relations between bodies; time is nothing over and above the temporal relations between events. Contrasted with **substantivalism**.
- **Source References**: G.W. Leibniz, *Third Paper to Clarke* (1716); Ernst Mach, *The Science of Mechanics* (1883); John Earman, *World Enough and Space-Time* (1989).
- **See also**: substantivalism about spacetime, relation, temporal ontology.

| Question                          | Position A                                    | Position B                                      |
|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------|
| Does time pass?                   | A-Theory: Yes, objectively                    | B-Theory: No, passage is perspectival           |
| Do past & future exist?           | Presentism: No (only present)                 | Eternalism: Yes (all times equally real)        |
| Is spacetime a substance?         | Substantivalism: Yes (independent entity)     | Relationalism: No (network of relations)        |
| How do objects persist?           | Endurance: 3D, wholly present at each time    | Perdurance: 4D, sum of temporal parts           |

## 10. Meta-Ontology

### Meta-Ontology
- **Definition**: The study of the methodology, epistemology, and semantics of ontological inquiry — second-order questions *about* ontology rather than first-order questions *within* ontology. Meta-ontology asks: What is an ontological dispute about? How, if at all, can ontological disputes be resolved? Is ontology a substantive inquiry or a matter of linguistic convention?
- **Source References**: Peter van Inwagen, "Meta-Ontology" (1998); David Chalmers, "Ontological Anti-Realism" (2009); Amie Thomasson, *Ontology Made Easy* (2015).
- **See also**: ontology, quantifier variance, deflationism, Carnap's internal/external distinction.

### Quantifier Variance (Hirsch)
- **Definition**: The thesis developed by Eli Hirsch that different ontological frameworks (e.g., one that includes composite objects and one that does not) employ different *meanings* of the existential quantifier ("there exists"). Each party speaks truly in their own language; the dispute between them is merely verbal. Quantifier variance is a form of ontological deflationism that undermines heavyweight ontological realism.
- **Source References**: Eli Hirsch, "Quantifier Variance and Realism" (2002); Hirsch, *Quantifier Variance and Realism: Essays in Meta-Ontology* (2011); Theodore Sider, *Writing the Book of the World* (2011), ch. 9 for a critique.
- **See also**: meta-ontology, deflationism, Quinean criterion of ontological commitment.

### Deflationism (Meta-Ontology)
- **Definition**: The view that ontological questions are easier, shallower, or less substantive than traditional metaphysics supposes. Deflationists hold that most ontological disputes can be resolved by conceptual analysis, linguistic frameworks, or trivial entailments. Varieties include neo-Carnapian deflationism, quantifier variance, and easy ontology (Thomasson).
- **Source References**: Amie Thomasson, *Ontology Made Easy* (2015); Eli Hirsch, *Quantifier Variance and Realism* (2011); Hilary Putnam, *Ethics without Ontology* (2004).
- **See also**: meta-ontology, quantifier variance, heavyweight vs lightweight realism.

### Heavyweight vs. Lightweight Realism
- **Definition**: A distinction concerning the depth of ontological questions. *Heavyweight realism* holds that ontological questions are deep, substantive, and often require fundamentally different pictures of reality to adjudicate — they are comparable to scientific questions. *Lightweight realism* (or deflationism) holds that ontological questions are shallow — they can be resolved by linguistic decisions, trivial inferences, or conceptual truths, without deep metaphysical investigation.
- **Source References**: David Chalmers, "Ontological Anti-Realism" (2009) in *Metametaphysics* (eds. Chalmers, Manley, and Wasserman); Theodore Sider, *Writing the Book of the World* (2011).
- **See also**: meta-ontology, deflationism.

### Carnap's Internal/External Distinction
- **Definition**: Rudolf Carnap's influential distinction between questions asked *within* a linguistic framework (internal questions, e.g., "Are there numbers?" answered within the framework of arithmetic: trivially, yes) and questions asked *about* which framework to adopt (external questions, which are practical decisions, not factual ones). Carnap uses the distinction to argue that traditional ontological disputes are pseudo-questions couched in the misleading "material mode of speech."
- **Source References**: Rudolf Carnap, "Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology" (1950); W.V.O. Quine, "On Carnap's Views on Ontology" (1951); Matti Eklund, "Carnap and Ontological Pluralism" (2009).
- **See also**: meta-ontology, deflationism, quantifier variance.

## Cross-Cutting Debates and Further Connections

This section briefly maps several cross-cutting philosophical tensions that recur across the categories above:

- **Realism vs. Nominalism** runs through categories 2 (properties), 6 (composition), and 10 (meta-ontology). A thoroughgoing realist posits universals, composite objects, and substantive ontological facts; a thoroughgoing nominalist denies all three.
- **Fundamentality vs. Derivative** structures categories 3 (grounding, dependence), 7 (Humean vs. non-Humean causation), and 9 (substantivalism vs. relationalism). Each debate concerns what is basic and what is constructed.
- **Actualism vs. Possibilism** unifies categories 4 (essence and modality), 5 (counterpart theory), and 8 (possible worlds). The disagreement about the ontological status of possibilia ramifies into semantics, logic, and the epistemology of modality.
- **Three-Dimensionalism vs. Four-Dimensionalism** connects categories 5 (persistence), 6 (composition — temporal parts), and 9 (eternalism vs. presentism). One's choice of temporal ontology strongly constrains one's theory of persistence and vice versa.
