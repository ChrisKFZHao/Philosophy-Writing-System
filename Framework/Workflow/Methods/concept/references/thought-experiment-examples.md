## Example: Thomson's "Violinist" (1971)

### Input

> Thomson's "Violinist" thought experiment (Thomson, "A Defense of Abortion", 1971):
> You wake up one morning to find yourself back-to-back in bed with a famous violinist.
> The violinist suffers from a fatal kidney ailment, and only your kidneys can filter his blood.
> The Society of Music Lovers kidnapped you while you were asleep and connected your circulatory system to the violinist's.
> If you unplug the tubes, the violinist will die; if you stay in place for nine months, he will recover,
> and you can then safely disconnect. Are you obligated to stay in place for nine months?

### Output

```
## Thought Experiment Analysis Report

### Scenario Decomposition
- Setting: You wake up in a hospital bed, connected to another person
- Actors: You (an ordinary person), the Violinist (needs your kidneys)
- Events: Kidnapped → connected → choice: stay in place or unplug
- Key Detail: (a) the violinist's life depends on your body;
             (b) you did not consent (you were kidnapped); (c) it takes 9 months.
- Counterfactual Hinge: Forced bodily connection — this is not a common medical scenario in the actual world

### Intuition-Triggering Mechanism
- Intuition source: Everyday-experience intuition about "the right to autonomous choice" (being coerced → no obligation)
- Reliability: High (the autonomy intuition is common in everyday moral reasoning)
- Possible contrary intuition: Conservatives may intuit "the value of life > autonomy"

### Conclusion Derivation Pathway
Scenario description
  → Intuitive response: "I have no obligation to stay in place" — I did not consent
    → Generalization: A person has no obligation to use their own body to sustain another person's life,
              even if that person would die without their help, especially when they did not consent
      → Conclusion: Prohibiting abortion entails granting the fetus a right to use the mother's body —
              but this conflicts with our general judgments about autonomy

Step 3: Generalizing from the "violinist case" to the "abortion case" involves analogical reasoning —
      is the relation between fetus and mother analogous to the relation between violinist and kidnap victim?
      Key disanalogies: (a) in most abortion cases, the mother has had sexual intercourse;
               (b) the violinist is a stranger to you, whereas the mother has a special filial obligation to the fetus.

### Presupposition Audit
- Presupposition 1: Bodily autonomy universally outweighs the obligation to save a life (in the absence of consent)
- Presupposition 2: The two cases are sufficiently structurally isomorphic along the dimension of "bodily use"
- Presupposition 3: Abortion and "unplugging" are similar lethal choices (though there are relevant differences)

### Alternative-Case Test
- "Replace the violinist with your adult child" — does the intuition change?
  → Many would feel a stronger obligation toward their own child, which challenges the validity of the basic analogy

### Force Assessment
| Dimension | Assessment |
|-----------|------------|
| Scenario conceivability | Medium (the scenario has a science-fictional flavor but is logically consistent) |
| Intuition reliability | Medium-High (the "autonomy" intuition is reliable, but the analogical validity is disputed) |
| Derivational validity | Partially valid (the transferability of the analogy is the central point of contention) |
| Presupposition transparency | Medium (key disanalogies in the analogy are insufficiently discussed) |
| Contribution to the argument | Core argument — without this thought experiment, Thomson's case would be substantially weakened |
```
