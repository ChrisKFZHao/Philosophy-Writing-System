# Methodological Analysis (method.dialectic.methodology)

> Internal method module — not a registered runtime skill. Loaded on demand by the consuming skill's mode contract through the manifest resolver; never loaded wholesale.

# Philosophical Methodology Analysis

## Overview

Philosophical methodology is a second-order inquiry: it asks not what arguments conclude, but how they reason. Every philosophical argument rests on methodological commitments — often implicit and unexamined — that determine what counts as evidence, which inferential moves are legitimate, what standards of proof the argument aspires to meet, and what kind of answer the argument is structurally capable of delivering.

A transcendental argument, a conceptual analysis, and an experimental philosophy study may all address the same philosophical question, but they differ fundamentally in their data sources, their inferential norms, their conception of philosophical progress, and their criteria for success. When we evaluate arguments without attending to methodology, we risk applying standards the argument never undertook to satisfy — demanding empirical evidence from a conceptual analysis, or conceptual precision from a phenomenological description — and missing structural weaknesses that render the argument unsound on its own terms. Methodological analysis makes these commitments explicit so they can be evaluated deliberately rather than absorbed uncritically from disciplinary habit.

The contemporary landscape of philosophical methodology has been shaped by several major developments that together constitute what some commentators call the methodological turn.

First, the rise of experimental philosophy beginning in the early 2000s challenged the armchair methods of mainstream analytic philosophy by treating claims about folk intuitions as empirical hypotheses requiring empirical testing — a challenge that forced defenders of traditional methods to articulate their methodological commitments with unprecedented clarity and to distinguish between what armchair methods can and cannot deliver.

Second, the growing influence of formal methods drawn from logic, probability theory, game theory, and computer science has expanded the toolkit available to philosophers while raising questions about whether formalization illuminates or distorts philosophical subject matter — a live debate in metaphysics, epistemology, and ethics.

Third, the increasing cross-pollination between analytic and continental traditions has brought genealogical, phenomenological, and hermeneutic methods into contact with conceptual analysis and naturalism, creating both productive methodological hybridity and deep methodological disagreement about what philosophical inquiry should aim at.

Fourth, the replication crisis in psychology and related fields has prompted methodological scrutiny of philosophical work that depends on empirical results, including naturalized epistemology, moral psychology, and experimental philosophy itself.

Fifth, recent work on conceptual engineering and metalinguistic negotiation has blurred the boundary between describing our concepts and revising them, raising new questions about what conceptual analysis is and what it is for.

In this environment, the ability to identify, classify, and critically evaluate methodological commitments is not a peripheral scholarly virtue but a core competency for responsible philosophical engagement. An argument cannot be assessed solely by its internal deductive logic when its methodological framework pre-selects certain kinds of evidence as admissible, excludes certain counterarguments from the space of serious consideration, or embeds substantive philosophical assumptions that the evaluator does not share.

This skill approaches methodology analysis with a particular philosophical stance: methodological pluralism without methodological relativism. It holds that there are genuine methodological disagreements — not all methods are equally good for all purposes — but that the evaluation of a method must be contextual, keyed to the specific argument and its specific aims, rather than proceeding from a pre-commitment to the superiority of any single method. The skill does not assume that conceptual analysis is always legitimate or always bankrupt; it does not assume that naturalism is always intellectually responsible or always reductive; it does not assume that phenomenology always accesses genuine experiential structures or always traffics in idiosyncratic introspection. Instead, it provides the analytical tools for making these evaluations on a case-by-case basis, with full awareness of the philosophical stakes. This skill provides a systematic, evidence-grounded, and philosophically sophisticated framework for detecting, classifying, and critically examining the methodological dimension of philosophical reasoning.

## Input

**Required:** A philosophical text, argument, or passage. This may be a complete journal article, a chapter or section of a monograph, a reconstructed argument in premise-conclusion form, or an argument map produced by the method.argument.argument-reconstruct skill. The input should be sufficiently self-contained that the argument's structure, aims, and evidential basis are discernible. Fragmentary passages (single paragraphs, isolated remarks) may not provide enough material for robust methodological analysis and should be contextualized with surrounding text where possible.

**Optional but Strongly Recommended:** The author's explicit methodological statements, drawn from prefaces, methodological sections, meta-philosophical essays, or interviews. When an author has articulated their methodological views elsewhere, this material provides crucial context for interpreting the methodological commitments operative in a particular argument — an author who has defended conceptual analysis elsewhere is more plausibly interpreted as employing conceptual analysis than one who has rejected it. Additionally useful: the disciplinary context (analytic, continental, pragmatist, or hybrid — since different traditions have different default methods), the publication venue (which often carries implicit methodological norms — a paper in *Mind* and a paper in *Continental Philosophy Review* operate with different methodological expectations), any known methodological criticisms the argument has received in the secondary literature, and the argument's intellectual genealogy — which philosophers or traditions it explicitly or implicitly follows in its methodological choices.

**What the skill cannot do given limited or ambiguous input:** When the input text is very short (a single paragraph or aphorism), highly elliptical, or written in a deliberately non-argumentative style, the skill's output will necessarily be tentative and partial. In such cases, the report should explicitly flag its tentativeness and identify what additional contextual information would be needed for a more definitive methodological analysis. The skill also cannot adjudicate foundational disputes about which methods are legitimate tout court — it provides tools for contextual evaluation but is not itself a complete theory of philosophical methodology that would settle the debates between, say, naturalists and proponents of a priori methods. Those debates are the subject matter of first-order methodological philosophy; this skill provides the analytical framework for engaging them, not their resolution.

## Output

A structured methodology report containing the following components. The report should present findings at a level of detail sufficient for a philosophically literate reader to evaluate the analysis independently — specific textual evidence, not merely summary judgments — while remaining concise enough to serve as a practical analytical tool rather than a commentary on the entire text.

**1. Detected Methods** — Each identified method is classified by type according to the standard taxonomy (see below), accompanied by specific textual or structural evidence for its presence in the argument. This section distinguishes between methods the author explicitly claims to employ (explicit methodology) and methods the argument actually exhibits in its operational detail (practiced methodology) — these do not always coincide, and the gap between professed and practiced method is often methodologically revealing. For example, an author may announce that they are performing conceptual analysis while their argument actually proceeds by reflective equilibrium (revising both principles and particular judgments iteratively rather than testing fixed analyses against case judgments). When multiple methods are detected, their relationships are characterized: are they complementary (each addressing a different aspect of the question), competing (offering incompatible approaches to the same question), nested (one method operating within the framework established by another), or merely juxtaposed (present in the same text without integration)?

**2. Method-Argument Fit Assessment** — An evaluation of whether the chosen method, or combination of methods, is appropriate for the argument's stated aims and the nature of the philosophical question at issue. This assessment considers four dimensions: (a) alignment of the method's characteristic strengths with the argument's epistemic goals — does the method excel at delivering the kind of conclusion the argument needs?; (b) relevance of the method's characteristic limitations — are the things the method cannot do things the argument does not need?; (c) resistance from the subject matter — does the question have features that make it especially amenable or resistant to the chosen method?; (d) appropriateness of the deliverable — does the method produce conclusions of the right kind, generality, and domain to address the question as framed? The fit assessment is comparative as well as absolute: it considers not just whether the method works for the task, but whether another readily available method would work better.

**3. Methodological Consistency Check** — A systematic examination of whether the argument adheres to its own methodological commitments throughout its entire arc, or whether it shifts methods at critical junctures without acknowledgment or justification. Consistency violations are catalogued by type: method-switching (changing methods at a point where the switch enables an otherwise unsupported move), scope inflation (making claims whose scope exceeds what the method can support), internal norm violation (failing to follow the norms internal to the method the argument claims to employ), and asymmetric application (applying stricter standards to opposing views than to the favored view). For each concern, the report provides specific textual evidence and explains the philosophical significance of the inconsistency.

**4. Limitation Analysis** — An inventory of what the detected method structurally cannot deliver, even when executed with maximal competence and good faith. Limitations are categorized as structural (constitutive of the method — no better execution can overcome them), scopal (limiting the domain within which results are valid), idealizational (arising from the gap between simplified models and the phenomena they model), and implementational (contingent failures of this particular execution rather than the method itself). For each limitation, the analysis assesses the severity for the argument: fatal (the argument's central conclusion requires something the method structurally cannot provide — the argument is unsound on its own methodological terms), restrictive (the argument's conclusions must be qualified, narrowed, or hedged because the method cannot fully support them as stated), or benign (the limitation is irrelevant to this argument's specific aims and claims — the argument does not need what the method cannot provide). The limitation analysis is the most important component of the report for evaluating whether the argument's conclusions are genuinely warranted, because methodological limitations are often the gap through which an otherwise compelling argument's force leaks away.

**5. Alternative Method Comparison** — For each major methodological alternative to the detected method, a counterfactual assessment: if this same philosophical question were addressed using that alternative method, would the conclusions differ in philosophically significant ways? For each alternative, the report identifies points of potential divergence (which steps in the argument are method-sensitive — which premises, inferences, or evidential appeals would change under the alternative method), estimates the direction and magnitude of divergence (convergence on the same conclusion, partial convergence on a qualified version, divergence to a different conclusion, or inapplicability because the alternative method cannot address this question at all), and assesses whether the methodological choice is underdetermined by the argument's aims — could a different method have served equally well or better? Where possible, the comparison is grounded in actual instances of the alternative method being applied to similar questions in the philosophical literature, not merely in abstract speculation about what the method "would" deliver. A robust argument should ideally be able to explain why its chosen method is more appropriate for its aims than the salient alternatives, or at minimum acknowledge the methodological choice as one that shapes the conclusions.

## Methodological Foundations

This skill's analytical framework draws on the following canonical sources in philosophical methodology. Each source articulates, defends, or exemplifies a distinctive methodological principle that informs the skill's approach to detecting and evaluating methods.

**Carnap, R.** (1950). *Logical Foundations of Probability*. University of Chicago Press. Carnap develops the method of explication: replacing vague, pre-theoretic concepts (explicanda) with precise, theoretically fruitful substitutes (explicata) that are similar enough to the original to serve its function but exact enough to support rigorous reasoning. His contribution to methodological analysis is the principle that methodological choice is fundamentally pragmatic rather than truth-apt — we do not ask whether a method is "correct" in some correspondence sense, but whether it is useful for our theoretical purposes. The four criteria for a good explication — similarity to the explicandum, exactness, fruitfulness, and simplicity — provide a general framework for evaluating methodological choices that can be extended beyond formal contexts to any case where a philosopher replaces a pre-theoretic notion with a more precise theoretical construct.

**Rawls, J.** (1971). *A Theory of Justice*. Harvard University Press (especially sections 4 and 9 on the justification of moral principles and the method of reflective equilibrium). Rawls articulates reflective equilibrium as a method for moral and political philosophy: we work back and forth between general principles and particular considered judgments about cases, revising each in light of the other, until we achieve a stable, coherent equilibrium in which our principles account for our judgments and our judgments are supported by our principles. His methodological legacy includes three insights: that justification is holistic rather than foundational — no class of judgments is immune to revision; that intuitions and theories are mutually constraining rather than hierarchically ordered — we may revise either; and that the aim of philosophical method is not deduction from self-evident axioms but the achievement of coherence among our commitments after critical scrutiny has filtered out bias, distortion, and error from our considered judgments.

**Chalmers, D. J.** (2012). *Constructing the World*. Oxford University Press (especially chapters 1-2 on scrutability and the extended methodological discussion of the role of concepts and a priori reasoning in philosophy). Chalmers provides the most rigorous contemporary defense of the method of cases — using thought experiments about possible scenarios to test conceptual analyses and modal claims — while engaging seriously with the experimental philosophy challenge. He distinguishes multiple grades of conceptual involvement in philosophical method, from full-blooded conceptual analysis appealing to a priori entailments from concepts, to more modest appeals to scrutability that require only that truths about a scenario be knowable given ideal reasoning from a sufficiently rich base. His work contributes the principle that methodological disputes about the role of concepts require precise articulation of what exactly concepts are being asked to do, and that defenses of armchair methods must specify the epistemic source of armchair knowledge rather than merely insisting on its possibility.

**Knobe, J. & Nichols, S.** (eds.) (2008). *Experimental Philosophy*. Oxford University Press. This volume collects the foundational papers of the experimental philosophy movement and articulates its core methodological challenge: if philosophical intuitions vary systematically with demographic factors (culture, gender, socioeconomic status), personality traits, and framing effects, then the traditional armchair method of testing philosophical theories against a single philosopher's intuitions is insufficiently calibrated and epistemically unreliable. The methodological principle this contributes is that claims about "what we would say" or "what is intuitive" in response to philosophical cases are, at bottom, empirical claims about human psychology, and as such they require empirical support rather than armchair pronouncement. This principle does not entail that all intuition-based philosophy is illegitimate, but it shifts the burden of proof: defenders of armchair methods must explain why their particular intuitions are epistemically privileged.

**Nietzsche, F.** (1887). *On the Genealogy of Morality*. Translated by C. Diethe (1994), Cambridge University Press. Nietzsche's genealogical method — tracing moral concepts (good, bad, guilt, conscience, ascetic ideals) through their historical emergence not to discover their timeless essence but to uncover the contingent power relations, psychological functions, and historical accidents that produced them — is the canonical source for debunking arguments in philosophy. His central methodological principle is that the origin and function of a concept, value, or practice is relevant to its normative authority. If we can show that a moral concept emerged from and continues to serve morally irrelevant or morally dubious functions, we have reason to question its claim on us — a reason that is not captured by deductive refutation (showing that the concept's content is inconsistent) but by a historical demonstration that alters our evaluative relationship to the concept. This challenges the methodological axiom that genetic considerations are irrelevant to normative evaluation and introduces a distinctive form of philosophical argument that operates by transforming self-understanding rather than by logical deduction.

**Husserl, E.** (1913). *Ideas Pertaining to a Pure Phenomenology and to a Phenomenological Philosophy, First Book*. Translated by F. Kersten (1982), Martinus Nijhoff. Husserl articulates the phenomenological reduction or *epoché* — bracketing the natural attitude, suspending the thesis of the existence of the external world, and attending purely to the structures of conscious experience as they are given in immanent intuition — which establishes phenomenology as a distinctive philosophical method with its own standards of evidence, its own domain (transcendental subjectivity), and its own form of rigor. The methodological principle is that first-person experience has a distinctive form of givenness and a distinctive authority that cannot be reduced to or replaced by third-person description without remainder. Phenomenology claims access to dimensions of experience — intentionality, temporality, embodiment as lived rather than as objectively described, intersubjectivity as experienced — that are methodologically invisible to naturalistic approaches treating consciousness as one object among others in the natural world.

**Williamson, T.** (2007). *The Philosophy of Philosophy*. Blackwell. Williamson mounts a sustained challenge to the "linguistic conception" of philosophy — the view that philosophical questions are fundamentally questions about words or concepts, and that philosophical method is a form of linguistic or conceptual analysis. He argues that thought experiments in philosophy are not disguised semantic intuitions about the application of words but exercises of ordinary counterfactual reasoning — the same cognitive capacity we employ in everyday life when we judge that "if I had left five minutes earlier, I would have caught the train." His central methodological principle is that philosophical method is continuous with ordinary cognition and scientific reasoning, not a special philosophical faculty requiring special certification. This continuity thesis, combined with his argument that philosophical knowledge is possible without a distinctive philosophical method, challenges the assumption that methodology must be a foundational discipline that legitimates philosophical practice before it can proceed — philosophy can be methodologically reflective without being methodologically foundationalist.

**Foucault, M.** (1969). *The Archaeology of Knowledge*. Translated by A. M. Sheridan Smith (1972), Pantheon Books. Foucault's archaeological method — analyzing the discursive formations, epistemic rules, and institutional conditions that make certain statements possible, intelligible, and authoritative within a given historical period while excluding others from the space of serious utterance — contributes the principle that what counts as a legitimate philosophical statement, a valid inference, or an acceptable method is itself historically constituted and embedded in relations of power and institutional authority. This introduces a reflexive dimension to methodological analysis: the methods we use to evaluate philosophical arguments are themselves the products of contingent historical formations, and any adequate methodological analysis must be capable of examining its own historical conditions of possibility rather than presenting its methodological categories as timeless and universal.

**Merleau-Ponty, M.** (1945). *Phenomenology of Perception*. Translated by D. A. Landes (2012), Routledge. Merleau-Ponty extends and transforms the phenomenological method beyond Husserl's transcendental framework by grounding phenomenological description in the lived body and the primacy of perception — the idea that perception is not a mental representation of a world external to consciousness but the fundamental mode of our embodied being-in-the-world, prior to and presupposed by reflective thought. His methodological contribution is the demonstration that phenomenology need not be tied to transcendental idealism: the structures of lived, embodied experience can be investigated as dimensions of situated, worldly existence without bracketing the world's existence — the reduction does not withdraw from the world but slackens the intentional threads that bind us to it so that they become visible. This provides a model for evaluating whether an argument's use of a method is faithful to that method's tradition, represents a legitimate development of it, or constitutes an abandonment of it.

## Taxonomy of Philosophical Methods

The skill classifies detected methods using the following taxonomy. These categories are not mutually exclusive — a single argument may employ several methods — and the boundaries between them are contested and philosophically substantive. The taxonomy is a heuristic instrument for identification and analysis, not a claim about the metaphysical structure of philosophical methodology or a ranking of methods by legitimacy.

Each entry in the taxonomy describes: the method's core practice (what it characteristically does), its typical markers (textual and structural features that indicate its presence), its major variants (distinct versions of the method within the same broad category), and its underlying commitment (the philosophical assumption that motivates adopting this method rather than another). Understanding what a method commits its user to — what it assumes about the nature of philosophy, the structure of its subject matter, and the sources of philosophical evidence — is as important as recognizing its presence, because methodological disagreements are often driven by disagreements about these underlying commitments rather than by disagreements about technique.

- **Conceptual Analysis** — Decomposing a concept into its necessary and sufficient conditions for application, or mapping its inferential role, its connections to other concepts, and its behavior across possible scenarios. The method proceeds by considering hypothetical cases — often with unusual, borderline, or counterfactual features — and testing proposed analyses against intuitive judgments about whether the concept applies. A proposed analysis is challenged by counterexample (a case where the analysis delivers the wrong verdict) and defended by refinement (adjusting the analysis to accommodate the counterexample while preserving its explanatory power). Typical markers include: explicit necessary-and-sufficient-conditions claims, appeals to "what we would say" about a case, the construction of Gettier-style counterexamples, and the search for exceptionless definitions. Variants include the Canberra Plan (using folk platitudes to fix reference via Ramsification), two-dimensional semantics (distinguishing primary and secondary intensions to handle necessary a posteriori truths), and conceptual engineering (actively revising concepts rather than merely analyzing them as found). The underlying commitment is that philosophical questions are, at some significant level, questions about our concepts — their structure, content, and application conditions — and that making this structure explicit resolves or dissolves philosophical problems.

- **Reflective Equilibrium** — Seeking coherence between general principles and particular judgments about cases by working back and forth, adjusting each in light of the other, until a stable, coherent equilibrium is achieved across one's entire set of relevant commitments. Unlike conceptual analysis, which typically treats particular case judgments as fixed data against which analyses are tested, reflective equilibrium allows that the judgments themselves may be revised in light of broader theoretical considerations. The method admits of narrow and wide variants: narrow reflective equilibrium seeks coherence among principles and judgments within a single domain; wide reflective equilibrium brings in background theories from other domains (psychology, sociology, metaphysics) that may bear on the reliability or probative value of particular judgments. Typical markers include: iterative revision of principles after encountering counterexamples, explicit discussion of which judgments to revise and which to retain, appeals to "considered judgments" that have been filtered for bias and emotional distortion, the language of coherence, fit, and equilibrium, and the acknowledgment that multiple equilibria may be possible. The underlying commitment is that justification is holistic and that no class of judgments is foundational or immune to revision — even our firmest considered judgments may be revised if the theoretical cost of retaining them is too high.

- **Experimental Philosophy** — Using systematic empirical methods — surveys, reaction-time measurements, behavioral experiments, corpus analysis, neuroimaging, cross-cultural studies — to investigate the psychological processes underlying philosophical judgments and to test claims about folk concepts, intuitions, and reasoning patterns.

The method may serve a positive function (providing evidence about the content and structure of folk concepts, testing psychological hypotheses relevant to philosophical debates, discovering factors that influence philosophical judgment) or a negative function (challenging armchair methods by demonstrating that philosophical intuitions are unstable across demographics, sensitive to normatively irrelevant factors like order effects or framing, or subject to cognitive biases that undermine their evidential value).

Typical markers include: reported experimental data with sample sizes and statistical analyses (t-tests, ANOVAs, effect sizes, confidence intervals), discussion of participant demographics and between-group comparisons, detailed descriptions of experimental vignettes and protocols, citations of the experimental philosophy literature, and explicit argumentation about the philosophical upshot of empirical findings about intuitions.

The underlying commitment is that at least some claims traditionally treated as a priori accessible from the armchair are in fact empirical claims about human cognition and must be held to empirical standards of evidence — a commitment that may be narrow (applying only to claims explicitly about folk concepts) or broad (applying to all claims that depend on intuitive judgments).

- **Genealogical Method** — Tracing the historical emergence and transformation of concepts, values, institutions, or practices through their contingent development to uncover the power relations, psychological functions, social dynamics, and historical accidents that produced them and that sustain them. Unlike straightforward intellectual history or conceptual history, genealogy is philosophically motivated and evaluatively engaged: it aims to alter our relationship to its objects — typically by showing that what appears necessary, natural, universal, or self-evident is in fact contingent, constructed, historically specific, and in the service of particular interests. The method does not refute concepts by logical argument; it subverts them by revealing their low or ignoble origins and their continuing implication in structures of domination or self-deception. Typical markers include: extended historical narrative, attention to etymologies and semantic shifts, examination of institutional, economic, and material conditions alongside ideas, debunking arguments that move from historical origins to normative deflation, and a critical or suspicious stance toward the self-understanding of the tradition being examined. The underlying commitment is that genetic considerations are normatively significant — that understanding where our concepts and values came from and what functions they serve is relevant to evaluating their authority over us.

- **Phenomenology** — Describing the structures of lived, first-person experience in its own terms, attending faithfully to how phenomena present themselves in conscious experience prior to and independently of theoretical interpretation, scientific explanation, or commonsense assumption. The method proceeds through careful attentiveness to experiential texture and, in its Husserlian form, the performance of the phenomenological reduction or *epoché* — bracketing or suspending the natural attitude (the unquestioned assumption that experience is of mind-independent objects existing in a world independent of consciousness) to focus on experience precisely as experience, examining its structures of intentionality, temporality, embodiment, and intersubjectivity as they are given. Typical markers include: first-person descriptive language, vocabulary of intentionality (consciousness as always consciousness-of-something), attention to the temporal structure of experience (retention, primal impression, protention), discussion of the lived body (*Leib*) as distinct from the objective body (*Koerper*), appeals to what is "given" or "presented" in experience as evidence, and methodological reflections on the reduction and its proper performance. The underlying commitment is that first-person experience is a sui generis domain with its own structures, its own form of evidence, and its own kind of rigor, not reducible to or replaceable by third-person description or scientific explanation without remainder — there are features of experience that can only be accessed from the first-person perspective.

- **Ordinary Language Philosophy** — Drawing philosophical conclusions from the fine-grained features of natural language use, including distinctions embedded in ordinary vocabulary, patterns of what competent speakers do and do not say in particular circumstances, what sounds linguistically odd or natural, and the conditions under which linguistic expressions are appropriately or inappropriately used.

The method, most prominently associated with J. L. Austin and the later Wittgenstein, holds that many traditional philosophical problems arise from or are sustained by misunderstandings of how language ordinarily functions — philosophers are misled by surface grammatical similarities into assimilating different language games, or they detach words from the contexts of use that give them meaning. Close attention to ordinary usage can dissolve rather than solve these problems.

Typical markers include: catalogues of usage patterns, analysis of subtle semantic distinctions marked by word choice ("by mistake" vs. "by accident," "voluntarily" vs. "intentionally"), appeals to what sounds odd or natural for a competent speaker to say in a given circumstance, diagnosis of philosophical claims as violations of linguistic propriety or as using words outside the conditions of their meaningful employment, and a therapeutic rather than constructive or systematic stance toward philosophical problems.

The underlying commitment is that language is not a transparent medium for thought but a complex form of social practice whose fine structure encodes distinctions that philosophical theorizing, in its drive for generality and systematization, tends to overlook or obliterate.

- **Naturalism** — Treating philosophical questions as methodologically continuous with scientific inquiry: philosophical claims should be consistent with, answerable to, or constrained by the best available empirical findings, and philosophical methods should not appeal to special faculties, intuitions, or sources of evidence that are unavailable to or in principle insulated from the sciences. Variants range along a spectrum from methodological naturalism (philosophy should use the methods of the sciences, but may address questions the sciences do not) to metaphysical naturalism (only those entities, properties, and processes recognized by the sciences exist) to liberal or relaxed naturalism (philosophy is autonomous in its questions and some of its methods, but its results must be consistent with and answerable to scientific findings). Typical markers include: appeals to the results of natural sciences (physics, biology, cognitive psychology, neuroscience), the continuity thesis (philosophy and science differ in generality and abstractness, not in fundamental kind), explicit rejection of a priori methods that claim independence from empirical constraint, arguments that philosophical theories must earn their place within a broadly scientific worldview, and a tendency to treat natural science as the paradigm of successful inquiry. The underlying commitment is that there is no "first philosophy" that certifies science from an external, pre-empirical standpoint — philosophy and science are part of a single, continuous, and mutually informing inquiry into the world and our place in it.

- **Formal Methods** — Employing the apparatus of formal logic, mathematics, probability theory, game theory, decision theory, formal semantics, or computational modeling to represent philosophical claims with precision, derive consequences with rigor, and discover structure that may be invisible to or unreliably tracked by informal reasoning. Formalization is not merely presentational but epistemically significant: it forces explicitness about all assumptions (since formal derivation cannot rely on unstated premises), enables the derivation of non-obvious consequences (theorems that follow from axioms but are not obvious on inspection), and can reveal hidden inconsistencies (by making it impossible to hold incompatible claims simultaneously without the formal system generating a contradiction). Typical markers include: logical or mathematical notation, explicit axiomatizations with specified formation and transformation rules, theorems accompanied by proofs, formal models with explicitly specified parameters and state spaces, possible-worlds semantics with accessibility relations, Bayesian updating with specified priors and likelihoods, equilibrium concepts from game theory, and computational simulations or agent-based models. The underlying commitment is that precision, explicitness, and derivational rigor are philosophical virtues — and that the cost of formalization (idealization, restricted domain, technical barrier to entry) is worth paying for the epistemic benefits it delivers.

- **Transcendental Method** — Arguing that certain substantive conditions must obtain because they are necessary presuppositions or conditions of possibility of some actual and (typically) undeniable fact — most commonly the possibility of experience, the possession of self-consciousness, the capacity for intentional action, or the practice of making normative claims. The argument takes a characteristic two-step form: (1) X (some undeniable fact about us or our situation) is possible only if Y (some substantive condition of a metaphysical, epistemological, or normative character) obtains; (2) X is actual (we do have experience, we are self-conscious, we do make normative claims); therefore Y must obtain. Typical markers include: the phrase "conditions of possibility" or its variants, arguments that proceed from something taken as given and work regressively to its necessary preconditions rather than building constructively from foundational premises, the claim to establish substantive conclusions (about the structure of the world, the nature of subjects, or the content of normative requirements) without relying on empirical evidence, and a concern with the limits and structure of possible experience, thought, or action. The underlying commitment is that reflection on the necessary conditions of our own cognitive and practical capacities can yield substantive metaphysical or normative knowledge — though the scope of such knowledge is limited to what is necessary for beings like us, leaving open that beings with different cognitive architectures might face different necessary conditions.

- **Dialectical Method** — Advancing philosophical understanding through the systematic identification, development, and resolution of contradictions — whether in the Hegelian sense of determinate negation (each philosophical position generates its own internal contradiction through the attempt to think its object adequately, and this contradiction in turn generates a more adequate position that preserves what was true in the previous position while overcoming its limitation) or the Socratic sense of elenchus (a position is tested by drawing out its implications until contradiction with other commitments the proponent holds is exposed, forcing either abandonment of the position or revision of the conflicting commitments). Typical markers include: working through successive philosophical positions each of which is shown to be unstable or one-sided, thesis-antithesis-synthesis triadic structures (in Hegelian dialectic), the claim that contradiction is productive and revelatory rather than merely destructive, and attention to the movement or development of concepts over time rather than their static analysis at a single moment. The underlying commitment is that philosophical truth is not statable in a single fixed proposition or system of propositions but emerges through, and in some sense consists in, the process of working through and overcoming inadequate positions — the truth is the whole, and the whole is the process of its own becoming.

- **Pragmatic Method** — Clarifying concepts and evaluating philosophical claims by tracing their conceivable practical consequences: what difference they would make, or could make, to action, inquiry, and experience. Most closely associated with C. S. Peirce, William James, and John Dewey, the pragmatic method is captured by Peirce's pragmatic maxim: "Consider what effects, that might conceivably have practical bearings, we conceive the object of our conception to have. Then, our conception of these effects is the whole of our conception of the object." The method treats philosophical disputes that make no conceivable practical difference — where both sides predict the same experiential outcomes, recommend the same actions, and support the same inquiries — as merely verbal or empty. Typical markers include: explicit invocation of the pragmatic maxim, appeals to what practical difference a distinction or claim makes, the diagnosis of metaphysical disputes as practically empty, attention to the continuities between philosophical inquiry and ordinary problem-solving, and an instrumentalist or functionalist orientation toward concepts (concepts are tools for navigating the world, not mirrors of reality). The underlying commitment is that philosophy is continuous with practical life and that the meaning and significance of philosophical claims are ultimately a function of their role in guiding action and inquiry.

## Steps

The following steps constitute a systematic procedure for methodological analysis. They are designed to be followed sequentially — each step's output informs the next — but in practice the analysis will involve iterative refinement: insights from later steps may prompt revisiting earlier classifications or inferences. The steps are grounded in the methodological foundations and taxonomy above, and each step's execution should be informed by the principles articulated in those sections. While the procedure is structured, it is not algorithmic: several steps require substantive philosophical judgment (evaluating fit, constructing counterfactual comparisons, mapping background assumptions), and the quality of the analysis depends on the analyst's philosophical sophistication as well as their fidelity to the procedure.

**1. Detect Explicit Methodological Signals**

Begin by scanning the text systematically for overt methodological declarations, meta-philosophical commentary, and signals about how the author understands their own philosophical practice. Authors frequently disclose their methodological commitments in introductory or setup sections (often headed "Methodology," "Methodological Preliminaries," "Approach," "Framework," "A Note on Method"), in the opening paragraphs where they characterize the kind of argument they are making, in transitional passages that explain why a certain move is legitimate, and in footnotes or asides responding to anticipated methodological objections.

Key phrases to search for include: "I employ the method of...," "this paper proceeds by conceptual analysis," "I adopt a naturalistic approach," "the argument takes the form of a transcendental deduction," "we model this situation formally as...," "following the phenomenological method...," "this is a genealogy of...," and any explicit naming of the method being used.

Additionally, attend to less direct but equally revealing signals. Authors may identify their philosophical tradition or influences in ways that carry methodological implications: "in the tradition of ordinary language philosophy," "following Rawls," "a Husserlian approach," "in the spirit of experimental philosophy." They may characterize what kind of claim they are making: "this is an empirical claim about folk intuitions," "this is a conceptual truth," "this is a phenomenological description." They may describe what would count as evidence for or against their view, which reveals methodological commitments even when the method itself is unnamed. They may position themselves against alternative methods: "unlike armchair conceptual analysis," "contra purely formal approaches," "against the naturalistic reduction of consciousness."

Record every explicit methodological claim, the textual location of each signal (section, paragraph, or line), and any apparent tensions between different explicit claims. An author might, for instance, claim to be doing conceptual analysis while also making empirical claims that require empirical support, or claim fidelity to a phenomenological method while making claims about neural correlates of consciousness that belong to naturalistic inquiry. These tensions are data for later steps — do not resolve them prematurely, but do not miss them either.

Pay attention to genre signals that carry implicit methodological commitments. A paper structured with formal definitions, lemmas, theorems, and proofs signals formal methods. A paper organized around the reporting and analysis of experimental results with participant demographics, statistical tests, and effect sizes signals experimental philosophy. A paper that moves narratively through historical periods tracing the transformation of a concept or practice signals genealogy. A paper written primarily in first-person descriptive prose attending to the fine texture of some experience signals phenomenology. Genre is not definitive — authors may subvert or hybridize genre expectations — but it provides a starting point for methodological interpretation and a check on explicit declarations: an author who claims to be doing formal work but whose paper contains no formal notation and no proofs is at minimum inviting the question of what "formal" means in this context.

**2. Identify Implicit Methodological Commitments from Argument Structure**

When explicit methodological signals are absent, fragmentary, or inconsistent with the argument's actual operation, infer methodological commitments from the argument's structural features. This step examines what the argument does methodologically, not what the author says about what it does. The gap between declared and practiced method is itself an important finding — it may indicate methodological confusion, a sophisticated eclecticism the author has not fully articulated, or a deliberate strategy of methodological camouflage.

The primary structural indicators are:

- **The evidential basis of the argument:** What does the argument treat as probative support for its claims? If the argument treats considered reactions to hypothetical cases (what one would say, what seems right to say) as decisive evidence, it is likely employing conceptual analysis or reflective equilibrium. If it treats systematically collected experimental data as decisive, it is employing experimental philosophy or some form of naturalism. If it treats historical evidence about the contingent emergence of a concept as probative for its normative status, it is employing genealogy. If it treats careful attention to the texture of first-person experience as a source of evidence, it is employing phenomenology. If it treats what competent speakers do and do not say as evidence, it is employing ordinary language philosophy.

- **The inferential patterns of the argument:** How does the argument move from its starting points to its conclusions? Deductive derivation from explicit definitions or principles characterizes conceptual analysis and formal methods. Iterative revision of principles in light of counterexamples characterizes reflective equilibrium. Statistical inference from data to population-level claims characterizes experimental philosophy. Inference to the best explanation from a historical narrative characterizes genealogy. The regressive inference from an actual fact to its necessary conditions of possibility characterizes transcendental method. The description of experiential structures as they present themselves, without inference to transcendent causes, characterizes phenomenology.

- **The standards of refutation the argument implicitly accepts:** What would the argument count as showing it wrong? If it could be refuted by a well-chosen counterexample case, it is using conceptual analysis or reflective equilibrium. If it could be refuted by a conflicting experimental result with better methodology or larger samples, it is using experimental philosophy. If it could be refuted by an alternative historical narrative that better accounts for the available evidence, it is using genealogy. If it could be refuted by an alternative phenomenological description that other practitioners of the reduction report as more faithful to the experience, it is using phenomenology.

- **How the argument treats potential objections:** The way an argument handles actual or anticipated objections reveals methodological commitments. Objections framed as counterexample cases, alternative definitions, or competing intuitive judgments reveal a methodology oriented around concepts and intuitions. Objections framed as methodological critiques of experimental design, confounds, or alternative empirical interpretations reveal an experimental or naturalistic methodology. Objections framed as competing historical narratives or genealogies reveal a genealogical method. Objections framed as "you are not attending carefully enough to the experience" or "your description is theory-laden" reveal a phenomenological method.

- **The relationship to the empirical:** How does the argument relate to empirical findings? Does it treat empirical results as irrelevant to its philosophical claims (suggesting a priori method), as the sole admissible evidence (suggesting radical naturalism), or as one source of evidence to be weighed alongside conceptual and intuitive considerations (suggesting moderate naturalism or wide reflective equilibrium)? Does it make claims with empirical content — for instance, about what most people would say, about the psychological processes underlying philosophical judgment, about the functions concepts serve in social life — without providing empirical evidence for those claims?

For each inferred implicit commitment, document the specific structural feature that grounds the inference. Distinguish between strong inferences (where the argument's structure clearly presupposes or requires the method — for example, an argument whose central move is a transcendental deduction) and weak inferences (where the structure is merely consistent with the method but does not require it). Acknowledge when multiple methodological interpretations of the same structural feature are possible, and explain why the chosen interpretation is preferred.

**3. Classify Each Method Using the Standard Taxonomy**

For each detected method — explicit and implicit, primary and subsidiary — map it onto the standard taxonomy provided above. The classification should be precise and evidence-based. For each method classified, provide:

- **The specific taxonomic fit:** Which category in the taxonomy does the method fall under, and why? Cite the features of the taxonomic category (as described) that are present in the argument's actual practice, with textual or structural evidence for each feature. Do not simply assert that the argument "uses conceptual analysis" — specify what about the argument makes it conceptual analysis (it proposes necessary and sufficient conditions, it tests them against hypothetical cases, it revises in response to counterexamples, it aims at exceptionless definitions, etc.).

- **Absent characteristic features:** Which features of the taxonomic category are notably absent despite being characteristic or even definitional of the method? A conceptual analysis that never considers counterexample cases or never provides explicit necessary and sufficient conditions is atypical. A phenomenological description that never mentions the reduction or *epoché* is an unusual instance of phenomenology. Noting absences helps refine the classification and may indicate that the method is being used in a modified, partial, or heterodox form.

- **The method's variant or subtype:** Where relevant, identify the specific variant of the method being used. Is the conceptual analysis of the Canberra Plan variety, the two-dimensional semantics variety, or the classical necessary-and-sufficient-conditions variety? Is the naturalism metaphysical, methodological, or liberal? Is the phenomenology transcendental (Husserlian), existential (Heideggerian), or embodied (Merleau-Pontian)? The precision is valuable because different variants of the same method have different strengths, limitations, and background assumptions.

When multiple methods are detected, characterize their relationships. Are they complementary — each addressing a different aspect of a complex question, one method providing the framework within which another operates? Are they competing — offering incompatible approaches to the same question, such that the argument's attempt to use both generates tension? Are they nested — one method operating within the framework or under the authority of another, as when reflective equilibrium incorporates empirical psychological findings (wide reflective equilibrium) or phenomenology incorporates hermeneutic interpretation (hermeneutic phenomenology)? Or are they merely juxtaposed — present in the same text without any integration, potentially indicating that the argument has not fully thought through its methodological commitments?

When a method resists classification — exhibiting features of multiple taxonomic categories without fitting cleanly into any one — resist the temptation to force it into the nearest available category. Instead, articulate precisely why it resists classification: which features pull in which directions, whether the resistance reflects a genuinely novel method, an eclectic practice that combines elements from multiple traditions, or a methodological confusion. The taxonomy is an analytical instrument, not a Procrustean bed — its value lies in sharpening our understanding of the argument's methodology, not in successfully assigning a label.

**4. Check Methodological Consistency**

Examine whether the argument maintains consistent methodological commitments throughout its entire trajectory — from the framing of the question, through the development of the argument's central moves, to the statement of its conclusions. Methodological consistency is not an aesthetic preference for tidiness; inconsistency can be structurally fatal to an argument if the method switch occurs at the precise point where it enables a crucial step that the argument's primary or professed method cannot justify.

Consistency analysis examines several distinct forms of potential methodological failure:

- **Method-switching:** Does the argument change methods at a critical juncture without acknowledgment or justification? The argument might, for instance, employ conceptual analysis to establish what a concept entails or what follows from its application conditions, but then transition without comment to drawing empirical conclusions about what the world actually contains. This is the classic "analysis to existence" gap: conceptual analysis can specify what a concept's application conditions are, but establishing that anything in the world satisfies those conditions requires empirical investigation or additional premises the method does not provide. An argument that engages in extensive empirical discussion of experimental results but then, when the data is ambiguous or inconvenient, retreats to armchair intuitive judgments about what "must be the case," is alternating between naturalistic and a priori standards in a way that undermines both.

- **Scope inflation:** Does the argument make claims whose scope or modal strength exceeds what its method can support? A phenomenological description of the author's own experiential structures may be offered as a description of universal structures of consciousness for all possible subjects, without any argument for the universality claim beyond the author's conviction that the description captures the experience adequately. A genealogical narrative about the contingent historical origins of a particular concept (say, the modern Western concept of punishment) may be offered as grounds for a universal normative conclusion about punishment as such, across all cultures and times, without addressing the possibility that different genealogies would support different normative conclusions. A formal model of a highly simplified and idealized scenario (perfectly rational agents with full information, or a two-player game with a restricted strategy space) may be offered as directly applicable to the complex, information-limited, multi-agent real-world case without any argument for why the idealization is benign rather than distorting.

- **Internal norm violation:** Does the argument violate the norms or standards internal to the method it claims to employ? A conceptual analysis that appeals exclusively to the analyst's own intuitions about cases, without considering alternative interpretations of those cases or the possibility that the intuitions are theory-laden, may violate the norm that conceptual analysis should be responsive to shared linguistic competence or considered judgments, not merely the analyst's idiosyncratic reactions. A reflective equilibrium that stops at the first point of coherence between principles and considered judgments, without exploring whether alternative equilibria are available or whether the equilibrium is stable under the addition of further considered judgments, may violate the norm that reflective equilibrium requires systematic rather than opportunistic coherence-seeking. A naturalistic argument that appeals to scientific results selectively — citing studies that support the favored view while ignoring a substantial body of studies that complicate or contradict it — violates the norm that naturalism requires honest and comprehensive engagement with the relevant empirical literature.

- **Asymmetric application of standards:** Does the argument apply stricter evidential or inferential standards to opposing views than to its own view? A paper that demands rigorous empirical evidence for claims made by experimental philosophers while making empirical claims about folk intuitions from the armchair with no empirical support is applying standards asymmetrically. A paper that criticizes opposing views for relying on "mere intuitions" while treating its own intuitive judgments as probative without argument for their special epistemic status is engaging in methodological double standards. This asymmetry may be motivated by a genuine methodological principle — the author may hold that some intuitions are epistemically special (e.g., rational intuitions about logical or mathematical truths) while others are not (e.g., culturally variable intuitions about moral cases). The principle must be stated and defended, not merely assumed or applied opportunistically when it favors the author's preferred conclusions.

For each consistency concern identified, provide specific textual evidence of the inconsistency — quotations or precise descriptions of argumentative moves — and explain why the inconsistency matters for the argument's soundness. The significance of a consistency failure depends on its location in the argument's structure: a method switch that enables a premise the argument's primary method cannot justify is more damaging than a switch that merely supplements an independently supported claim; an asymmetric application of standards that protects the argument's central thesis is more damaging than one that appears in a peripheral aside. Where an apparent inconsistency might be resolvable through charitable interpretation, acknowledge the possibility and specify what interpretive work would be required to resolve it (what the author would need to have meant, what unstated premises would need to be supplied, what additional argumentative moves would need to be made). Do not assume that every apparent inconsistency is a genuine failure — methodological analysis is not a search for "gotcha" moments — but do not assume every inconsistency is resolvable either. The analysis should be fair but not credulous, critical but not hostile.

**5. Evaluate Method-Argument Fit**

Assess whether the chosen method or combination of methods is appropriate for the argument's stated aims, its implicit goals (as inferred from the argument's structure and argumentative trajectory), and the nature of the philosophical question it addresses. This is not a general evaluation of the method's philosophical merits — it is a contextual, task-specific evaluation of whether this particular method is well-suited to this particular philosophical task.

Fit assessment proceeds along four dimensions:

- **Alignment of strengths and goals:** Each method has distinctive strengths — the kinds of conclusions it is especially good at establishing, the kinds of evidence it handles well, the kinds of objections it can accommodate. Conceptual analysis excels at clarifying the inferential structure and application conditions of our concepts, revealing hidden commitments and exposing inconsistencies in our conceptual scheme. Reflective equilibrium excels at achieving coherence and systematicity among our commitments when we lack foundational premises. Experimental philosophy excels at establishing the distribution, stability, and determinants of folk intuitions across populations. Genealogy excels at revealing the contingency and constructedness of what presents itself as natural or necessary. Phenomenology excels at articulating the fine structure of lived experience and recovering dimensions of experience invisible to third-person methods. Formal methods excel at deriving precise, often non-obvious consequences from explicitly stated assumptions and detecting hidden inconsistencies. Assess whether the argument's goals require the distinctive strengths of the method chosen, or whether the method is being used for a purpose it is not distinctively good at — a formal model deployed to capture the richness of lived experience, or a phenomenological description deployed to settle a question about the neural correlates of consciousness.

- **Relevance of known limitations:** Each method has known, characteristic limitations — the kinds of conclusions it cannot establish, the kinds of questions it cannot answer. These limitations are not defects; they are features of the method's design, consequences of its focus. The question for fit assessment is whether the argument's goals require resources that fall within the method's limitations. If the argument aims to establish a normative conclusion and employs a method that can only deliver descriptive results (experimental philosophy without normative premises), the limitations are relevant and the fit is poor. If the argument's aims are modest and fall well within the method's capabilities, the fit is good. The key is to match the specific limitations of the specific method to the specific requirements of the specific argument — not to offer generic observations about methods' limitations in general.

- **Resistance from the subject matter:** Some philosophical questions have features that make them especially amenable or especially resistant to particular methods. Questions about the nature of consciousness may resist purely conceptual or linguistic approaches — not because those approaches are generally flawed, but because consciousness has an experiential, qualitative dimension that seems to resist capture by conceptual analysis or formal modeling. Questions about social ontology (the nature of social groups, institutions, and norms) may resist individualistic methods because social phenomena are constitutively relational — their existence depends on collective attitudes and practices that are not reducible to the attitudes and practices of individuals in isolation. Questions about fundamental metaphysics may resist naturalistic methods because the naturalistic framework itself presupposes answers to some of the very metaphysical questions at issue. The analysis should consider whether the question has features that push back against the method — not in the sense that the method cannot address the question (it may be able to), but in the sense that the method may systematically distort or flatten the phenomena it is trying to examine.

- **Right kind of conclusion:** Does the method deliver conclusions of the appropriate kind, at the appropriate level of generality, and in the appropriate domain, given what the argument purports to establish? A formal model of an idealized two-agent bargaining scenario may deliver precise conclusions about rational choice within that model — but if the argument's aim is to tell us something about justice in actual, large-scale human societies with massively complex institutional structures, the conclusion is of the wrong kind and generality. A genealogical demonstration that guilt emerged historically from the internalization of cruelty may deliver a debunking of guilt's claim to moral authority — but if the argument's aim is to provide a positive normative framework for moral psychology, the conclusion is of the wrong kind (debunking rather than constructive). A transcendental argument may establish necessary conditions of the possibility of experience — but if the argument's aim is to establish the actual structure of the empirical world, the conclusion is at the wrong level: necessary conditions constrain what is possible but do not determine what is actual.

The fit assessment should culminate in a clear overall judgment: good fit (the method is well-suited to the task and its limitations are largely irrelevant to this argument's aims), adequate fit (the method works but with some strain — the argument's aims push against the method's limitations in ways that require qualification of the conclusions), or poor fit (the method cannot deliver what the argument needs, and the argument's success would require methodological resources it does not have).

**6. Assess Limitations and Scope Restrictions**

For each detected method, catalogue what it structurally cannot deliver — the conclusions it cannot establish, the questions it cannot answer, the objections it cannot accommodate — even when executed with maximal competence, good faith, and intellectual sophistication. This is the most important component of the report for evaluating whether the argument's conclusions are warranted, because it identifies the gap between what the method can deliver and what the argument claims.

Limitations fall into four categories, which should be distinguished in the analysis:

- **Structural limitations** are constitutive of the method — they cannot be overcome by better execution, only by changing or supplementing the method. Conceptual analysis cannot establish that anything in the world satisfies the application conditions it specifies — it can tell you what knowledge is (if the analysis is correct), but not that anyone has it. This limitation is structural because conceptual analysis works with concepts and their entailments, and concepts do not guarantee instantiation. Phenomenology cannot adjudicate between competing phenomenological descriptions when different practitioners of the reduction genuinely report different experiential structures — there is no third-person tribunal to which phenomenological disputes can be appealed, because the phenomenological evidence is essentially first-personal. Experimental philosophy cannot establish normative conclusions from purely descriptive premises — this is the structural limit imposed by the is-ought gap, which applies to experimental methods as much as to any other descriptive method. Formal methods cannot establish that their axioms or modeling assumptions are true of the world — they can derive consequences from assumptions, but the truth of the assumptions is an extra-formal matter.

- **Scope limitations** concern the domain within which the method's results are valid — the range of cases, subjects, or contexts to which the conclusions can legitimately be extended. Transcendental arguments establish what is necessary for beings with our specific cognitive architecture, leaving open the possibility that beings with different cognitive architectures — different forms of intuition, different categories, different forms of self-consciousness — would face different necessary conditions. Conceptual analysis of English words like "knowledge," "freedom," or "person" establishes facts about the English-language concepts associated with those words, leaving open whether speakers of other languages possess the same concepts or draw the same conceptual distinctions. Genealogies of particular historical trajectories — the Western concept of the self, the Christian-moral concept of conscience — establish facts about those trajectories, leaving open whether other trajectories were historically possible and whether different trajectories would support different normative assessments of the resulting concepts. Phenomenological descriptions by particular phenomenologists (Husserl, Merleau-Ponty, Levinas) in particular historical and cultural contexts may describe structures of experience specific to those contexts rather than universally human.

- **Idealization limitations** concern the gap between the simplified, abstracted models or scenarios the method works with and the complex, messy phenomena the argument aims to illuminate. Every method involves some degree of idealization — conceptual analysis works with simplified hypothetical cases rather than the full complexity of real-world situations; formal models abstract away from most features of real agents and real interactions; thought experiments bracket real-world complications to isolate a single variable or relationship. The methodological question is whether the idealization is benign (the features abstracted away are irrelevant to the conclusion, or their reintroduction would not change the result) or malign (the conclusion depends on features that are artifacts of the idealization and would not survive in more realistic conditions). The analysis should assess whether the argument acknowledges the idealizations it employs, whether it discusses their potential effects on the conclusion, and whether it provides bridging principles connecting the idealized model or scenario to the target phenomenon.

- **Implementation limitations** concern contingent failures of execution in this particular instance rather than the method's constitutive boundaries. An experimental philosophy study with an underpowered sample, a confounded experimental design, or poorly constructed vignettes that do not adequately operationalize the philosophical concept under investigation has implementation problems, not structural problems — a better-designed study employing the same method might succeed where this one fails. A genealogy that ignores relevant historical evidence, relies on contested interpretations without acknowledging the controversy, or draws sweeping conclusions from a thin evidentiary base has execution problems. A formal argument with a logical error, an omitted premise, or a proof that does not actually establish what it claims has implementation problems. These should be noted in the analysis, but distinguished from the method's inherent limitations — implementation problems indicate that a better-executed version of the same method might succeed, whereas structural limitations indicate that no version of this method, no matter how well executed, can deliver what the argument needs.

For each limitation identified, assess its severity for this particular argument: fatal (the argument's central conclusion requires something the method structurally cannot provide — the argument is unsound on its own methodological terms), restrictive (the argument's conclusions must be qualified, narrowed, or hedged — the conclusion as stated is too strong, but a weaker version could be supported), or benign (the limitation is irrelevant to this specific argument's aims and claims — the argument does not need what the method cannot provide).

**7. Consider Whether Alternative Methods Would Yield Different Conclusions**

For at least two or three major methodological alternatives to the argument's primary detected method, construct a disciplined counterfactual assessment: if this same philosophical question — the question the argument purports to answer — were addressed using that alternative method, would the conclusions differ in philosophically significant ways? This step serves two analytical purposes: it tests whether the argument's conclusions are methodologically robust (holding across different methodological approaches) or method-dependent (an artifact of the particular method chosen), and it assesses whether the methodological choice is underdetermined by the argument's stated aims (such that a different method could have served equally well or better, making the specific methodological choice arbitrary from the perspective of the argument's own goals).

For each alternative method under consideration:

- **Identify points of potential divergence.** Examine the argument step by step and identify which steps are method-sensitive. Not every step in an argument is sensitive to methodological choice. Facts about logical entailment, for example, are method-invariant: if premise P entails conclusion C, that entailment holds regardless of how you established P. But which premises are available, which evidence is treated as probative, which inferential moves are legitimate, which objections are treated as serious, and which conclusions are treated as established — all these are method-sensitive. The conceptual analysis of knowledge as justified true belief plus an anti-Gettier condition depends on the methodological choice to test analyses against intuitive judgments about hypothetical cases. An experimental philosophy study of knowledge attributions that asks participants to rate whether characters in vignettes "know" or "merely believe" depends on the methodological choice to operationalize knowledge attributions as survey responses. A phenomenological description of the experience of knowing — the felt sense of certainty, the absence of doubt, the way the known presents itself as settled — depends on the methodological choice to investigate knowledge from the first-person perspective. Identify which steps in the argument would likely differ under alternative methods and which would likely remain stable.

- **Estimate the direction and magnitude of divergence.** For each alternative method, make a substantiated estimate of whether it would: (a) converge on the same conclusion (different method, same result — the conclusion is robust across methods); (b) partially converge (a qualified or weakened version of the conclusion would hold under the alternative method); (c) diverge (the alternative method would yield a different or incompatible conclusion); or (d) be inapplicable (the alternative method cannot address this question at all — it lacks the conceptual or evidential resources to engage the question). Substantiate these estimates by reference to actual applications of the alternative method to similar questions in the philosophical literature, where available. The estimate should not be a bare assertion but a reasoned judgment grounded in understanding of what the alternative method does and what it requires.

- **Assess methodological underdetermination.** If the argument's stated aims could be served equally well by multiple methods, and the choice among them makes no difference to the conclusions, then the methodological choice is underdetermined — the argument's success does not depend on the correctness of its specific methodological commitments. If different methods would yield different conclusions, the methodological choice is load-bearing — the argument's success depends not only on the quality of its execution but on the correctness of its methodological commitments. In the latter case, the argument should ideally provide some justification for its methodological choice beyond mere preference or disciplinary habit, and the absence of such justification is a methodological weakness.

**8. Map Methodological Dependence on Background Assumptions**

Identify and assess the substantive philosophical assumptions on which the argument's methodological choices depend — the background commitments about the nature of philosophy, the structure of the domain under investigation, the capacities of human cognition, and the goals of philosophical inquiry that make a particular method seem appropriate, natural, or even obligatory. Methods are not neutral, interchangeable instruments that can be adopted and discarded without philosophical consequence; they embed and depend upon substantive views that are themselves philosophically contestable. Exposing these background assumptions is a central function of methodological analysis, particularly when evaluating arguments across methodological traditions that may not share the relevant assumptions.

Key categories of methodological dependence include:

- **Assumptions about the nature of the subject matter.** What does the method presuppose about the domain it investigates? Conceptual analysis presupposes that the philosophical question is (at least in significant part) about our concepts — their structure, content, and application conditions — rather than (or in addition to) about mind-independent reality. If the question were entirely about mind-independent reality, conceptual analysis would be the wrong tool, because our concepts might misrepresent that reality. Naturalism presupposes either that the entities and processes recognized by the sciences exhaust reality (metaphysical naturalism) or that scientific methods are the only or best route to knowledge (methodological naturalism). Phenomenology presupposes that first-person experience is a domain with its own structure and its own form of evidence, irreducible to third-person description — a presupposition denied by eliminativists about consciousness and by those who hold that all genuine evidence is publicly accessible.

- **Assumptions about the nature of philosophical progress.** What does the method assume about what it means to make progress in philosophy? Formal methods often assume that progress consists in increasing precision, explicitness, and derivational rigor — that a formalized argument is better than an informal one, other things equal. Reflective equilibrium assumes that progress consists in increasing coherence and systematicity among our commitments — that a more coherent set of beliefs is epistemically better than a less coherent one. Genealogical methods often assume that progress may consist in liberation from inherited conceptual frameworks — seeing through the claims to naturalness or necessity of concepts that have contingent, interested histories — rather than in accumulating positive doctrine. The pragmatic method assumes that progress consists in resolving problems that arise in practice and improving our ability to navigate the world, not in achieving contemplative insight into timeless truths.

- **Assumptions about cognitive capacities.** What does the method assume about what human beings can know, and how? Conceptual analysis and reflective equilibrium assume that philosophers have sufficiently reliable access to their own concepts and considered judgments — that introspection or rational reflection on one's conceptual competence is a source of evidence, even if fallible. This assumption has been challenged by experimental philosophy's demonstrations of intuition instability and sensitivity to irrelevant factors. Phenomenology assumes the capacity for disciplined, methodologically controlled attention to the structures of experience — that one can, through training and the performance of the reduction, become a more reliable reporter of experiential structures. This assumption has been challenged by those who doubt that introspection can be rendered sufficiently objective or disciplined to count as a genuine method. Formal methods assume that important philosophical truths can be captured in formal systems — that the loss of nuance, context, and particularity involved in formalization is worth paying for the gain in precision and rigor.

- **Assumptions about the relationship between philosophy and other disciplines.** Does the method assume that philosophy is autonomous from the sciences, continuous with them, or in some other relationship? The a priori methods (conceptual analysis, transcendental method, much of phenomenology and ordinary language philosophy) assume that philosophy has a distinctive domain and distinctive methods that are not answerable to empirical findings — though they may disagree about what that domain is (concepts, conditions of possibility, lived experience, language use). Naturalism assumes that philosophy is continuous with the sciences, that its results must be consistent with and answerable to scientific findings, and that there is no "first philosophy" that certifies science from an external, pre-empirical standpoint. Genealogy assumes a complex relationship to empirical historical scholarship — it depends on historical evidence but uses it for philosophical purposes that go beyond what historians would claim.

For each identified dependence, assess the argument's handling of the assumption: is it explicitly argued for within the text (the assumption is defended, not merely presupposed)? Is it assumed without argument but noted (the author acknowledges the assumption and either flags it as a premise the reader must accept or defers its defense to other work)? Or is it entirely unexamined (the assumption operates in the background without the author appearing to recognize that it is an assumption)? Assumptions that are widely shared within a particular tradition may not need defense when addressing others within that tradition — but methodological analysis, which may be used across traditions, should identify them so that readers from other traditions can see where their disagreement with the argument begins, and so that the argument's dependence on tradition-specific assumptions is explicit.

After completing these eight steps, the analysis should be synthesized into the structured report format described in the Output section. The synthesis is not a mechanical aggregation of step outputs — it requires judgment about which findings are most significant, how the various analytical dimensions interact (limitations may explain fit problems; background assumptions may explain consistency failures), and what the overall methodological assessment of the argument amounts to. The analyst should ask: if a reader takes away only one or two insights from this methodology report, what should they be? These central insights should be foregrounded in the report, with supporting detail organized around them rather than presented as a flat list of observations.

## Quality Criteria

A high-quality philosophical methodology analysis meets the following standards:

- **Comprehensiveness:** The analysis detects all major methodological commitments operative in the text, not only those the author explicitly acknowledges or announces in the introduction. It examines what the argument actually does methodologically — its evidential practices, inferential patterns, standards of refutation, treatment of objections — rather than restricting itself to what the argument says about its methodology. It identifies subsidiary methods operating alongside the primary method, inherited methods from the argument's intellectual tradition, and methods operative in how the argument handles objections and alternative views. An analysis that identifies only the method named in the introduction has done only the most preliminary and least informative work.

- **Evidence-Grounded:** Every methodological attribution, every classification, and every consistency concern is supported by specific evidence from the text. Evidence may be direct (quotations in which the author explicitly states methodological commitments), structural (descriptions of argumentative features that imply methodological commitments), or comparative (contrasts between what the argument does at different points). Inferences from absence — claims that the argument should have done something but did not, such as providing empirical evidence for empirical claims — are explicitly flagged as such, with the significance of the absence explained. The standard is that a reader who disagrees with the analysis should be able to identify exactly what textual feature the analysis is responding to and evaluate the analysis's interpretation of it.

- **Taxonomic Precision:** Classifications use the standard taxonomy with appropriate specificity. When an argument clearly exemplifies a method in its canonical form, the classification is crisp and confident. When an argument is a borderline case, uses a modified or hybrid variant, or combines features of multiple categories, the classification acknowledges the complexity rather than forcing it into the nearest category. The reasoning that supports the classification — why this category rather than that one, why this variant rather than that one — is transparent and available for critical evaluation.

- **Fairness to the Author:** Methodological critique is conducted with the principle of charity in view. The analysis does not assume that the author is helpless to respond, that apparent inconsistencies are irremediable, or that methodological choices the analyst would not have made are therefore mistaken. It considers, where plausible, whether charitable interpretation might resolve apparent tensions — while also maintaining that charity has limits and that not every methodological problem in a text is an artifact of uncharitable reading. It acknowledges that authors may have methodological sophistication not fully displayed in any single text and that methodological choices may be justified by considerations the text does not explicitly articulate. The appropriate stance is critical but not hostile, charitable but not credulous.

- **Actionable Insight:** The analysis explains why methodological choices matter for the argument's success or failure. It does not merely catalogue methods or describe them in abstract taxonomic terms; it shows how specific methodological features of the argument shape, enable, or constrain the specific conclusions it reaches. A reader who finishes the methodology report should come away understanding not just what method the argument used, but what difference the methodological choice made — how the argument would have been different, and how its conclusions would have been different, had a different method been employed.

- **Comparative Depth:** The consideration of alternative methods is substantive and engaged, not perfunctory. For each alternative method considered, the analysis explains what applying that method to this question would actually involve — what evidence it would treat as relevant, what inferential patterns it would employ, what objections it would face. It does not caricature alternative methods or reduce them to straw-man versions. Where available, it draws on actual instances of the alternative method being applied to similar philosophical questions, grounding the counterfactual comparison in actual philosophical practice rather than abstract speculation about what the method "would" deliver.

- **Reflexive Awareness:** The analysis acknowledges that methodological analysis is itself a methodological enterprise with its own commitments. The taxonomy is not a neutral, God's-eye classification of philosophical methods but a particular way of carving the methodological space, informed by specific views about what distinguishes one method from another. The evaluative criteria — coherence, fit, consistency — are not self-evident standards but reflect meta-philosophical positions about what makes for good philosophical methodology. The analysis should be transparent about these commitments rather than pretending to a neutrality it cannot achieve. In particular, it should acknowledge that its own method — the detection, classification, and evaluation of methodology — draws on conceptual analysis, reflective equilibrium, and other methods, and that these choices could themselves be subjected to methodological analysis.

- **Cross-Tradition Utility:** While the taxonomy and canonical sources are predominantly drawn from analytic and post-analytic traditions, the analysis is usable for arguments from any philosophical tradition. When encountering methods not well-represented in the taxonomy — Buddhist *prasanga* argumentation, Nyaya epistemology, classical Chinese correlative reasoning, Africana philosophical methods, hermeneutics, deconstruction, scholastic disputed questions, or others — the analysis acknowledges the taxonomic limitation and engages the method in its own terms rather than forcing it into categories designed for other traditions. It may note where the taxonomy could be expanded to accommodate the method more adequately.

These criteria are not merely a checklist to be mechanically applied; they represent the virtues of good methodological analysis. The analysis that satisfies all of them will not only correctly identify the argument's methods but will make a genuine contribution to understanding how the argument works and what its methodological choices commit it to. Conversely, an analysis that satisfies some criteria brilliantly while neglecting others — providing comprehensive method detection without evidence-grounded support, or precise classification without actionable insight into why it matters — is incomplete. The criteria should inform every stage of the analysis, from initial method detection through final report construction.

## Relations to Other Skills

**Depends on:**
- **method.argument.argument-reconstruct** — Methodology analysis requires a clear, structured understanding of the argument's components: its premises, conclusions, inferential moves, assumptions, and the relationships among its parts. When the argument to be analyzed is not already reconstructed in premise-conclusion form, run method.argument.argument-reconstruct first to establish the argument's structure. The methodology analysis then examines how these structural elements are generated and justified — whether the premises are delivered by conceptual analysis, reflective equilibrium, empirical investigation, phenomenological description, or some other method — and whether the method consistently supports the argument's moves throughout.

**Feeds into:**
- **method.dialectic.contextualize** — Once methodology analysis has identified the argument's methodological commitments, method.dialectic.contextualize can situate those commitments within their disciplinary, institutional, and intellectual-historical context. Methodological choices are not made in a vacuum: they reflect training, tradition, institutional norms, and intellectual genealogy. A conceptual analysis in the style of Frank Jackson's Canberra Plan has a different disciplinary context than a conceptual analysis in the style of ordinary language philosophy, and the contextualization enriches the understanding of why the argument proceeds as it does.
- **method.argument.charity-check** — Methodological inconsistency is one form of potential uncharitableness that method.argument.charity-check should examine: if an argument appears to switch methods or violate its own methodological norms, charity-check asks whether a more charitable interpretation would resolve the tension. Conversely, method.argument.charity-check may identify cases where the most charitable interpretation requires attributing a method to the author that they do not explicitly claim — the methodology analysis provides the methodological framework for evaluating whether this attribution is warranted.

**Cross-reference with:**
- **method.concept.intuition-pump** — When methodology analysis detects conceptual analysis, reflective equilibrium, or other intuition-based methods, method.concept.intuition-pump provides deeper, more granular analysis of the specific thought experiments, cases, and intuition-eliciting devices the argument deploys. Methodology analysis identifies the meta-choice to rely on intuitions as a class of evidence; method.concept.intuition-pump evaluates whether the particular intuitions being elicited are reliable, whether the cases that elicit them are well-designed, and whether the intuitive responses can bear the argumentative weight placed on them.
- **method.concept.thought-experiment** — Formal, conceptual, and transcendental arguments frequently deploy thought experiments as central argumentative devices. Phil-thought-experiment examines the internal structure, plausibility, and dialectical function of individual thought experiments, while methodology analysis examines the higher-order methodological decision to use thought experiments as a class and the assumptions about philosophical inquiry this decision embeds. The two skills are complementary: methodology analysis answers "why use thought experiments at all?", while method.concept.thought-experiment answers "does this particular thought experiment work?"
- **method.concept.conceptual-analysis** — When the detected method is conceptual analysis specifically, method.concept.conceptual-analysis provides finer-grained evaluation of the particular analytical techniques deployed (decomposition into necessary and sufficient conditions, the method of cases, two-dimensional semantics, Canberra Plan Ramsification, inferential role analysis, etc.). Methodology analysis situates conceptual analysis as one method among others in the broader methodological landscape; method.concept.conceptual-analysis evaluates conceptual analysis on its own terms.

**Meta-level relationship:**
This skill is reflexively applicable to all other philosophical skills in the suite. Every skill embeds methodological commitments about how a particular kind of philosophical reasoning should be conducted. The method.argument.argument-reconstruct skill employs a method of argument analysis with its own assumptions about argument individuation, premise identification, and the norms of charitable reconstruction. The method.dialectic.dialectical-map skill employs a method of mapping philosophical terrain with its own commitments about what counts as a distinct position and what counts as a dialectically significant relationship among positions. The method.argument.charity-check skill employs its own conception of what charity requires and when it should be suspended, reflecting a methodological stance in the theory of interpretation. Methodological analysis is the skill that can examine the methodology of any of its sibling skills — and, at the limit, its own methodology. This reflexive structure is not a defect but a necessary feature: a methodological analysis skill that could not examine its own methodological commitments would be self-undermining, and a methodology skill unaware of its own methodology would lack the reflexive awareness that it recommends for others.

## Boundary Conditions

**Eclectic or Mixed-Method Arguments**

Many significant philosophical works deliberately employ multiple methods — a naturalistic framework combined with conceptual analysis of folk concepts, a phenomenological description that informs and constrains formal modeling of perception, a genealogical narrative that sets up a reflective equilibrium exercise. When faced with a mixed-method argument, the analysis must distinguish between coherent methodological pluralism and methodological incoherence. Coherent pluralism deploys different methods for different sub-tasks within a complex inquiry, with each method appropriate to its sub-task, with bridging principles explaining how the results of different methods relate to each other, and without contradictions among the methods' background assumptions. Incoherent pluralism uses method-switching opportunistically — to evade the limitations of one method or to claim the authority of multiple methods without doing the work any of them require — or combines methods with incompatible background assumptions without acknowledging or resolving the tension. When evaluating mixed-method arguments, the analysis should assess: whether the combination is justified by the complexity of the question (some questions genuinely require multiple methodological approaches), whether the argument provides integration (bridging principles, consistency arguments) rather than mere juxtaposition (different methods applied to different parts without any account of how the results relate), and whether the combination papers over tensions that honest methodological reflection would expose.

**Methodologically Silent Texts**

Some philosophical texts contain no explicit methodological discussion, and their argumentative structure resists easy classification — they may be too loose, aphoristic, literary, or embedded in a tradition that does not articulate methodological norms. In such cases, the analysis should acknowledge the interpretive difficulty frankly rather than forcing a methodological classification onto resistant material. It should note what structural features can be identified, however tentatively, and consider whether the methodological silence itself is likely deliberate and philosophically motivated. Several important philosophical traditions — those influenced by the later Wittgenstein, by Kierkegaard's indirect communication, by certain strands of non-Western thought, or by post-structuralist suspicion of methodological discourse — may regard methodological explicitness as itself a philosophical mistake: a form of theoretical overreach, a misunderstanding of what philosophical activity is, or an inappropriate importation of scientific norms into a domain where they do not belong. In these cases, methodological silence is not a deficit to be corrected but a deliberate stance, and the analysis should engage with the rationale for that stance rather than treating it as a failure to meet an external standard the text never accepted.

**Radical Methodological Innovation**

When an argument introduces a method that appears genuinely novel — not fitting into any established taxonomic category and not well-described as a combination of existing methods — the analysis faces the challenge of evaluating a method for which no established criteria exist. In such cases, the report should: (a) describe the method's distinctive features as precisely as possible — its evidential base (what it treats as evidence), its inferential norms (what counts as a valid move), its standards of success (what it would mean for the method to work well), and its characteristic products (what kind of conclusions it delivers); (b) situate the method relative to the nearest established methods, explaining both the similarities (why one might be tempted to classify it as a variant of an existing method) and the differences (why that classification fails); (c) evaluate it primarily in terms of its own self-professed aims and standards rather than imposing external standards of methodological adequacy; (d) assess whether the innovation is likely to be generative — whether it opens new lines of inquiry or new forms of argument that other philosophers might adopt — or whether it appears idiosyncratic to this particular argument and unlikely to be adopted. The analysis should be open to the possibility that radically novel methods may require expanding or revising the taxonomy rather than being judged deficient by existing categories, while also maintaining appropriate critical distance: not every methodological departure is an innovation, and not every novelty is an improvement.

**Method-as-Conclusion Arguments**

Some philosophical arguments have as their primary conclusion a methodological thesis — that a particular method is the correct, the only, or the best way to conduct philosophical inquiry in some domain. Classic examples include: defenses of phenomenology as the only rigorous method for investigating consciousness, defenses of naturalism as the only intellectually responsible philosophical stance, critiques of conceptual analysis that conclude it should be replaced by experimental methods, or arguments that formal methods are the only route to clarity in metaphysics and that informal methods are hopelessly confused. When method IS the conclusion, methodological analysis must operate at two distinct but related levels. At the object level, it evaluates the argument for the methodological conclusion: does the argument's reasoning support its claim about method? Does the argument presuppose the method it defends (circularity risk)? Is the argument methodologically consistent — does it practice what it preaches during the argument itself? At the meta level, it reflects on its own methodological standpoint: what method is the analysis using to evaluate the argument, and is that method one that the argument's conclusion, if accepted, would delegitimize? If the argument concludes that only naturalistic methods are legitimate and the analysis evaluates it using a priori conceptual analysis of its argumentative structure, the analysis is applying standards the argument rejects, and this should be acknowledged and addressed rather than ignored.

**Pre-Methodological Historical Texts**

Historical texts written before the modern self-consciousness about philosophical methodology — ancient, medieval, Renaissance, and many early modern works — may employ methods that do not map neatly onto the contemporary taxonomy.

Plato's elenctic method is recognizably dialectical but differs from modern dialectical method in its assumptions, aims, and characteristic moves. Aristotle's method of "saving the appearances" (*phainomena*) has affinities with reflective equilibrium but differs in important respects — the appearances include not only the opinions of the many and the wise but also empirical observations, and the goal is not merely coherence but explanation. Medieval scholastic methods of disputed questions, with their formalized objections, responses, and determinations, have no direct modern equivalent.

When analyzing pre-methodological texts: (a) interpret the text's method first in its own terms and the terms of its historical intellectual context, drawing on relevant scholarly literature about the text's methodology in its historical setting; (b) provide translation into the modern taxonomy where possible, but flag the respects in which the translation is approximate or anachronistic — for instance, noting that Aristotle's method of "saving the appearances" is not identical to Rawlsian reflective equilibrium despite sharing a coherentist structure; (c) note where historical methods differ substantively from their apparent modern counterparts despite surface similarities — ancient dialectic as practiced by Plato or Aristotle is a fundamentally different activity from Hegelian or Marxist dialectic, and treating them as variants of "the" dialectical method obscures more than it illuminates; (d) consider whether the historical method has resources or insights that contemporary methods have lost or abandoned, rather than treating historical methods as merely primitive or undeveloped versions of modern ones; and (e) be attentive to the possibility that the very category of "philosophical method" as a distinct object of study is itself a modern construction, and that applying it to pre-modern texts involves a hermeneutic decision that should be made explicit rather than taken for granted.

**Additional edge cases:** The taxonomy and analytical framework of this skill cannot anticipate every methodological configuration that philosophical arguments may exhibit. Analysts should be alert to additional edge cases not covered above, including: arguments that employ what they explicitly identify as "no method" or that advocate methodological anarchism; arguments that are performatively self-referential, such that their method cannot be distinguished from their content; arguments embedded in non-standard formats (dialogues, aphorisms, letters, fictional narratives) where format and method are intertwined; and arguments that operate within a tradition whose methodological norms are so different from those captured in the taxonomy that applying the taxonomy itself becomes an exercise in philosophical translation across incommensurable frameworks. In all such cases, the analysis should prioritize accurate description and fair engagement over taxonomic completeness, acknowledging the limits of the framework while using it as far as it remains useful.

## ARS Interface

In the Academic Research System (ARS) pipeline, the method.dialectic.methodology skill serves two primary functions, integrating with other ARS skills at specific stages of the research and writing process:

**For methodology chapters, statements, and research design:** When composing a dissertation methodology chapter, a grant proposal's methodology statement, a thesis introduction, or any formal declaration of research method, invoke method.dialectic.methodology on drafts of your own argument to audit your methodological commitments before submitting to reviewers or committee members. The skill's structured report format identifies whether your declared methods (what you say you are doing) match your practiced methods (what you actually do in the argument), whether your methodological commitments are internally consistent and free of method-switching at critical junctures, whether your chosen methods are genuinely well-suited to your research aims rather than merely customary in your subfield, what limitations your methods impose on your conclusions, and what methodological objections a reviewer or committee member from a different tradition might raise. This systematic self-audit strengthens methodology sections by making implicit commitments explicit and by anticipating methodological critiques before they arrive from gatekeepers. Recommended sequence: use ars-plan or ars-outline to establish the research question, approach, and argument structure; then apply method.dialectic.methodology to audit the methodological coherence of the plan before committing to a full draft; then finalize the methodology statement with methodological self-awareness informed by the audit.

**For situating research within methodological debates:** When preparing a paper for journal submission, a dissertation chapter for committee review, or a conference presentation, invoke method.dialectic.methodology on the draft to identify which methodological debates and disputes your work participates in — whether you intend it to or not. Authors often make methodological choices without full awareness of the contentious debates surrounding those choices: they may use conceptual analysis without realizing that experimental philosophers have mounted serious challenges to its evidential basis; they may adopt a naturalistic framework without engaging the extensive literature on whether naturalism can accommodate normativity or consciousness; they may deploy formal methods without addressing concerns about idealization and applicability. The skill's taxonomy classification, limitation analysis, and alternative-method comparison help you articulate why your methodological choices are genuinely appropriate for your specific project (not just customary or convenient), anticipate methodological objections from reviewers from different traditions, and strengthen the paper's introduction and discussion sections with explicit methodological self-awareness that signals scholarly sophistication.

**Integration with specific ARS skills:**
- Pair with **ars-plan** during early planning: after establishing the research question and general methodological approach, apply method.dialectic.methodology to audit the plan's methodological coherence and identify potential tensions before writing begins. Methodological problems are cheaper to fix at the planning stage than after a full draft exists.
- Pair with **ars-outline** during structural planning: after outlining the paper's sections and argumentative flow, apply method.dialectic.methodology to check whether the planned argument's structure is methodologically coherent — whether the introduction's methodological framing matches the body's argumentative practice, whether the conclusion's claims respect the method's limitations.
- Pair with **ars-rebuttal-audit** when a reviewer or committee member has raised methodological objections to your work: the rebuttal audit handles the dialectical structure of the response (what must be conceded, what can be rebutted, what requires clarification), while method.dialectic.methodology identifies the specific methodological commitments behind the reviewer's challenge — is the objection methodological (the reviewer disagrees with your choice of method and favors a different one) or substantive (the reviewer accepts your method but disputes your results)?
- Pair with **ars-reviewer** during pre-submission preparation: use ars-reviewer to simulate the likely reviewer response to your paper; if the simulated reviewer raises methodological concerns, use method.dialectic.methodology to identify and address those concerns before actual submission.
- Pair with **ars-revision-coach** during revision cycles: when a revise-and-resubmit decision requires deepening or clarifying the paper's methodology, method.dialectic.methodology identifies exactly where and how the argument's methodological commitments need to be articulated, defended, or revised.

**Practical use in the ARS workflow:** The most common pattern is to invoke method.dialectic.methodology at three critical junctures. Early in the project, after drafting the research plan but before substantial writing, to verify that the planned methodology is coherent and fit for purpose. Mid-project, after a substantial draft exists, to audit the draft's methodological practice against the declared methodology and identify gaps. Pre-submission, to generate the methodological self-awareness that good methodology statements and discussion sections require and to anticipate methodological objections. At each juncture, the skill's output should feed back into the manuscript: the detected methods inform the methodology statement, the limitation analysis informs the discussion of scope and caveats, and the alternative method comparison informs the framing of the paper's contribution relative to other possible approaches. The skill is most valuable when used iteratively — methodological self-awareness deepens with each application as the author becomes more conscious of the methodological choices embedded in their own argumentative practice.
