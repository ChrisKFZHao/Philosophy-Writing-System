## Example 1: Robust Non-Naturalist Realism (Enoch)

### Input

*— Adapted from Enoch's indispensability argument for robust realism (Enoch, 2011, Taking Morality Seriously)*

> Deliberation is an ineliminable feature of human practical life. When we deliberate about what to do, we cannot but treat some considerations as reasons that apply to us independently of our desires or contingent commitments. If, in the midst of deliberation, I ask whether I have reason not to inflict gratuitous suffering, the question does not feel open — I find myself committed to the answer that I do have such a reason, and this commitment is not conditional on my having a desire to avoid inflicting suffering. This deliberative indispensability of irreducibly normative truths is best explained by the hypothesis that such truths exist — that there are stance-independent, non-natural normative facts that make our deliberative commitments truth-tracking. Therefore, robust non-naturalist moral realism is true: there exist irreducibly normative moral facts, they are mind-independent, and they are not reducible to natural facts.

### Output

```
## Moral Realism Position Analysis Report

### 1. Position Classification

Position: Robust Non-Naturalism (Enoch, Moore)

Textual evidence:
- "irreducibly normative truths" — explicit non-naturalism commitment
- "stance-independent, non-natural normative facts" — mind-independence plus non-naturalism
- "not reducible to natural facts" — explicit anti-reductionism
- "deliberative indispensability" — Enoch's signature argument form
- Truth-tracking language ("truth-tracking") — cognitivist commitment

### 2. Key Commitments

Cognitivism — Explicit: treats moral judgments as tracking truths ("truth-tracking").
Truth-status — Explicit: claims some moral judgments are true ("such truths exist").
Mind-independence — Explicit: stance-independent, not conditional on desires.
Existence of moral facts — Fundamental: irreducibly normative facts exist at the
fundamental ontological level.

### 3. Argument Structure Analysis

Type: Existence argument (arguing that moral facts exist) via indispensability inference.

Positive argument (reconstructed):
[P1] Deliberation is an ineliminable, non-optional human practice.
[P2] Within deliberation, we are unavoidably committed to the existence of
     irreducibly normative reasons that apply independently of our desires.
[P3] The best explanation of this unavoidable commitment is that such irreducibly
     normative truths actually exist (indispensability premise).
[C]  Therefore, robust non-naturalist moral realism is true.

Dialectical role: First-order (directly for realism). The argument is a positive
existence argument, not primarily a negative attack on rival views.

### 4. Dialectical Vulnerabilities

| Challenge | Rating | Justification | Response Resources |
|-----------|--------|---------------|-------------------|
| Queerness (Mackie) | High | Posits irreducibly normative facts — exactly the kind of entity Mackie targets. These facts are metaphysically queer (non-natural yet normative) and epistemologically queer (accessed via deliberation, not perception). | The indispensability argument IS the response — if deliberative commitment is non-optional, we must accept the ontology it commits us to. The "companions in guilt" strategy is also available (epistemic norms are similarly non-natural). |
| Disagreement | Medium | Non-natural facts provide no mechanism for convergence. Persistent moral disagreement is prima facie evidence against a single set of non-natural moral facts. | Enoch can respond: some disagreement is predicted (difficult access to non-natural facts) and convergence on some moral truths exists. |
| Supervenience (Blackburn) | High | Non-natural moral facts supervene on natural facts, but this necessary connection is mysterious for a non-naturalist. What explains it? | Enoch must treat supervenience as a brute modal fact or provide a non-naturalist explanation — a recognized challenge. |
| Evolutionary Debunking (Street) | High | No tracking account: evolutionary pressures track fitness, not non-natural moral truth. Why think our deliberative commitments track truth? | Enoch's response: third-factor explanations (survival and moral truth may share common causes) or pre-established harmony. |
| Open Question (Moore) | Not applicable | Moore's argument supports non-naturalism, not challenges it. | N/A |
| Frege-Geach | Not applicable | Cognitivist position. | N/A |

### 5. Recommended Counter-positions

1. Error Theory (Mackie): Directly challenges the core claim — deliberative indispensability
   does not entail ontological commitment. Deliberation may require treating considerations
   AS IF they were reasons without their actually being reasons.
2. Constructivism (Korsgaard): Grants deliberative indispensability but derives
   normativity from the constitutive standards of agency rather than from non-natural facts.
3. Quasi-Realist Expressivism (Blackburn): Explains deliberative phenomenology without
   ontological commitment — we project normative attitudes, and the "feeling of truth"
   is explained by our commitment to these attitudes, not by non-natural facts.

### 6. Spectrum Position Map

```
Robust Non-Naturalism <-- [THIS ARGUMENT]
       |
       | (fault line: metaphysical naturalism)
       |
Quietist Realism --> Cornell Realism --> Analytic Functionalism --> ...

## Example 2: Naturalist Moral Realism (Railton, Boyd)

### Input

*— Adapted from Railton's reductive naturalism and Boyd's Cornell realism*

> Moral facts are natural facts. The property of being morally right reduces to the property of being such as to be endorsed by an impartial, fully informed observer under ideal conditions. Just as water reduces to H2O through empirical discovery, moral rightness reduces to this complex natural property through moral theorizing. There is no need to posit non-natural properties or special faculties of moral intuition — moral properties are as natural as chemical properties, and moral knowledge is acquired through the same broadly empirical methods we use in science. Moral facts exist, are mind-independent (the impartial observer standard is not a matter of anyone's actual attitudes), and are reducible to natural facts. Therefore, reductive naturalist moral realism is the correct metaethical position.

### Output

```
## Moral Realism Position Analysis Report

### 1. Position Classification

Position: Reductive Naturalism (Railton)

Textual evidence:
- "Moral facts are natural facts" — naturalism commitment
- "reduces to the property of being such as to be endorsed by an impartial, fully
  informed observer" — reductive identity claim with a specified reduction base
- "no non-natural properties" — explicit exclusion of non-naturalism
- "as natural as chemical properties" — natural kind analogy
- "broadly empirical methods" — epistemological naturalism

### 2. Key Commitments

Cognitivism — Explicit: moral claims are truth-apt, and some are true.
Truth-status — Explicit: moral facts exist and some moral judgments are true.
Mind-independence — Explicit: impartial observer standard is not determined by
anyone's actual attitudes. This captures response-independent natural facts.
Existence of moral facts — Derivative: moral facts exist but are reducible to natural
facts. Ontological parsimony is achieved through reduction.

### 3. Argument Structure Analysis

Type: Nature argument (arguing what kind of thing moral facts are) with existence
commitment.

Positive argument (reconstructed):
[P1] Moral rightness is the property of being endorsed from an impartial, fully
     informed standpoint under ideal conditions. (reduction claim)
[P2] This property is a natural property — it involves no non-natural elements.
[P3] Natural properties are metaphysically unproblematic — they fit within a
     naturalistic worldview.
[C]  Therefore, moral facts exist as natural facts, reducible to impartial endorsement
     properties. Reductive naturalism is true.

Dialectical role: First-order (positive case for naturalism). The argument advances
a reductionist identity claim supported by a natural kind analogy.

### 4. Dialectical Vulnerabilities

| Challenge | Rating | Justification | Response Resources |
|-----------|--------|---------------|-------------------|
| Open Question (Moore) | High | For the proposed reduction: "X is endorsed by an impartial observer, but is X right?" The question seems open — it is not analytic that impartial endorsement constitutes rightness. | Railton's response: the reduction is synthetic a posteriori, not analytic. As with "water is H2O," the identity is discovered, not known by definition. The open question only challenges analytic reduction, not synthetic identity. |
| Moral Twin Earth (Horgan & Timmons) | Medium | A community using "right" to refer to what maximizes aggregate welfare vs. what an impartial observer would endorse — are they disagreeing or talking past each other? If the reduction is synthetic and externalist, the semantic extension is fixed by the property, not by speakers' concepts, making cross-community disagreement mysterious. | The Cornell realist version (Boyd) appeals to causal regulation rather than description — "right" is causally regulated by the natural property, preserving disagreement. |
| Queerness (Mackie) | Low | Reductive naturalism avoids queerness by reducing moral properties to natural ones. No metaphysically queer entities are posited. | N/A |
| Evolutionary Debunking | Medium | If rightness reduces to what conduces to flourishing under ideal conditions, evolution may partially track this — but the "ideal conditions" component may not be fitness-tracking. | Railton can argue: our capacity to take up the impartial standpoint is itself fitness-enhancing (social cooperation). |
| Supervenience (Blackburn) | Low | Naturalist realism explains supervenience easily — moral properties ARE natural properties (identity) or are grounded in them. No mystery. | N/A |
| Disagreement | Low | Disagreement is explained by differing empirical beliefs about what the impartial observer would endorse, which is expected on a naturalist picture. | N/A |

### 5. Recommended Counter-positions

1. Error Theory (Mackie): Even naturalized moral properties may be queer in their
   action-guiding character — a natural fact that intrinsically motivates is still
   metaphysically odd.
2. Expressivism (Blackburn): Challenges the reduction at the semantic level — moral
   language functions expressively, not descriptively, undermining the identity claim.
3. Robust Non-Naturalism (Enoch): Argues the reduction loses normativity — a natural
   property, even if endorsed by an ideal observer, does not intrinsically provide
   reasons. The normative force is lost in reduction.

### 6. Spectrum Position Map

```
... --> Analytic Functionalism --> Reductive Naturalism <-- [THIS ARGUMENT]
                                          |
                                          | (fault line: mind-independence of normative force)
                                          |
                                  Response-Dependence --> Constructivism --> ...

## Example 3: Error Theory (Mackie) — Queerness Argument

### Input

*— Adapted from Mackie's argument from queerness (Mackie, 1977, Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong)*

> If there were objective moral values, they would be entities or qualities or relations of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe. They would have to be intrinsically action-guiding — the mere recognition that something is wrong would necessarily provide a reason not to do it. Nothing else in the natural world has this property. Correspondingly, if we were aware of them, it would have to be by some special faculty of moral perception or intuition, utterly different from our ordinary ways of knowing anything else. The ontological extravagance and epistemological mystery of such entities gives us good reason to deny their existence. The best explanation of our moral experience — the sense that some things are objectively right or wrong — is not that we are detecting objective moral properties, but that we are projecting our subjective attitudes onto the world. Therefore, all positive first-order moral judgments are false. Error theory is the correct metaethical position.

### Output

```
## Moral Realism Position Analysis Report

### 1. Position Classification

Position: Error Theory (Mackie)

Textual evidence:
- "objective moral values" are "entities of a very strange sort" — queerness claim
- "intrinsically action-guiding" — metaphysical queerness component
- "special faculty of moral perception" — epistemological queerness component
- "projecting our subjective attitudes" — projection explanation for moral experience
- "all positive first-order moral judgments are false" — error-theoretic conclusion
- No positive normative alternative offered — consistent with error theory (not fictionalism)

### 2. Key Commitments

Cognitivism — Explicit: moral judgments are truth-apt (otherwise they could not be false).
The argument presupposes cognitivism to generate the error-theoretic conclusion.
Truth-status — Explicit: all positive moral claims are false (error). Moral claims
are systematically false because they presuppose the existence of objective values
that do not exist.
Mind-independence — The argument's target is mind-independent moral facts. Error theory
claims such facts do not exist — the mind-independence question does not arise for
non-existent entities.
Existence of moral facts — Non-existent: moral facts do not exist. The argument
concludes that moral discourse has a false presupposition (the existence of objective
values) and therefore all positive moral claims are false.

### 3. Argument Structure Analysis

Type: Negative argument (against moral realism) advancing error theory as the conclusion.

Positive argument for error theory (reconstructed):
[P1] If objective moral values existed, they would be metaphysically queer —
     intrinsically action-guiding, unlike any natural entity.
[P2] If objective moral values existed, they would be epistemologically queer —
     requiring a special faculty of moral intuition for access.
[P3] The ontological extravagance and epistemological mystery of such entities
     provides strong reason to deny their existence.
[P4] Our moral experience (the sense of objective moral rightness/wrongness) is
     best explained by the projection of subjective attitudes, not by detection
     of objective properties.
[C]  Therefore, objective moral values do not exist, and all positive first-order
     moral judgments are false (error theory).

Negative argument targets: Robust non-naturalism, quietist realism, naturalist realism
— any position that posits mind-independent moral facts. The queerness argument targets
the metaphysical and epistemological commitments of all realist positions, though with
varying force (strongest against non-naturalism).

Dialectical role: First-order (directly against realism, for error theory).

### 4. Dialectical Vulnerabilities

| Challenge | Rating | Justification | Response Resources |
|-----------|--------|---------------|-------------------|
| Companions in Guilt (Cuneo) | High | Epistemic facts (e.g., "one ought to believe what the evidence supports") share the queerness features — they are irreducibly normative and non-natural. If the queerness argument succeeds against moral facts, it equally succeeds against epistemic facts. But we cannot coherently reject epistemic normativity. | Mackie could restrict the argument to practical normativity (excluding epistemic), but this requires a principled distinction between practical and epistemic "oughts" — a difficult task. |
| Queerness Overstated | Medium | The argument may overstate the queerness of moral facts. Naturalist realists (Railton, Boyd) argue moral facts are natural facts, not metaphysically queer. The queerness argument applies primarily to non-naturalist realism. | Mackie's response: naturalized moral facts lose their action-guiding character — they are no longer intrinsically reason-giving, which undermines their claim to be "moral" facts. |
| Projection Explanation Incomplete | Medium | The argument claims projection explains moral experience, but is the projection explanation complete? Expressivism (Blackburn, Gibbard) provides a developed account of how projection works and why moral discourse mimics descriptivism. Error theory, by contrast, merely notes the projection without providing a detailed semantics. | Mackie's error theory is compatible with expressivism about the speech act function of moral language; he does not need to provide a full semantics. |
| Frege-Geach | Low (not applicable) | Error theory is cognitivist — moral sentences are truth-apt (they are assertions), so embedding in unasserted contexts is not problematic. The problem is only that all positive moral assertions are false. | N/A |

### 5. Recommended Counter-positions

1. Robust Non-Naturalism (Enoch): Indispensability response — irreducibly normative
   truths are indispensable to deliberation, and entities indispensable to a
   non-optional practice are ontologically legitimate, queerness notwithstanding.
2. Naturalist Realism (Railton): Denies the queerness premise — moral properties
   reduce to natural properties and are accessed by ordinary empirical methods.
3. Quasi-Realist Expressivism (Blackburn): Accepts the anti-realist starting point
   but argues that expressivism can earn the right to realist-sounding discourse,
   avoiding both the queerness objection (no moral facts) and the error-theoretic
   conclusion (moral discourse is not systematically false, merely non-descriptive).

### 6. Spectrum Position Map

```
... --> Constructivism --> Error Theory <-- [THIS ARGUMENT]
                                |
                                | (fault line: truth-status — some true vs. all false)
                                |
                        Expressivism --> Fictionalism --> Relativism
```
```

## Example 4: Expressivism (Gibbard, Blackburn) — Quasi-Realism

### Input

*— Adapted from Gibbard's norm-expressivism and Blackburn's quasi-realism*

> Moral judgments do not describe facts or express beliefs about a moral reality. To judge that lying is wrong is not to attribute a property of wrongness to lying — it is to express one's acceptance of a norm that prohibits lying. Moral language has an expressive function: it voices our practical commitments and coordinates our attitudes. However, this does not mean moral discourse is merely emotive or that we cannot speak of "moral truth" or "moral facts." Quasi-realism shows that expressivism can earn the right to realist-sounding discourse — once we understand that calling something "morally true" is itself an expression of endorsement (endorsing the norm in question), we can participate fully in moral discourse, including making truth claims, without ontological commitment to moral facts. Therefore, expressivism avoids both the queerness of non-natural facts and the revisionary character of error theory while preserving the surface grammar of moral discourse.

### Output

```
## Moral Realism Position Analysis Report

### 1. Position Classification

Position: Expressivism — Quasi-Realist (Blackburn, Gibbard)

Textual evidence:
- "do not describe facts or express beliefs about a moral reality" — anti-descriptivism
- "express one's acceptance of a norm" — norm-expressivism (Gibbard)
- "expressive function" — expressivist semantics
- "voice our practical commitments and coordinate our attitudes" — the positive
  function of moral language
- "earn the right to realist-sounding discourse" — quasi-realism marker
- "making truth claims, without ontological commitment to moral facts" — quasi-realist
  deflationism about moral truth
- "avoids both the queerness... and the revisionary character of error theory" —
  dialectical positioning between realism and error theory

### 2. Key Commitments

Cognitivism — No: the primary function of moral language is expressive, not descriptive.
However, quasi-realism licenses the use of truth-talk through a deflationary/minimalist
conception of truth ("calling something true is itself an expression of endorsement").

Truth-status — Yes (minimal): quasi-realism permits moral truth claims, but "truth" is
understood deflationarily — "it is true that lying is wrong" is an expression of
endorsement of the norm prohibiting lying, not a report of correspondence with moral
reality.

Mind-independence — No: moral "facts" are projective — they are expressions of our
attitudes and practical commitments, not mind-independent features of reality. The
quasi-realist project explains why we experience morality as objective without
committing to the existence of objective moral facts.

Existence of moral facts — Projected: moral facts do not literally exist. Moral
discourse projects our attitudes onto the world, and quasi-realism explains why this
projection does not involve error (because moral claims are not assertions of
mind-independent facts in the first place).

### 3. Argument Structure Analysis

Type: Dialectical/defensive — the argument defends expressivism against the objection
that anti-realism cannot account for the surface features of moral discourse.

Positive argument for quasi-realist expressivism (reconstructed):
[P1] Moral judgments have an expressive function: they express acceptance of norms
     or practical commitments, not beliefs about moral reality.
[P2] Simple expressivism (Ayer) fails to explain the surface features of moral
     discourse — truth claims, embedding in conditionals, moral reasoning.
[P3] Quasi-realism supplements expressivism with a systematic account of how
     expressivists can legitimately use truth-talk, fact-talk, and knowledge-talk:
     these are expressions of further normative endorsement, not ontological
     commitments.
[P4] Quasi-realist expressivism thus avoids the queerness objection (no queer entities),
     the Frege-Geach problem (compositional semantics provided), and the revisionary
     character of error theory (moral discourse is not systematically false — it is
     not truth-apt in the correspondence sense at all).
[C]  Therefore, quasi-realist expressivism is the most defensible metaethical position.

Dialectical role: Defensive in part (responding to Frege-Geach and truth-talk objections)
but also first-order positive (arguing for expressivism as superior to realism and error theory).

### 4. Dialectical Vulnerabilities

| Challenge | Rating | Justification | Response Resources |
|-----------|--------|---------------|-------------------|
| Frege-Geach Problem | Medium | Although quasi-realism has developed sophisticated responses (Blackburn's attitudinal semantics, Gibbard's factual-normative worlds), critics (Schroeder, Unwin) argue the compositional semantics remains problematic — the attitudes expressed in embedded contexts may not compose as required. | Gibbard's (2003) "Thinking How to Live" provides a detailed compositional semantics; Blackburn's (1998) "Ruling Passions" addresses the embedding challenge. The response is developed but contested. |
| Creeping Realism (Wright) | Medium | If quasi-realism successfully mimics all the surface features of realism — truth, facts, knowledge, objectivity — what distinguishes it from genuine realism? If the functional role of "moral truth" in quasi-realism is indistinguishable from realism, the distinction may collapse. | Blackburn's response: the distinguishing feature is the underlying metaphysics — expressivism explains moral discourse WITHOUT ontological commitment to moral facts. The surface similarity is the quasi-realist achievement, not a problem. |
| Queerness (Mackie) | Not applicable | Expressivism avoids queerness entirely — no moral facts are posited. | N/A |
| Disagreement | Low | Expressivism can explain disagreement as clash of attitudes rather than contradictory beliefs. Disagreement is genuine (we are genuinely in conflict) without requiring a shared subject matter of fact. | N/A |
| Evolutionary Debunking | Low | Expressivism is compatible with evolutionary explanations of moral judgment — evolution shapes our attitudes, and moral judgments express those attitudes. No truth-tracking problem arises. | N/A |

### 5. Recommended Counter-positions

1. Robust Non-Naturalism (Enoch): Challenges the expressivist explanation of deliberative
   phenomenology — we experience ourselves as responsive to reasons, not merely as
   expressing attitudes.
2. Error Theory (Mackie): Argues expressivism faces a dilemma: either quasi-realism
   collapses into realism (losing its anti-realist advantage) or it fails to account
   for the objectivity of moral discourse (leaving error theory as the more honest
   anti-realism).
3. Cornell Realism (Boyd): Challenges the semantic starting point — moral predicates
   function as natural kind terms picking out natural properties, not as expressive
   devices.

### 6. Spectrum Position Map

```
Error Theory --> Expressivism (Quasi-Realist) <-- [THIS ARGUMENT]
                        |
                        | (fault line: truth-aptness through deflationism)
                        |
                Expressivism (Standard) --> Fictionalism --> Relativism
```
```

## Example 5: Position Classification — An Ambiguous Case

### Input

*— A text whose metaethical commitments are ambiguous between constructivism and response-dependence*

> Moral requirements are not discovered ready-made in the fabric of the universe, nor are they mere projections of our subjective attitudes. Rather, moral requirements are constituted by what we would agree to under conditions of free, rational deliberation. When we ask whether an action is morally right, we are asking whether it would be endorsed from a practical standpoint that all rational agents could share. This practical standpoint is not arbitrary — it is constrained by the constitutive features of practical reason itself, including consistency, universality, and responsiveness to the interests of all affected. Thus, moral facts exist, but they are not mind-independent in the way that physical facts are — they are constituted by the operation of practical reason under idealized conditions. Some might call this "constructivism"; others might call it a form of "response-dependence" realism. The truth may lie somewhere in between.

### Output

```
## Moral Realism Position Analysis Report

### 1. Position Classification

Classification: Ambiguous — between Constructivism (Korsgaard, Street) and
Response-Dependence (McDowell, Wiggins).

The argument's own language acknowledges the ambiguity: "some might call this
'constructivism'; others might call it... 'response-dependence' realism."

Evidence for Constructivism:
- "constituted by what we would agree to under conditions of free, rational deliberation"
  — procedural constructivism language
- "constitutive features of practical reason" — Korsgaardian constitutivism
- "not mind-independent in the way that physical facts are" — stance-dependence
- "practical standpoint" — constructivist terminology

Evidence for Response-Dependence:
- "what we would agree to under conditions of free, rational deliberation" — this is
  structurally parallel to response-dependence (X is right = X is such as to elicit
  the appropriate response from agents under ideal conditions)
- "not mere projections" — resists expressivism, aligning with realism-adjacent views
- "moral facts exist" — realist commitment
- "endorsed from a practical standpoint all rational agents could share" — ideal
  response condition

Diagnostic question: Does the argument treat the "practical standpoint of rational
agency" as constitutive (constructivism — moral facts are constituted by the
procedure of practical reason) or as a detection condition (response-dependence —
moral facts are detected by, but not constituted by, the standpoint)?

If constitutive: Constructivism. The procedure constitutes moral facts rather than
tracking pre-existing ones.

If detection: Response-dependence realism. The ideal standpoint is the condition
under which we correctly detect pre-existing moral facts (analogous to secondary
qualities — color as a disposition to cause certain experiences in normal observers).

### 2. Key Commitments (per interpretation)

Common to both interpretations:
- Cognitivism: Yes. Moral judgments are truth-apt.
- Truth-status: Yes, some moral claims are true.
- Existence: Moral facts exist (at the constructed or response-dependent level).

Differences:
- Mind-independence (constructivism): No — moral facts are constituted by the
  practical standpoint. Stance-dependent.
- Mind-independence (response-dependence): Partial — dispositional. Moral facts are
  response-dependent but not stance-constituted. The practical standpoint detects
  rather than constitutes moral facts.

### 3. Argument Structure Analysis

Type: Nature argument (what kind of thing are moral facts?).

The argument is first-order positive about moral facts existing but avoids committing
to the specific mechanism of their dependence on practical reason. This strategic
ambiguity may be intentional — the argument is positioning itself against both
robust realism (on one side) and expressivism/error theory (on the other) without
taking sides in the constructivism vs. response-dependence debate.

### 4. Dialectical Vulnerabilities

| Challenge | Rating | Justification |
|-----------|--------|---------------|
| Queerness | Medium-Low | Both interpretations avoid positing non-natural entities. Constructivism: moral facts are constituted by practical reason, not metaphysically queer. Response-dependence: analogous to secondary qualities, not metaphysically extravagant. |
| Disagreement | Medium | Idealized rational deliberation may still yield disagreement if there are multiple defensible standpoints. The argument needs an account of why the practical standpoint uniquely determines answers. |
| Euthyphro-style | Medium (constructivism) | Does the practical standpoint constitute moral facts because the facts are what they are, or are the moral facts what they are because constituted by the standpoint? Constructivism faces the question of whether the constitutive standards are themselves arbitrary. |

### 5. Recommended Counter-positions

1. Robust Non-Naturalism: Challenges both — why think moral facts are constituted
   by or dependent on the practical standpoint? Perhaps the standpoint tracks
   pre-existing normative facts.
2. Expressivism: Challenges the cognitivist assumption — the "practical standpoint"
   language may be better explained by expressivism (expressing endorsement from a
   shared standpoint) without ontological commitment.
3. Error Theory: Challenges the existence claim — idealized rational agreement may
   be systematically unachievable or indeterminate, in which case no moral facts
   (constructed or response-dependent) exist.

### 6. Spectrum Position Map

```
Cornell Realism --> Analytic Functionalism --> Reductive Naturalism
                                                     |
Response-Dependence <-- [Ambiguous: THIS vs. below]   |
        |                                             |
Constructivism <-- [Ambiguous: THIS vs. above]        |
        |
Error Theory --> Expressivism --> ...
```

### Resolution Guidance

To resolve the ambiguity, additional textual evidence would be needed. If the text
were to claim that "there are no moral facts prior to the procedure of practical
deliberation — the procedure constitutes the facts," the classification would be
constructivism. If the text were to claim that "moral facts are there to be discovered
by agents under ideal conditions, analogous to how colors are there to be perceived
by normal observers," the classification would be response-dependence realism.
```
