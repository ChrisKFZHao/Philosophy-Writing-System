## Example 1: Companions in Guilt -- Moral Facts vs. Mathematical Facts

### Input

> You reject moral facts as metaphysically queer -- they would have to be non-natural, causally inert, intrinsically normative entities of a sort not found anywhere else in our ontology. But mathematical facts share precisely these features: they are non-natural, causally inert, and (on many accounts) known through a priori intuition rather than empirical observation. Yet you do not reject mathematical facts. By parity of reasoning, if you reject moral facts for their queerness, you must also reject mathematical facts -- or else identify a relevant difference between them. Since rejecting mathematical facts is a cost you are unwilling to pay, you should reconsider your rejection of moral facts.
>
> This is a canonical companions-in-guilt argument, modeled after Cuneo (2007) and Enoch (2011), responding to Mackie's (1977) argument from queerness against moral realism.

### Output

```
## Parity Analysis Report

### Parity Claim Restatement

**Explicit parity statement:** Moral facts and mathematical facts are relevantly similar with respect to the features that allegedly make moral facts objectionable -- non-naturalness, causal inertness, and reliance on a priori intuition -- such that the error theorist's rejection of moral facts on grounds of queerness compels (by parity) the rejection of mathematical facts.

**Logically presupposed parity claim:** If feature F (queerness) is sufficient grounds for rejecting entities of type T1 (moral facts), and entities of type T2 (mathematical facts) also possess F, then consistency requires rejecting T2 as well.

**Rhetorically needed parity claim:** The set of features that make moral facts objectionable is exactly the set of features mathematical facts possess, with no additional feature of moral facts that independently justifies their rejection. The parity argument succeeds only if the queerness of moral facts is not overdetermined by features absent from mathematical facts.

### Respect of Comparison

The respect of comparison is a cluster of properties: non-naturalness (not reducible to natural facts), causal inertness (no causal powers), and epistemological peculiarity (known through a priori intuition rather than empirical observation). This is a descriptive respect of comparison: the cases are claimed to share these features as a matter of metaphysical fact. The respect is structural as well -- both types of fact are claimed to stand in the same relation to natural facts (they are not reducible to them) and to empirical epistemology (they are not known through ordinary empirical means).

### Difference Inventory

**Descriptive differences:**
1. Moral facts are normative (they tell us what we ought to do); mathematical facts are non-normative (they tell us what is the case, not what ought to be the case).
2. Moral facts have a connection to motivation and action (moral judgments motivate); mathematical facts do not.
3. Moral facts are subject to widespread, persistent disagreement across cultures; mathematical facts are not (or are less so).
4. Moral facts supervene on natural facts in a way that some find metaphysically puzzling; mathematical facts have a different relationship to the physical world.
5. The epistemology of moral facts is contested (intuitionism, coherentism, etc.); the epistemology of mathematical facts involves proof, which has no clear moral analogue.

**Normative differences:**
6. Moral facts are action-guiding in a practical sense; mathematical facts are not.
7. Denying moral facts has practical consequences (it may license immoral behavior); denying mathematical facts does not (or has different practical consequences).

**Structural differences:**
8. Mathematical facts form a systematic, axiomatizable body of knowledge with clear criteria of correctness (proof); moral facts do not (or their systematicity is far more contested).
9. Moral facts are embedded in a web of social practices (blame, praise, punishment); mathematical facts are embedded in a different set of practices (calculation, proof, application in science).

### Relevance Assessment

| Difference | Status | Principle |
|------------|--------|-----------|
| (1) Normativity | Potentially relevant but underdetermined | If normativity is an additional source of queerness beyond non-naturalness and causal inertness -- i.e., if the objection is not merely that moral facts are non-natural but that they are non-natural *and* normative -- then this difference may justify rejecting moral facts while retaining mathematical facts. The principle would be: "Non-natural, causally inert entities are acceptable only if they are non-normative." Whether this principle is defensible is contested. |
| (2) Motivational connection | Potentially relevant but underdetermined | If the queerness objection is partly driven by the puzzle of how non-natural facts can motivate action, and mathematical facts do not motivate, the objection applies asymmetrically. The principle would be: "Facts that are both non-natural and motivationally efficacious are uniquely objectionable." |
| (3) Disagreement | Potentially relevant but underdetermined | Persistent disagreement is sometimes taken as evidence against realism (the argument from disagreement). If moral disagreement is deeper and more intractable than mathematical disagreement, this constitutes an additional reason to reject moral realism that does not extend to mathematical realism. However, there is also deep disagreement in mathematics (e.g., about the status of the Axiom of Choice), so the asymmetry may be overstated. |
| (4) Supervenience peculiarity | Potentially relevant but underdetermined | Mackie's original argument highlighted the supervenience of the moral on the natural as a specific puzzle. If mathematical facts do not stand in a similarly puzzling supervenience relation, this is a relevant difference. |
| (5) Proof vs. intuition | Genuinely relevant (subject to debate) | If mathematical facts are known by proof -- a reliable, public, checkable method -- while moral facts are known by intuition -- a method whose reliability is contested -- then the epistemological objection applies asymmetrically. The principle: "Entities accessible only through contested epistemic methods are more objectionable than entities accessible through uncontested methods." |
| (6-7) Practical consequences | Clearly irrelevant | The practical consequences of denying a class of facts do not bear on whether those facts exist. The queerness argument is metaphysical, not pragmatic. |
| (8) Systematicity | Potentially relevant but underdetermined | If systematicity and proof-based justification provide a non-queer epistemology for mathematics, and moral epistemology lacks an analogue, then mathematical facts are less queer. |
| (9) Social embedding | Clearly irrelevant | The social practices surrounding a class of facts do not bear on their metaphysical status. |

### Verdict

**Parity indeterminate.** The relevance of the key differences between moral and mathematical facts -- normativity, motivational connection, epistemological access, systematicity -- depends on the precise formulation of the queerness argument and on substantive positions in metaethics, philosophy of mathematics, and epistemology that are not settled by the materials supplied. If the queerness objection targets only non-naturalness and causal inertness, parity holds. If it targets a broader syndrome that includes normativity and epistemic inaccessibility, parity may fail. The analysis identifies what each party would need to establish: the parity proponent must show that the objectionable features are genuinely shared; the parity opponent must show that the unshared features (normativity, epistemic peculiarity) are independently sufficient to ground the rejection of moral facts.

### Suppressed Disanalogies

1. **Independently plausible:** Moral facts are essentially contested in a way mathematical facts are not. The concept of a "moral fact" is itself the subject of metaethical dispute; the concept of a "mathematical fact" is not (or is less so). If the concept of the disputed entity is itself contested, the parity argument may compare a clear case (mathematical facts) with an unclear one (moral facts).

2. **Merely possible:** Mathematical facts are reducible to logical facts, and logical facts are not queer because they are constitutive of thought itself; moral facts have no such reduction. This disanalogy is philosophically live (logicism about mathematics) but not established.

### Dialectical Consequences

**If parity holds:** The error theorist must either (a) extend the error theory to mathematics (a cost most are unwilling to pay), (b) abandon the error theory about morality, or (c) provide a principled account of why the queerness of moral facts is qualitatively different from the queerness of mathematical facts. The parity argument shifts the burden to the error theorist; it does not, by itself, establish moral realism.

**If parity fails:** The error theorist retains the argument from queerness against moral facts, but the burden is now on them to articulate the specific feature of moral facts that warrants rejection and that is absent from mathematical facts. The debate advances: the queerness argument must be reformulated with greater precision about what exactly is objectionable and why.

**Boomerang risk:** If parity holds, the error theorist might respond that the argument cuts both ways: perhaps mathematical facts are queer and should be rejected. The parity proponent must be prepared to defend mathematical realism independently, or else the parity argument "boomerangs" into a general skepticism about non-natural facts.
```

## Example 2: The Argument from Marginal Cases (Singer)

### Input

> If we justify our differential treatment of humans and non-human animals by citing some cognitive capacity -- rationality, self-consciousness, linguistic ability -- then, by parity, we must also exclude those humans who lack that capacity from the scope of moral consideration. Infants, severely cognitively disabled adults, and senile elderly persons lack the capacities that are supposed to distinguish humans from animals. Yet we do not -- and should not -- treat these "marginal cases" of humanity as lacking moral status. Therefore, the cited capacity cannot be the ground of moral status, and our differential treatment of animals lacks justification.
>
> This is the argument from marginal cases, most influentially developed by Peter Singer in *Animal Liberation* (1975) and *Practical Ethics* (1979).

### Output

```
## Parity Analysis Report

### Parity Claim Restatement

**Explicit parity statement:** Non-human animals and marginal-case humans (infants, severely cognitively disabled adults) are relevantly similar with respect to the capacities that are cited to justify differential moral treatment, such that the denial of moral status to animals compels (by parity) the denial of moral status to marginal-case humans.

**Logically presupposed parity claim:** If feature F (e.g., self-consciousness) is the ground of moral status, and both animals and marginal-case humans lack F, then consistency requires treating them as having the same moral status -- either both have it, or neither does.

**Rhetorically needed parity claim:** There is no alternative ground of moral status that includes all humans while excluding all non-human animals. The argument succeeds fully only if every candidate ground of moral status either includes animals or excludes some humans.

### Respect of Comparison

The respect of comparison is the absence of a specific cognitive capacity -- typically, rationality, self-consciousness, linguistic ability, or moral agency -- that is claimed to distinguish humans from non-human animals. This is a descriptive respect: marginal-case humans and animals share the property of lacking the capacity in question. The respect is also normative: the capacity is claimed to be the ground of moral status, so its absence is claimed to be normatively decisive.

### Difference Inventory

**Descriptive differences:**
1. Marginal-case humans are members of the species Homo sapiens; animals are not.
2. Marginal-case humans typically have human parents and are embedded in human families.
3. Marginal-case humans have the *potential* to develop the relevant capacities (in the case of infants) or *once possessed* them (in the case of the senile).
4. Marginal-case humans have human DNA and a human biological form.
5. Animals belong to species with different typical capacities; marginal-case humans are atypical members of a species whose typical member has the relevant capacities.

**Normative differences:**
6. Marginal-case humans are protected by existing social practices, legal frameworks, and moral norms.
7. Killing a marginal-case human typically causes grief and harm to other humans (family members); killing an animal may not (or does so to a lesser degree).

**Structural differences:**
8. Excluding marginal-case humans from moral status threatens to undermine the moral status of all humans (slippery slope).
9. Including animals in moral status has far-reaching practical consequences (for agriculture, research, etc.) that including marginal-case humans does not.

### Relevance Assessment

| Difference | Status | Principle |
|------------|--------|-----------|
| (1) Species membership | Genuinely relevant only if speciesism is defensible | The principle "species membership is a ground of moral status" is precisely what the parity argument challenges. The parity proponent argues that species membership, like race or sex, is a morally arbitrary characteristic. The opponent must defend species membership as non-arbitrary -- a steep dialectical burden. Singer himself calls speciesism a prejudice analogous to racism. |
| (2) Relational properties (family ties) | Potentially relevant | If moral status is grounded in relational properties (e.g., being loved, being part of a moral community), then marginal-case humans may have moral status that animals lack. The principle: "Relational properties can ground moral status." This is a live position (e.g., Scanlon's contractualism, care ethics), but it has the counterintuitive implication that an unloved orphan lacks moral status. |
| (3) Potentiality | Genuinely relevant (for infants) but limited scope | The principle: "Potential to develop a capacity grounds the same moral status as actual possession of the capacity." This protects infants but not the permanently cognitively disabled. The parity argument is only partially blocked: the permanently disabled remain as marginal cases with no potentiality defense. |
| (4) Human DNA / biological form | Clearly irrelevant (absent further argument) | Mere biological classification, without a connection to morally relevant properties, is speciesism by definition. The opponent must show why human DNA is morally significant. |
| (5) Typical vs. atypical | Potentially relevant but underdetermined | The principle: "Moral status can be grounded in the typical capacities of the species to which an individual belongs, even if that individual lacks them." This "species norm" account is defended by some (e.g., Scanlon briefly considers it) but is widely criticized as ad hoc. |
| (6) Existing social practices | Clearly irrelevant | The fact that differential treatment is entrenched does not justify it. This is a status-quo bias, not a normative principle. |
| (7) Harm to third parties | Potentially relevant | Killing a marginal-case human may cause more harm (to family, to social trust) than killing an animal. But this is a reason to prohibit killing, not a ground of the individual's moral status. It also does not apply to marginal-case humans without families. |
| (8) Slippery-slope risk | Clearly irrelevant to the parity claim | The practical risk of a policy does not determine whether the parity claim is sound. This is a pragmatic consideration, not a truth-directed one. |
| (9) Practical consequences | Clearly irrelevant | The inconvenience of treating animals morally is not a principled reason to deny them moral status. |

### Verdict

**Parity holds (with qualification).** On the central question -- whether the absence of a given cognitive capacity justifies differential moral treatment -- parity holds between animals and marginal-case humans. No proposed relevant difference survives scrutiny without invoking species membership as an independent ground of moral status, which is precisely what the argument challenges. The potentiality defense protects only a subset of marginal cases (infants, not the permanently disabled). Relational properties provide a possible basis for differential treatment, but this basis is independent of the cognitive-capacity criterion that the original argument targets. The parity argument succeeds in shifting the burden: the defender of differential treatment must either (a) bite the bullet and exclude marginal-case humans (a position almost no one accepts), (b) identify a non-cognitive-capacity ground of moral status that includes all humans and excludes all animals (a position no one has successfully articulated), or (c) extend moral status to animals.

### Suppressed Disanalogies

1. **Merely possible:** Marginal-case humans possess an immaterial soul; animals do not. This disanalogy is not independently plausible in a secular philosophical context and is not dialectically available unless the opponent is willing to defend substance dualism.

### Dialectical Consequences

**If parity holds (as it does):** The opponent must either (a) accept the conclusion and extend moral status to animals, (b) identify a novel ground of moral status that distinguishes all humans from all animals, or (c) reject the premise and bite the bullet on excluding marginal-case humans. Options (b) and (c) are the live dialectical alternatives. Option (b) requires philosophical work (a novel theory of moral status). Option (c) is widely regarded as a reductio of the opponent's position. The parity argument does not, by itself, establish that animals have moral status; it establishes that the opponent's stated grounds for denying moral status to animals overgeneralize.

**If parity fails:** This would require identifying a genuine, principled, non-speciesist relevant difference. The philosophical literature has been searching for such a difference for decades without consensus. A successful response would be a major contribution to moral philosophy.
```

## Example 3: A Parity Chain -- The Slippery Slope in Abortion Ethics

### Input

> Consider the developmental spectrum from conception to birth. At conception, we have a single cell. At birth, we have a newborn infant. Between these endpoints are the zygote, the embryo at various stages, and the fetus at various stages. At no single point along this spectrum is there a sudden, non-arbitrary transition in moral status. Each adjacent stage differs from the next only incrementally -- a few cells, a rudimentary organ, the first neural activity. If a zygote lacks moral status, and each adjacent pair of stages is relevantly similar, then by repeated application of parity reasoning, a fetus at any stage lacks moral status. Therefore, if abortion is permissible at conception, it is permissible at any point up to birth.
>
> This is a parity-chain (sorites-style) argument about the moral status of the fetus. The argument claims that relevant similarity is transitive across the developmental spectrum.

### Output

```
## Parity Analysis Report

### Parity Claim Restatement

**Explicit parity statement:** Each adjacent pair of developmental stages (e.g., zygote and early embryo, 12-week fetus and 13-week fetus) is relevantly similar with respect to the properties that ground moral status, such that no single pair can sustain a difference in moral permissibility.

**Logically presupposed parity claim:** Relevant similarity is transitive across the chain: if stage S1 is relevantly similar to S2, and S2 to S3, ..., and Sn-1 to Sn, then S1 is relevantly similar to Sn. The transitivity of relevant similarity is the suppressed logical premise.

**Rhetorically needed parity claim:** The transitivity holds even though the accumulation of small differences across many stages may produce a relevant difference between the endpoints. The argument succeeds fully only if the tolerance principle -- small differences do not justify different treatment -- does not yield to the accumulation of small differences.

### Respect of Comparison

The respect of comparison is the set of properties that are taken to ground moral status at each developmental stage: sentience, viability, consciousness, brain activity, and so forth. The claim is that between any two adjacent stages, these properties differ only minimally, and the minimal difference is not sufficient to ground a moral-status difference.

### Difference Inventory

The differences are organized by stage pairs rather than globally, because the parity claim is about adjacent stages.

**Zygote vs. early embryo (1-2 weeks):** Cell differentiation begins; twinning is still possible. Minimal neurological development.

**Early embryo vs. late embryo (2-8 weeks):** Organogenesis; the neural tube forms; primitive brain structures appear. Significant developmental event: the beginning of brain activity.

**Embryo vs. early fetus (8-12 weeks):** All major organs present; reflexive movement; brain activity increases. Significant developmental event: the fetus begins to move.

**Early fetus vs. mid-gestation fetus (12-24 weeks):** Viability threshold (around 24 weeks with intensive care); sentience may emerge; pain perception is debated. Significant developmental event: the threshold of viability.

**Mid-gestation fetus vs. late-gestation fetus (24-38 weeks):** Full brain development; consciousness may emerge; the fetus can survive outside the womb. Significant developmental event: the emergence of consciousness (if it occurs before birth).

**Late-gestation fetus vs. newborn:** The newborn is physically separate from the mother; breathes air; interacts socially. Significant developmental event: birth itself (change in location and dependence).

### Relevance Assessment

The key question is whether each adjacent pair is *relevantly* similar -- i.e., whether the differences between adjacent stages are small enough that they cannot justify a difference in moral status, even though the cumulative differences between the endpoints clearly can.

**Assessment by pair:**

| Pair | Relevant Difference? | Rationale |
|------|---------------------|-----------|
| Zygote / early embryo | No | The differences (cell division, onset of differentiation) do not introduce any property widely recognized as grounding moral status. |
| Early embryo / late embryo | Potentially yes | The onset of brain activity (around 6-8 weeks) may be morally significant. If consciousness or sentience is the ground of moral status, and brain activity is a necessary condition for either, this transition may be relevant. |
| Late embryo / early fetus | Potentially yes (if the previous was not) | Reflexive movement and increasing brain complexity may be morally significant, for the same reasons. |
| Early fetus / mid-gestation (24 weeks) | Genuinely yes (viability and sentience) | Viability is often treated as morally significant in abortion law and ethics. The capacity for sentience (if it emerges around this stage) is widely recognized as grounding moral status. |
| Mid-gestation / late-gestation | Genuinely yes (consciousness) | The possible emergence of consciousness is a major candidate for a moral-status threshold. |
| Late-gestation / newborn | Genuinely yes (birth) | Birth marks the transition to an independent existence. Even if the difference is merely one of location, many moral theories treat birth as normatively significant (it changes the relationship and the available options for sustaining the infant). |

### Transitivity Assessment

**Tolerance principle:** The claim that small differences between adjacent stages do not justify different treatment.

**Accumulation test:** Even if each adjacent pair is relevantly similar (because the differences are small), the small differences accumulate. The difference between a zygote and a newborn is enormous, and it includes the emergence of brain activity, sentience, viability, and (possibly) consciousness. The accumulation of small differences across a chain can produce a relevant difference between the endpoints, even if no single adjacent pair triggers a break. This blocks the transitivity of parity: from the fact that S1 is relevantly similar to S2 and S2 to S3 (and so on), it does not follow that S1 is relevantly similar to S37.

**Higher-order similarity test:** The parity proponent cannot establish direct parity between a zygote and a newborn. The endpoints are manifestly different in morally significant ways. The argument relies entirely on the transitivity of the intermediate steps, and the transitivity fails.

**Vagueness consideration:** The developmental spectrum is a classic sorites case. The concept "has moral status" is vague, and there is no sharp boundary between "has moral status" and "lacks moral status." But the absence of a sharp boundary does not entail that the endpoints are relevantly similar -- only that any boundary will be somewhat arbitrary. This is a general problem with vague concepts, not a special problem for this parity argument.

### Verdict

**Parity fails (the chain is not transitive).** The argument depends on the transitivity of relevant similarity, and the transitivity fails because small, individually insignificant differences can accumulate to produce a morally significant difference between the endpoints. Specifically, there are at least three candidate transition points -- the onset of brain activity, the emergence of sentience, and viability -- where a morally significant change plausibly occurs. Even if each transition is gradual rather than instantaneous, the graduality does not mean the transition is irrelevant; it means that boundary-drawing will be somewhat arbitrary, which is a feature of all vague concepts and does not undermine the reality of the distinction between the endpoints.

### Suppressed Disanalogies

1. **Independently plausible:** The developmental spectrum is not continuous in all relevant respects. There are genuinely novel emergent properties -- the first neural activity, the first capacity for pain, the first capacity for consciousness -- that do not appear gradually but emerge when a threshold of complexity is reached. If emergence is real, then adjacent stages across an emergence threshold are not relevantly similar, and the parity chain has a genuine break.

### Dialectical Consequences

**Given that parity fails:** The proponent of the parity-chain argument must either (a) identify a different chain where no candidate thresholds exist, (b) defend the transitivity of relevant similarity against the accumulation challenge, or (c) abandon the parity-chain strategy and argue directly about the moral status of the fetus at specific stages. The failure of the parity chain does not establish that late-term abortion is impermissible; it establishes that the permissibility of early abortion does not, by itself and without additional argument, entail the permissibility of late-term abortion. The opponent (defending differential treatment of early and late abortion) still bears the burden of identifying the specific grounds for the differential treatment -- but they need not defend the transitivity of relevant similarity across the entire spectrum.

**For the proponent:** The parity-chain argument is rhetorically effective but logically flawed. The proponent should instead argue that specific candidate thresholds (viability, sentience) do not ground a morally significant difference, rather than relying on the general claim that no adjacent pair can ground a difference.
```

## Example 4: A Failed Parity Claim with a Relevant Difference

### Input

> You say that government surveillance of private communications is an unacceptable violation of privacy. But you happily use social media platforms that collect and analyze your data, track your location, and build detailed profiles of your behavior. Your objection to government surveillance is inconsistent: if you accept corporate surveillance, by parity you should accept government surveillance, since both involve the collection and analysis of personal data without your ongoing, informed consent.
>
> This is a parity argument frequently deployed in debates about digital privacy. It claims that corporate and government surveillance are relevantly similar, such that accepting one compels accepting the other.

### Output

```
## Parity Analysis Report

### Parity Claim Restatement

**Explicit parity statement:** Government surveillance and corporate surveillance are relevantly similar with respect to the features that ground privacy concerns -- collection of personal data, tracking of behavior, and absence of ongoing informed consent -- such that accepting corporate surveillance compels acceptance of government surveillance.

**Logically presupposed parity claim:** If feature F (unauthorized data collection) is insufficient to ground an objection to X (corporate surveillance), and Y (government surveillance) also has feature F, then feature F is also insufficient to ground an objection to Y.

**Rhetorically needed parity claim:** Government and corporate surveillance share not only the surface features of data collection but also the deeper features that make data collection objectionable -- or, if they differ in deeper features, those differences are not relevant to the privacy objection.

### Respect of Comparison

The respect of comparison is the collection and analysis of personal data without ongoing, informed consent. This is a descriptive respect: both forms of surveillance involve data collection. The claim is that this shared feature is the feature that grounds the objection to government surveillance, so if it is insufficient to ground an objection to corporate surveillance, it cannot ground the objection to government surveillance.

### Difference Inventory

**Descriptive differences:**
1. Government surveillance is backed by the coercive power of the state (arrest, prosecution, imprisonment); corporate surveillance is backed by market incentives (targeted advertising, service denial).
2. Government surveillance has a monopoly on legitimate force; corporations do not.
3. Government surveillance can result in deprivation of liberty; corporate surveillance typically results in commercial consequences.
4. Government surveillance is subject to constitutional constraints (in some jurisdictions); corporate surveillance is subject to market and regulatory constraints.
5. Government surveillance is not opt-in (one cannot choose a different government); corporate surveillance is partially opt-in (one can choose different platforms, though with limited options).
6. Government surveillance collects data for law enforcement, national security, and governance; corporate surveillance collects data for profit.

**Normative differences:**
7. Government surveillance raises distinct concerns about democratic accountability and the separation of powers.
8. Corporate surveillance raises distinct concerns about market power and consumer exploitation.

**Structural differences:**
9. The scale and scope of government surveillance (in terms of coercive consequences) is qualitatively different from corporate surveillance.
10. The legal frameworks governing each are different, with different remedies available for abuse.

### Relevance Assessment

| Difference | Status | Principle |
|------------|--------|-----------|
| (1) Coercive power | Genuinely relevant | The objection to government surveillance is not merely about data collection but about data collection *by an entity with the power to imprison you*. The harm of erroneous or abusive government surveillance (loss of liberty) is categorically different from the harm of erroneous or abusive corporate surveillance (targeted advertising, account suspension). The principle: "The severity and nature of the potential harm from data misuse is relevant to the permissibility of data collection." |
| (3) Liberty deprivation | Genuinely relevant | Same reasoning as (1). The consequences of government surveillance are asymmetrically severe. |
| (5) Opt-in / exit options | Genuinely relevant | One can choose not to use Facebook; one cannot choose not to be governed. The voluntariness of the relationship affects the permissibility of data collection. The principle: "Voluntary data-sharing arrangements are less objectionable than involuntary ones." |
| (2) Monopoly on force | Potentially relevant | The state's unique powers make its surveillance qualitatively different, but this is largely captured by (1). |
| (6) Purpose of data collection | Potentially relevant | The purpose for which data is collected may affect the justification, but if the means are the same, the purpose may not fully distinguish the cases. |
| (7) Democratic accountability | Potentially relevant | Government surveillance is in principle subject to democratic control; corporate surveillance is not. This cuts both ways: democratic accountability may legitimate government surveillance (if properly authorized) or make it more dangerous (if majorities authorize oppressive surveillance). |

### Verdict

**Parity fails.** There are several genuinely relevant differences between government and corporate surveillance that justify differentiating between them without inconsistency. The most decisive differences are: (1) the coercive consequences of government surveillance (loss of liberty) are categorically more severe than those of corporate surveillance; (2) government surveillance is involuntary in a way corporate surveillance is not -- one can opt out of specific platforms but not out of state jurisdiction. These differences are directly relevant to the privacy objection: the objection to government surveillance is substantially driven by the unique harms that state power makes possible, not merely by the fact of data collection. The parity argument equivocates between a thin description of the shared feature ("data collection") and a thicker description that includes the consequences and context of that data collection, which differ crucially between the cases.

### Suppressed Disanalogies

1. **Independently plausible:** Government surveillance can be combined with other state powers (asset seizure, travel restrictions, no-fly lists) to produce harms far beyond what any corporation can inflict. Corporate surveillance lacks this combinatory potential.

### Dialectical Consequences

**Given that parity fails:** The opponent (the privacy advocate who objects to government but not corporate surveillance) is not inconsistent. Their position is coherent: they object to government surveillance because of the unique harms state power makes possible, not because data collection per se is unacceptable. The opponent may have independent reasons to object to corporate surveillance (and many privacy advocates do), but those reasons need not be the same as their reasons for objecting to government surveillance. The parity argument fails to establish inconsistency.

**For the proponent of the parity argument:** The argument can be repaired by narrowing the claim: if the objection to government surveillance is based solely on data collection rather than on the consequences of state power, then parity may hold. But most sophisticated privacy advocates do not base their objection solely on data collection; they base it on the combination of data collection with state power.

**For the respondent:** The most efficient response is to point to the coercive-power difference and the voluntariness difference. This requires no elaborate theoretical apparatus and is dialectically decisive against the simple form of the parity argument.
```

## Example 5: Formal vs. Substantive Parity -- The Problem of Trivial Parity

### Input

> You hold that only entities with causal powers exist. Numbers, you say, have no causal powers; therefore, numbers do not exist. But propositions also lack causal powers. By parity of reasoning, if you deny the existence of numbers because they lack causal powers, you must also deny the existence of propositions. Since you accept that propositions exist, you face a contradiction: either numbers exist, or propositions do not exist, or causal efficacy is not the correct criterion of existence.

This argument illustrates the distinction between formal and substantive parity. Formally, numbers and propositions share the property "lacks causal powers." But does this formal resemblance amount to substantive parity -- are they relevantly similar in the respects that matter for the causal criterion of existence?

### Output

```
## Parity Analysis Report

### Parity Claim Restatement

**Three levels distinguished:**

**Explicit parity statement:** Numbers and propositions are relevantly similar with respect to causal inertness, so the causal criterion of existence applies equally to both.

**Logically presupposed parity claim:** Causal inertness is a feature of entities, and if it is sufficient grounds for denying the existence of entities of type T1 (numbers), it is sufficient grounds for denying the existence of entities of type T2 (propositions), unless T2 differs from T1 in a respect that bears on the applicability of the causal criterion.

**Rhetorically needed parity claim:** The case against numbers' existence, grounded in their causal inertness, does not depend on any feature of numbers that propositions lack. If there is a feature of numbers that makes them existentially suspect beyond their causal inertness -- e.g., that they are abstract objects of a certain kind, that they are "Platonic" in a way propositions are not -- then parity does not compel the rejection of propositions.

### Respect of Comparison

The respect of comparison is a single property: lacking causal powers. This is a descriptive respect. Both numbers and propositions are abstract objects, and abstract objects are standardly characterized as causally inert.

**Ambiguity critical:** The respect of comparison is very thin -- a single property shared by a vast range of entities (numbers, propositions, properties, sets, universals, possible worlds, fictional characters, etc.). The thinness of the respect raises the question of whether the parity claim is trivial: if all that numbers and propositions share is the abstract-object feature of causal inertness, and the objection to numbers depends on features beyond causal inertness, then formal parity holds but substantive parity does not.

### Difference Inventory

**Descriptive differences:**
1. Numbers are mathematical objects; propositions are semantic objects (the bearers of truth-values).
2. Numbers are typically understood as existing necessarily and having their properties essentially; propositions also exist necessarily, but their nature is differently conceived.
3. Numbers can be reduced to sets (on some accounts); propositions resist set-theoretic reduction.
4. Numbers have a clear criterion of identity (if two numbers have the same successors, they are the same number); the criterion of identity for propositions is contested (are "P" and "P and Q or not-Q" the same proposition?).
5. Numbers are the subject matter of mathematics; propositions are the subject matter of logic and semantics.
6. Propositions are about things (they have intentionality); numbers are not about anything.
7. Numbers feature in scientific explanations (mathematical physics); propositions' role in explanation is different.

**Normative differences:**
8. Denying numbers requires revising mathematics; denying propositions requires revising logic and semantics.

**Structural differences:**
9. Propositions are the contents of thought and language; numbers are not.
10. Numbers stand in mathematical relations (greater than, successor of); propositions stand in logical relations (entailment, consistency).

### Relevance Assessment

| Difference | Status | Principle |
|------------|--------|-----------|
| (1) Mathematical vs. semantic | Potentially relevant | If the case against numbers depends on a nominalist argument specific to mathematics (e.g., Field's fictionalism, which reconstructs mathematics without quantifying over numbers), then the objection does not generalize to propositions. The causal criterion may not be the whole story. |
| (6) Intentionality | Genuinely relevant | Propositions have a property -- intentionality, aboutness -- that numbers lack. If the causal criterion is motivated by a broader naturalistic worldview that makes an exception for intentional phenomena (on the grounds that intentionality is irreducible but indispensable), then the parity claim fails. The principle: "Intentional entities are exempt from the causal criterion because intentionality is not the kind of property that causal efficacy is supposed to track." |
| (9) Thought/language contents | Genuinely relevant | Propositions are so deeply embedded in our practices of thinking and speaking that rejecting them may be pragmatically impossible (a "Moorean fact"). This is not a metaphysical difference but a pragmatic one: the cost of rejecting propositions may be higher than the cost of rejecting numbers. |
| (4) Identity criteria | Potentially relevant | If the contested nature of propositional identity criteria makes propositions more ontologically suspect than numbers, this cuts against the parity claim (propositions are worse off, not better). |
| (7) Role in scientific explanation | Potentially relevant | If the indispensability argument supports the existence of mathematical objects (because they are indispensable to science), but propositions are not similarly indispensable, then the parity claim may actually cut the other way: numbers exist (contra the nominalist) but propositions do not. |

### Verdict

**Parity is trivial (with a formal shell).** The respect of comparison -- "lacks causal powers" -- is so thin that formal parity holds trivially: yes, numbers and propositions both lack causal powers. But this formal parity does no substantive work because the argument against numbers is not, or not only, that numbers lack causal powers. The broader nominalist case against abstract objects depends on further features -- the specific difficulties of mathematical epistemology, the success of nominalist reconstructions of mathematics, the parsimony considerations specific to mathematical ontology -- that do not all carry over to propositions. To make the parity argument non-trivial, the proponent must specify a thicker respect of comparison that connects causal inertness to the specific grounds of the ontological objection and show that those grounds apply symmetrically to both numbers and propositions. The analysis explains why the respect is too thin: it fails to connect to the actual argumentative motivations behind the causal criterion.

### Suppressed Disanalogies

1. **Independently plausible:** The causal criterion of existence is not a freestanding principle; it is motivated by a specific epistemological concern (how can we have knowledge of causally inert objects?). Propositions may be epistemically accessible in a way numbers are not -- we grasp propositions whenever we think or speak, whereas grasping numbers may require a special faculty of mathematical intuition. If the epistemological concern is what drives the causal criterion, and propositions are epistemically unproblematic, then parity fails.

### Dialectical Consequences

**Given that parity is trivial:** The parity argument does not advance the debate. The proponent of the parity argument needs to reconstruct the opponent's case against numbers in its strongest form -- not merely as "numbers lack causal powers, therefore they don't exist" but as the full argument, including the epistemological and parsimony considerations that do the real work. If those considerations do not apply to propositions, parity fails. If they do, parity is substantive and the analysis can proceed.

**For the proponent:** The argument can be repaired by articulating the full grounds of the nominalist objection and showing that they apply symmetrically. This requires engaging with specific nominalist arguments (Field, Azzouni, etc.) rather than relying on a sloganized version of the causal criterion.

**For the respondent:** The most efficient response is to point out the triviality of the respect of comparison: "Propositions differ from numbers in their intentionality and their role in thought and language, and these differences are relevant to the argument against numbers because [specific reason]. The causal criterion is not the whole story."
```

This example illustrates a crucial methodological point: the distinction between formal and substantive parity is not merely terminological. A parity argument that achieves formal parity (the two cases share the cited feature) without substantive parity (the cited feature is not what drives the differential treatment) fails to do any argumentative work, even though it is technically true. The analysis identifies this failure mode and explains what the proponent must do to repair it.
